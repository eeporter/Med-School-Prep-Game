# Anemia part 1: basics, small-cell and large-cell anemias, porphyrias and iron overload

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials for USMLE Step 1, Hematology & Oncology (anemia basics pp. 27-28, microcytic pp. 28-36, macrocytic pp. 37-42, anemia of chronic disease p. 54, porphyrias pp. 58-59, iron poisoning and left shift p. 60). Normal-size and hemolytic anemias are in guide 40. Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Sort every anemia by the **MCV (mean corpuscular volume)**: **under 80 fL = microcytic** (not enough hemoglobin: iron, heme or globin is short), **80-100 = normocytic**, **over 100 = macrocytic** (DNA cannot be made: B12 or folate). Then use **iron studies** for the small cells and **methylmalonic acid** for the big ones.

## Anemia basics (pp. 27-28)
- Anemia = **less red cell mass**. Hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct) and red cell (RBC) count stand in for it.
- **Symptoms come from low oxygen delivery:** weakness, tiredness, shortness of breath, pale conjunctiva and skin, headache, lightheadedness, and **angina** in people with coronary disease.
- Your notes define anemia as **Hb under 13.5 g/dL in men and under 12.5 g/dL in women** (p. 27).

**Other source (cutoffs):** MSD uses **Hb under 13.6 g/dL in men and under 12 g/dL in women** (Hct under 40% and 37%) ([MSD: Evaluation of Anemia, updated Sep 2024](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/approach-to-the-patient-with-anemia/evaluation-of-anemia)).

## Microcytic anemias: hemoglobin = heme (iron + protoporphyrin) + globin (p. 28)
**Why the cells are small:** red cell precursors keep dividing until they have enough hemoglobin. With too little hemoglobin they make an **extra division**, so the cells end up smaller (p. 28).

| Which part is missing | Disease |
| --- | --- |
| **Iron** | Iron deficiency; anemia of chronic disease (iron locked away; microcytic only late) |
| **Protoporphyrin** | Sideroblastic anemia, lead poisoning |
| **Globin** | Thalassemias |

### The iron studies table (pp. 28-32, 54)
Know what each test means first (pp. 28-29):
- **Serum iron:** iron in the blood.
- **TIBC (total iron-binding capacity):** how much **transferrin** (the iron carrier) is in the blood.
- **Saturation:** percent of transferrin carrying iron.
- **Ferritin:** the **storage** form, in macrophages and liver.

| | Ferritin (stores) | TIBC (transferrin) | Serum iron | Saturation | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Iron deficiency** | **Low** | **High** | Low | Low | High RDW, high FEP |
| **Anemia of chronic disease** | **High** | **Low** | Low | Low | Inflammation, high FEP |
| **Sideroblastic** | High | Low | **High** | **High** | **Ringed sideroblasts** (iron overload) |
| **Thalassemia** | Normal (standard teaching) | Normal | Normal | Normal | **High RBC count**, target cells |

RDW = red cell distribution width (how varied the cell sizes are). FEP = free erythrocyte protoporphyrin (protoporphyrin with no iron to bind).

Memory hook: **ferritin and TIBC move in opposite directions.** Low stores make the liver build more transferrin to catch any iron it can; high stores (or inflammation) turn transferrin down.

### Iron deficiency (pp. 28-30)
- **Most common anemia**; lack of iron is the world's most common nutritional deficiency, affecting about **a third** of people.
- **Absorbed in the duodenum** (as Fe2+; stomach acid keeps it in that form). **Ferroportin** moves iron out of gut cells into blood; **transferrin** carries it; **ferritin** stores it safely.
- **Causes by age:**

| Age | Usual cause |
| --- | --- |
| Infants | Breast milk is low in iron |
| Children | Poor diet |
| Adults 20-50 | **Peptic ulcer** (men); **heavy periods or pregnancy** (women) |
| Older adults | **Colon polyps or cancer** (Western world); **hookworm** (*Ancylostoma duodenale*, *Necator americanus*) in developing countries |
| Any age | Malnutrition, malabsorption, **gastrectomy** (less acid) |

- **Always look for blood loss, especially GI**, in an adult with iron deficiency (p. 29).
- **Order of changes:** (1) **ferritin falls, TIBC rises** (stores gone); (2) serum iron and saturation fall; (3) **normocytic** anemia (fewer cells, normal size); (4) **microcytic, hypochromic** anemia (p. 29).
- **Signs:** pallor, **pica** (eating ice, dirt and other non-foods), **spoon nails (koilonychia)**, glossitis, cheilosis. **Plummer-Vinson syndrome** = iron deficiency + **esophageal webs** + glossitis: trouble swallowing and a beefy-red tongue (p. 30).
- **Treatment:** oral iron (ferrous sulfate). A **reticulocyte** rise (young red cells with blue-staining leftover ribosomal RNA) is the first sign it is working (p. 30).

**Correction (rate of response):** p. 30 says Hb should rise about **2 g/dL per week** for the first three weeks. MSD gives a slower rise: reticulocytes go up after **about 1 week**, and Hb "begins to rise after 1 to 2 weeks with an ~ 1 g/dL increase at 14 days in responders"; the anemia "should be corrected within 2 months" ([MSD: Iron Deficiency Anemia, updated Apr 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-deficient-erythropoiesis/iron-deficiency-anemia)). A rise of about 2 g/dL **over** three weeks fits MSD; 2 g/dL **each** week does not.

**Other source (iron deficiency details):** MSD: a ferritin **under 30 ng/mL** is specific for iron deficiency (up to 100 can still fit when inflammation is present); iron is absorbed in the duodenum **and upper jejunum**; a typical dose is 65 mg elemental iron (325 mg ferrous sulfate) **every other day**, because larger or more frequent doses raise **hepcidin** and are mostly not absorbed; IV iron is for intolerance, failure, ongoing large losses or a need for fast repletion (same MSD page).

### Anemia of chronic disease (p. 54)
- Chronic inflammation (rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, endocarditis), cancer, chronic kidney disease. **Most common anemia in hospitalized patients.**
- **Mechanism:** the liver makes **hepcidin** (an acute-phase protein). Hepcidin **blocks ferroportin** in gut cells and macrophages, so less iron is absorbed and stored iron is trapped. The purpose is to **starve bacteria of iron**.
- **Labs:** **high ferritin, low TIBC**, low serum iron, low saturation, high FEP.
- **Treatment:** treat the cause; **erythropoietin (EPO)** helps some patients, especially those with cancer.

### Sideroblastic anemia (pp. 31-32)
**Heme synthesis in four steps (p. 31):**
1. **ALA synthase** turns succinyl-CoA (plus glycine, standard teaching) into **ALA (aminolevulinic acid)**; needs **vitamin B6**; this is the **rate-limiting** step.
2. **ALA dehydratase** turns ALA into porphobilinogen.
3. More steps make protoporphyrin.
4. **Ferrochelatase** puts iron into protoporphyrin to make heme, inside the **mitochondria**.

- Without protoporphyrin, iron **piles up in the mitochondria**, which sit in a ring around the nucleus: **ringed sideroblasts** (seen with Prussian blue stain in the marrow).
- **Congenital:** most often an **ALA synthase** defect.
- **Acquired:** **alcohol** (mitochondrial poison), **lead** (blocks ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase), **B6 deficiency**, most often from **isoniazid** for tuberculosis.
- **Labs:** iron overload: **high ferritin, low TIBC, high serum iron, high saturation**.
- **Treatment:** **pyridoxine (B6)**.

**Other source (sideroblastic anemia):** MSD says the inherited form is most often **X-linked** (*ALAS2* gene) and responds to pyridoxine "usually incompletely"; the most common acquired cause is **myelodysplastic syndrome**; copper deficiency and zinc are other causes; the smear shows a **dimorphic** (two-population) picture ([MSD: Sideroblastic Anemias, updated Mar 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-deficient-erythropoiesis/sideroblastic-anemias)).

### Lead poisoning (p. 33)
- Lead blocks **ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase** → less heme.
- **Who:** young children in old housing built **before 1978** (paint dust and chips; hand-to-mouth behavior; the child's brain is more exposed), and adults in **battery manufacturing**.
- **"LEAD" mnemonic (your notes):**
  - **L**ead **L**ines: on the gums (Burton lines) and on the metaphyses of long bones on x-ray.
  - **E**ncephalopathy and **E**rythrocyte **basophilic stippling**.
  - **A**bdominal colic, constipation and sideroblastic **A**nemia.
  - **D**rops: **wrist drop and foot drop**.
- **Basophilic stippling:** clumped ribosomes, because lead blocks RNA breakdown. Diagnosis is the **blood lead level**.
- **Your notes' treatment:** dimercaprol and EDTA first line; **succimer** for children ("it 'sucks' to be a kid who eats lead").

**Other source (lead chelation):** MSD chelates children with **encephalopathy or a blood lead over 45 mcg/dL**, and adults with symptoms plus a level over 70. **Succimer** is first line for "asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients with mildly elevated lead level"; **dimercaprol plus calcium disodium EDTA** is for **encephalopathy**. CDC's action level for children is **over 3.5 mcg/dL**. Encephalopathy is likely above 100 mcg/dL ([MSD: Lead Poisoning, updated Apr 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/lead-poisoning)).

### Thalassemias (pp. 34-36)
Normal hemoglobins: **HbA (α2β2)**, **HbA2 (α2δ2)**, **HbF (α2γ2)** (p. 34).

**Alpha thalassemia** (4 alpha genes on **chromosome 16**; usually **deletions**):

| Genes lost | Result |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | No symptoms (silent carrier) |
| 2 | Mild anemia with a **high RBC count**. **Cis** (both on one chromosome; Asian ancestry) raises the risk of severe disease in children; **trans** (one on each; African ancestry) does not |
| 3 | **HbH disease**: extra beta chains form **β4 tetramers (HbH)** that damage cells; severe anemia |
| 4 | **Hydrops fetalis**, death in the womb; gamma chains form **γ4 (Hb Barts)** |

**Beta thalassemia** (2 beta genes on **chromosome 11**; usually **point mutations** in promoter or splice sites; African and Mediterranean ancestry). **β0** = no beta chain made; **β+** = some made.
- **Minor (β/β+):** usually no symptoms; **high RBC count**, microcytic cells, **target cells**; electrophoresis shows slightly less HbA, **more HbA2** (your notes: about 5% vs normal 2.5%) and slightly more HbF.
- **Major (β0/β0):** severe anemia **after about 6 months**, once protective **HbF** fades. Extra **alpha chains clump** and kill precursors (ineffective erythropoiesis) and cells are removed by the spleen. The marrow expands: **"crew-cut" skull x-ray**, **"chipmunk" face**, liver and spleen enlarge (**extramedullary hematopoiesis**). Risk of **aplastic crisis from parvovirus B19**. Needs **chronic transfusions**, which cause **iron overload (secondary hemochromatosis)**. Smear: microcytes, target cells, **nucleated red cells**. Electrophoresis: little or **no HbA**, more HbA2 and HbF.
- **HbS/β-thalassemia:** mild to moderate sickle cell disease, depending on how much beta chain is made.
- **Target cells** appear when cells have **too little volume** (thalassemia, iron deficiency) or **too much membrane** (liver disease, **after splenectomy**, since the spleen normally trims extra membrane) (p. 36).

**Other source (thalassemia):** MSD gives the beta-thalassemia minor cutoff as **HbA2 above 3.5-4%**. For treatment it lists transfusion with **iron chelation**, **luspatercept** for transfusion-dependent beta thalassemia, and says **allogeneic stem cell transplantation and gene therapy** "are potentially curative options" ([MSD: Thalassemias, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/thalassemias)).

**Iron deficiency or thalassemia trait?** Both are microcytic. Thalassemia trait has a **high or normal RBC count** with normal iron studies; iron deficiency has a **low RBC count**, low ferritin and high RDW (pp. 30, 34-35; standard teaching for the comparison).

## Macrocytic anemias (MCV over 100) (pp. 37-42)
**Why B12 and folate matter (p. 37):**
1. Folate circulates as **methyl-THF (methyltetrahydrofolate)**.
2. It hands its **methyl group to B12**.
3. B12 hands it to **homocysteine**, making **methionine**, and the freed folate can now help make DNA.
4. No B12 or no folate → DNA cannot be made → nuclei mature late while the cytoplasm keeps growing → **big cells (megaloblasts)**, **hypersegmented neutrophils (more than 5 lobes)**, and changes in fast-dividing gut cells (**glossitis**).

| | **Folate deficiency** | **B12 deficiency** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stores last | **Months** (small stores) | **Years** (big liver stores) |
| Absorbed | Jejunum | **Terminal ileum** (with intrinsic factor) |
| Homocysteine | **High** | **High** |
| **Methylmalonic acid** | **Normal** | **High** |
| Nerve damage | **None** | **Subacute combined degeneration** |
| Pages | 37-38 | 38-40 |

### Folate deficiency (pp. 37-38)
- Comes from green vegetables and some fruits.
- **Causes:** poor diet (**alcohol use**, elderly), **more demand** (pregnancy, cancer, **hemolytic anemia** such as sickle cell), and **drugs: methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin**.
- High homocysteine raises the risk of **blood clots**.

### B12 (cobalamin) deficiency (pp. 38-40)
**Absorption, step by step:**
1. B12 in food is bound to animal proteins and must be released.
2. **R-binder** from saliva carries it through the stomach.
3. **Pancreatic proteases** in the duodenum cut it free of R-binder.
4. It binds **intrinsic factor** (from gastric **parietal cells**) and is absorbed in the **terminal ileum**.

**Correction (B12 release from food):** p. 38 says salivary enzymes (amylase) free B12 from food proteins. MSD says food B12 is released in the **stomach's acid environment**, where it then binds R protein (haptocorrin) ([MSD: Vitamin B12 Deficiency, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nutritional-disorders/vitamin-deficiency-dependency-and-toxicity/vitamin-b12-deficiency)). Amylase digests starch, not protein.

- **Causes (your notes):** **pernicious anemia** (autoimmune attack on **parietal cells** in the stomach body → no intrinsic factor; the most common cause), **gastrectomy**, **pancreatic insufficiency**, **terminal ileum disease** (**Crohn disease**, the fish tapeworm ***Diphyllobothrium latum***), and diet only in **vegans**.
- **Subacute combined degeneration** (methylmalonic acid builds up and damages myelin):
  - **Dorsal columns** → lost vibration and position sense.
  - **Corticospinal tracts** → upper motor neuron signs.
  - **Spinocerebellar tracts** → ataxic gait.
- **Never treat with folate alone:** it fixes the anemia but the **nerve damage gets worse** (p. 40).
- Reticulocytes rise sharply once B12 is given (p. 40).

**Correction (enzyme step):** p. 40 says B12 is the cofactor for converting "methylmalonic acid to succinyl CoA". Your Biochemistry notes give the step correctly: "Isomerization of methylmalonyl CoA forms succinyl CoA" (El-Husseiny Biochemistry, p. 223). The enzyme is **methylmalonyl-CoA mutase** (standard teaching); methylmalonic acid is what builds up when the step fails.

**Other source (B12 details):** MSD says the most common cause overall is **poor absorption from low stomach acid in older adults**; it also lists gastric bypass, **metformin** and repeated **nitrous oxide** exposure. Nerve damage **can occur without anemia or big cells**. Treatment can be **high-dose oral B12** (1000-2000 mcg daily, absorbed "by mass action, even when intrinsic factor is absent") or IM injections for severe deficiency or nerve symptoms (same MSD page). MSD's folate page: liver stores last "a several-month supply"; absorption is in the duodenum and upper jejunum; "vitamin B12 deficiency must be excluded before treating with folate" ([MSD: Folate Deficiency, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nutritional-disorders/vitamin-deficiency-dependency-and-toxicity/folate-deficiency)).

### Orotic aciduria (p. 41)
- **UMP synthase** defect (pyrimidine synthesis); autosomal recessive.
- Child with failure to thrive, developmental delay and **megaloblastic anemia that does not respond to B12 or folate**; orotic acid in urine; **normal ammonia** (unlike OTC deficiency).
- **Treatment:** uridine monophosphate (your notes). See guide 35 for the approved drug, **uridine triacetate**.

### Macrocytosis without megaloblasts (p. 42)
- DNA synthesis is normal and there are **no hypersegmented neutrophils**: **alcohol use, liver disease**.
- **Diamond-Blackfan anemia:** red cell precursors cannot make ribosomes properly → anemia in the **first year**; **high % HbF**; up to half have short stature, facial changes and **triphalangeal thumbs**.

## Porphyrias (pp. 58-59)
**Rule:** early-step enzyme defects cause **nerve and gut attacks** (acute porphyrias); later-step defects cause **skin sensitivity to light** (cutaneous porphyrias) (p. 59).

| | **Acute intermittent porphyria (AIP)** | **Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enzyme | **Porphobilinogen deaminase** (old name uroporphyrinogen I synthase); autosomal dominant | **Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase** |
| Builds up | **Porphobilinogen and ALA** | **Uroporphyrin** (tea-colored urine) |
| Picture | **5 P's:** **P**ainful abdomen, **P**ort-wine **P**ee, **P**olyneuropathy, **P**sychological changes, **P**recipitated by drugs | **Blisters** on sun-exposed skin, darker skin; worse with **alcohol**; linked to **hepatitis C** |
| Triggers | **CYP450 inducers** (most antiseizure drugs, griseofulvin, rifampin), alcohol, smoking, **starvation** | Alcohol |
| Treatment | **Hemin and glucose**: both turn **ALA synthase** down | **Phlebotomy**, sun avoidance, **hydroxychloroquine** |

- Your notes call PCT the **most common porphyria** (p. 59).

**Recent change (AIP prevention):** MSD now lists **givosiran**, "a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that selectively targets and markedly down-regulates hepatic ALA synthase-1", given monthly under the skin to prevent repeated attacks. MSD lists AIP as the most common **acute** porphyria and screens for attacks with **urine porphobilinogen** ([MSD: Acute Porphyrias, updated Mar 2026](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/the-porphyrias/acute-porphyrias)).

**Other source (PCT):** MSD adds smoking, estrogens, **HIV** and liver **iron** (test for *HFE* hemochromatosis mutations) as risk factors, uses **low-dose** chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine, and says to cure hepatitis C with direct-acting antivirals ([MSD: Porphyria Cutanea Tarda, updated Apr 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/the-porphyrias/porphyria-cutanea-tarda)).

## Iron poisoning and iron overload (p. 60)
| | **Acute** | **Chronic** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | Children who swallow adult iron tablets (look like candy); high death rate | Hereditary (primary) or transfusion-related (secondary: thalassemia, sickle cell) **hemochromatosis** |
| How it harms | Cell death from **lipid peroxidation** of membranes | Iron deposits in organs |
| Signs | Vomiting, GI bleeding, lethargy; later **scarring and bowel obstruction** | **Arthropathy, cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, hypogonadism** |
| Your notes' treatment | Chelation (IV **deferoxamine**, oral deferasirox), gastric lavage | **Phlebotomy** (if not anemic) or chelation |

**Other source (acute iron poisoning):** MSD gives IV **deferoxamine** for severe poisoning (for example serum iron over 500 mcg/dL, acidosis or shock) and **whole-bowel irrigation** when tablets show on x-ray. It says "Gastric lavage is usually not helpful" and "Activated charcoal does not adsorb iron"; it does not list deferasirox for acute poisoning. Over **60 mg/kg** of elemental iron can be severe; a quiet **latent period** can follow the first GI phase before shock and liver failure ([MSD: Iron Poisoning, updated Apr 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/iron-poisoning)).

**Left shift (p. 60):** more immature neutrophils (bands, metamyelocytes) in blood, usually with infection or inflammation. With immature red cells too it is a **leukoerythroblastic reaction**: severe anemia or a marrow filled by fibrosis or tumor.

## Clinical vignette
A 68-year-old man has fatigue and shortness of breath on stairs. Hb 9.2 g/dL, MCV 72 fL, high RDW, ferritin low, TIBC high. He takes no aspirin and has no obvious bleeding.
- **Diagnosis:** iron deficiency anemia.
- **Why:** microcytic anemia with low ferritin and high TIBC = empty iron stores (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, pp. 29-30).
- **Next step:** look for **GI blood loss**, especially **colon cancer**, with endoscopy and colonoscopy; your notes name colon polyps or cancer as the usual cause at this age (p. 29), and MSD says anemia may be the only sign of hidden GI cancer (MSD Iron Deficiency Anemia).

## Common traps
- **Ferritin low = iron deficiency; ferritin high with low iron = chronic disease; iron high and saturation high = sideroblastic.**
- **Microcytic with a high RBC count and normal iron = thalassemia trait.** Do not give iron.
- **HbA2 up = beta-thalassemia minor; HbH = 3 alpha genes gone; Hb Barts = 4 gone.**
- **Methylmalonic acid separates B12 (high) from folate (normal);** homocysteine is high in both.
- **Folate alone for B12 deficiency makes the nerves worse.**
- **Megaloblastic anemia that ignores B12 and folate = orotic aciduria.**
- **Basophilic stippling + abdominal pain + wrist drop = lead.**
- **Abdominal pain + neuropathy + dark urine after a new antiseizure drug or fasting = AIP: give hemin and glucose.**
- **Blisters on the backs of the hands in a drinker with hepatitis C = PCT.**

## Practice questions
1. A 30-year-old woman with heavy periods has Hb 9.5 g/dL, MCV 74 fL, ferritin 6 ng/mL and high TIBC. Which laboratory change is expected first after she starts oral iron?
   - **Answer:** a rise in the reticulocyte count.
   - **Why:** the marrow responds within about a week by releasing young red cells; Hb rises more slowly (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, p. 30; MSD: reticulocytosis "usually occurs after 1 week").
   - **Trap:** expecting the Hb to rise first; MSD expects only about a 1 g/dL rise at 14 days.
2. A 24-year-old man of Mediterranean descent has Hb 12.1 g/dL, MCV 64 fL, RBC count 6.1 million/mcL and normal ferritin. What will electrophoresis most likely show?
   - **Answer:** increased HbA2 (beta-thalassemia minor).
   - **Why:** very small cells with a high RBC count and normal iron stores; beta-thal minor raises HbA2 and slightly raises HbF (p. 35).
   - **Trap:** iron deficiency, which has a low ferritin and a low RBC count.
3. A man on treatment for tuberculosis develops microcytic anemia with high serum iron and ferritin. A marrow stain shows iron-filled rings around nuclei. What should be given?
   - **Answer:** pyridoxine (vitamin B6).
   - **Why:** isoniazid causes B6 deficiency; B6 is the cofactor of ALA synthase, so protoporphyrin is short and iron is trapped in mitochondria (ringed sideroblasts) (pp. 31-32).
   - **Trap:** iron supplements; iron is already overloaded.
4. A 58-year-old woman has numb feet, loss of vibration sense, a positive Romberg sign and an MCV of 112 fL. Methylmalonic acid is high. Antibodies to parietal cells are present. Treatment?
   - **Answer:** vitamin B12 (cobalamin).
   - **Why:** pernicious anemia destroys parietal cells, so no intrinsic factor; high methylmalonic acid confirms B12 rather than folate, and dorsal column damage is subacute combined degeneration (pp. 39-40).
   - **Trap:** folic acid, which can correct the anemia while the nerve damage worsens.
5. A 25-year-old woman starts phenobarbital and two days later has severe abdominal pain, weakness in her arms and reddish-brown urine. Her abdomen is soft. What is the best treatment for this acute attack?
   - **Answer:** IV hemin (with glucose).
   - **Why:** acute intermittent porphyria; CYP450 inducers turn ALA synthase up, and hemin and glucose turn it down (pp. 58-59).
   - **Trap:** givosiran, which also lowers ALA synthase-1 but is given monthly to prevent repeated attacks (MSD Acute Porphyrias); or treating as a surgical abdomen.
6. A 3-year-old in a house built in 1955 has belly pain, constipation and irritability. The smear shows microcytic cells with coarse blue dots. Blood lead is 52 mcg/dL; no encephalopathy. Which chelator?
   - **Answer:** oral succimer.
   - **Why:** lead poisoning with basophilic stippling (p. 33); MSD chelates children above 45 mcg/dL and uses succimer first when there is no encephalopathy.
   - **Trap:** dimercaprol plus EDTA, which MSD saves for encephalopathy.
