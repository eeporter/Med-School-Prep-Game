# ACE inhibitors and renal artery stenosis

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
From Elissa's question log: "How do ACE inhibitors interact with unilateral vs. bilateral renal artery stenosis, and what is the effect on renal hemodynamics?"
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Cardiovascular (renal artery stenosis pp. 119-121) and Essentials of Nephrology (ACE inhibitors, ARBs and aliskiren pp. 150-151). Each fact is also checked against StatPearls and the MSD Manual; where a different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
A stenotic kidney keeps its GFR up by squeezing the efferent arteriole with angiotensin II; an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor takes that squeeze away, so GFR falls. That is dangerous when both kidneys (or a solitary kidney) depend on it.

## Core mechanism
1. Renal artery stenosis (RAS) lowers pressure reaching the glomerulus. The kidney senses low perfusion and releases renin, activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). [StatPearls: Renovascular Hypertension](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/)
2. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the **efferent** arteriole. Squeezing the outflow raises glomerular pressure, so GFR is preserved even though renal plasma flow (RPF) is low. [StatPearls: Renal Blood Flow and Filtration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482248/)
3. ACE inhibitors block formation of angiotensin II and dilate the efferent arteriole, lowering glomerular capillary pressure. [StatPearls: ACE Inhibitors](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK431051/)
4. **Unilateral** RAS: the stenotic kidney's GFR drops, but the healthy kidney carries total GFR, so serum creatinine changes little. MSD notes that ACE inhibitors and ARBs "can be used in unilateral and, if glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is monitored closely, in bilateral renal artery stenosis". [MSD Manual: Renal Artery Stenosis and Occlusion](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/renovascular-disorders/renal-artery-stenosis-and-occlusion) (That the healthy kidney is what keeps total GFR up is reasoning from the physiology in items 2-3, standard teaching.)
5. **Bilateral** RAS or a **solitary** kidney: there is no backup kidney, so total GFR falls and creatinine rises. For Step 1, ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are treated as contraindicated here (MSD notes they can be used cautiously with close GFR monitoring). [StatPearls: Renovascular Hypertension](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/), [StatPearls: ARBs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537027/)
6. **Your notes on the stenotic vs the healthy kidney:** in unilateral RAS the stenotic kidney's renin still raises angiotensin II and aldosterone for the whole body, so **both** kidneys retain salt and water and blood pressure rises. Bilateral RAS can lead to chronic kidney disease; unilateral RAS does not, because the normal kidney still clears creatinine and urea (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 120). Long-standing RAS makes the juxtaglomerular (JG) cells of the affected kidney grow (hypertrophy and hyperplasia) (p. 120).
7. **What each kidney looks like (your notes, p. 121):** the narrowed artery shields the stenotic kidney from high pressure, so it shows **atrophy and cortical thinning** from poor blood supply. The other kidney takes the full high pressure and shows **hypertensive nephrosclerosis**: hyaline arteriolosclerosis, and in severe hypertension hyperplastic "onion-skin" arteriolosclerosis.
- **Other source (how strict to be):** your nephrology notes say ACE inhibitors are "used with caution" in bilateral RAS (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 151); your cardiovascular notes say they are "not given" in bilateral disease (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 121). StatPearls treats it as a contraindication and MSD allows careful use with close GFR monitoring. For an exam question, the answer is still: avoid in bilateral RAS or a solitary kidney.

## Hemodynamics at a glance
| Situation | Efferent tone | Glomerular pressure | GFR | RPF | Filtration fraction (GFR/RPF) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Angiotensin II acting (e.g. RAS, volume depletion) | Constricted | Up | Up / preserved | Down | Up |
| ACE inhibitor or ARB given | Dilated | Down | Down | Up | Down |
| Loss of afferent dilation (NSAIDs block prostaglandins) | — | Down | Down | Down | Unchanged |

## High-yield facts
- Atherosclerosis is the most common cause of RAS, usually in older adults (MSD: more common in men over 50) with vascular risk factors; fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) usually affects young women and the distal two-thirds of the renal artery. [StatPearls: Renovascular Hypertension](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/), [MSD Manual: Renovascular Hypertension](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/hypertension/renovascular-hypertension) (atherosclerosis in the "proximal one-third")
- Your notes describe atherosclerotic RAS in the **proximal third** of the artery, usually in older male smokers, and fibromuscular dysplasia in the **distal two-thirds** or segmental branches with a **"string of beads"** look, usually in young or middle-aged women (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 120). Clinical clues in your notes: hypertension that resists treatment with no family history, **kidneys of unequal size**, and an epigastric or flank bruit (p. 121).
- Clues to RAS: abdominal bruit, resistant hypertension, recurrent flash pulmonary edema, unexplained hypokalemia with metabolic alkalosis (from high aldosterone), a small kidney on one side, and creatinine rising more than 30% after starting an ACE inhibitor. [StatPearls: Renovascular Hypertension](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/)
- **Your notes' ACE inhibitor summary (El-Husseiny Nephrology, pp. 150-151):** captopril, enalapril, lisinopril, ramipril ("-pril") block angiotensin II formation, so aldosterone falls, **renin rises** (lost negative feedback) and bradykinin is not broken down. Uses: hypertension, heart failure (lower mortality), proteinuria and diabetic nephropathy, and preventing harmful heart remodeling. Adverse effects by the mnemonic **CATCHH**: **C**ough, **A**ngioedema, **T**eratogen (fetal kidney malformations), raised **C**reatinine (lower GFR), **H**yperkalemia, **H**ypotension.
- Angioedema from ACE inhibitors is driven by bradykinin, so they are **contraindicated in C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency** (hereditary angioedema) (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 150).
- **Aliskiren** blocks renin directly (angiotensinogen is not converted to angiotensin I). It does not affect bradykinin, shares the hyperkalemia, low GFR and hypotension risks, and is relatively contraindicated with an ACE inhibitor or ARB (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 151).
- ACE inhibitor adverse effects: dry cough, the most frequent side effect (up to 20% in North American and European populations, up to 40% in Asian populations, per MSD; bradykinin is not broken down), angioedema (the most serious), hyperkalemia, and fetal toxicity, so they are avoided in pregnancy. [StatPearls: ACE Inhibitors](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK431051/), [MSD Manual: Medications for Hypertension](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/hypertension/medications-for-hypertension)
- ARBs (losartan, candesartan, valsartan, "-sartan"; El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 151) block the angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor, do not raise bradykinin, so cough is less common; they share the pregnancy and hyperkalemia warnings and the bilateral RAS caution; angioedema is much less frequent than with ACE inhibitors. [StatPearls: ARBs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537027/), [MSD Manual: Medications for Hypertension](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/hypertension/medications-for-hypertension)
- In chronic kidney disease, the same efferent dilation is protective: lower glomerular pressure slows GFR decline and proteinuria. [StatPearls: ACE Inhibitors](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK431051/)

## Compare and contrast: the two causes of RAS
| Feature | Atherosclerotic RAS | Fibromuscular dysplasia |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Part of artery | Proximal one-third (near the aorta) | Distal two-thirds and branches |
| Mechanism | Plaque narrows lumen | Abnormal growth of the arterial wall |
| Angiogram look | Narrowing near the origin | "String of beads" (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 120) |

## Clinical vignette
A 72-year-old smoker with peripheral artery disease starts lisinopril for hypertension. Two weeks later his serum creatinine has risen from 1.0 to 1.8 mg/dL. An abdominal bruit is heard. What explains the creatinine rise?

**Answer:** bilateral renal artery stenosis; the ACE inhibitor removed angiotensin II-driven efferent constriction that was keeping GFR up in both kidneys.
**Why:** a rise of 80% (well over 30%) after an ACE inhibitor, an older patient with atherosclerosis, and a bruit all point to RAS. With both kidneys affected, there is no healthy kidney to hold total GFR. [StatPearls: Renovascular Hypertension](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/)
**Trap:** thinking the drug is directly nephrotoxic. It is a hemodynamic GFR drop (efferent dilation), not tubular injury.

## Common traps
- Angiotensin II constricts the **efferent**, not the afferent, arteriole. (Your nephrology notes' ACE mnemonic on p. 30 reads "Angiotensin II inhibitors Constrict Efferent"; that is a slip, see the Correction in [02-renal-formulas.md](02-renal-formulas.md).)
- In unilateral RAS the **stenotic** kidney shrinks (atrophy) and the **other** kidney shows hypertensive arteriolar damage; students often swap them (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 121).
- Renin goes **up** on an ACE inhibitor, not down (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 150).
- Unilateral RAS does not make ACE inhibitors absolutely contraindicated; the problem is bilateral disease or a solitary kidney.
- Cough is from bradykinin (ACE inhibitors), so switching to an ARB fixes it; angioedema can still occur with ARBs, though rarely.
- NSAIDs block the prostaglandins that keep the afferent arteriole dilated, lowering GFR. [StatPearls: NSAIDs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547742/) An ACE inhibitor plus an NSAID cuts GFR from both ends: less inflow and less outflow resistance (standard teaching).

## Practice questions
1. Angiotensin II is infused into a healthy volunteer. What happens to GFR, RPF and filtration fraction?
   - **Answer:** GFR up (or preserved), RPF down, filtration fraction up.
   - **Why:** efferent constriction raises glomerular pressure while reducing flow through the kidney. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482248/)
   - **Trap:** applying the afferent-constriction pattern (GFR and RPF both down, FF unchanged).
2. A 28-year-old woman has severe hypertension and a bruit at the right costovertebral angle. What is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** fibromuscular dysplasia of the renal artery.
   - **Why:** FMD is the classic cause of renovascular hypertension in young women. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/)
   - **Trap:** choosing atherosclerosis, which is the most common cause overall but typical in older adults.
3. A patient with only one kidney (the other was donated) has renal artery stenosis in the remaining kidney. Why should an ACE inhibitor be avoided?
   - **Answer:** the patient's whole GFR depends on angiotensin II constriction of that kidney's efferent arterioles.
   - **Why:** a solitary kidney behaves like bilateral RAS; blocking angiotensin II drops total GFR. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551587/)
   - **Trap:** thinking only "bilateral" is the rule; a solitary stenotic kidney counts too.
4. A patient on enalapril develops a persistent dry cough. Which drug change keeps RAAS blockade but removes the cause of the cough?
   - **Answer:** switch to an ARB such as losartan.
   - **Why:** ARBs block the AT1 receptor without stopping bradykinin breakdown. [StatPearls: ARBs](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537027/)
   - **Trap:** an ARB is still contraindicated in pregnancy and bilateral RAS.
