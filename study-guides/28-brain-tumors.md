# Brain tumors in adults and children

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (neurocutaneous syndromes pp. 180-182, adult tumors pp. 183-186, childhood tumors pp. 187-189). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual and pathology sources; where a newer or different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Sort by **age and place**: adult tumors sit mostly in the **cerebral hemispheres** (glioblastoma, metastases) or on the coverings and nerves (meningioma, schwannoma); childhood tumors sit mostly in the **cerebellum and back of the skull** (pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, ependymoma) or above the sella (craniopharyngioma). Then match each tumor to its **one histology picture**: pseudopalisading necrosis (glioblastoma), whorls and psammoma bodies (meningioma), Rosenthal fibers (pilocytic), fried eggs (oligodendroglioma), perivascular pseudorosettes (ependymoma), motor-oil cysts with wet keratin (craniopharyngioma).

## Core mechanism
1. **Most brain tumors are metastases.** "Brain metastases are about 10 times more common than primary tumors", and "over 80% of brain metastases are multiple". Sources include adenocarcinoma "of the lung, breast, colon, thyroid, or kidneys" and malignant melanoma. [MSD Manual: Overview of CNS Tumors](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/central-nervous-system-tumors/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors) Arithmetic: 10 metastases for every 1 primary means 10 / (10 + 1) = 10/11, about 91%, of brain tumors are metastases. That they tend to sit at the gray-white junction is standard teaching. **Other source:** your notes say about **half** of brain and spinal cord tumors are metastases, and that glioblastoma and meningioma are the most frequent primary tumors (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 183).
2. **Symptoms come from pressure and place.** "Headache is the most common symptom"; it "may be most intense when patients awake from deep nonrapid eye movement (non-REM) sleep", and with very high pressure it "may be accompanied by vomiting". "Papilledema develops in approximately 25 to 35% of patients with a brain tumor". [MSD Manual: Overview of CNS Tumors](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/central-nervous-system-tumors/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors) Your notes: tumors above the tentorium cause **seizures**, weakness and sensory change; posterior fossa tumors cause **ataxia** and dysmetria; any large tumor raises pressure (early-morning headache and vomiting, papilledema) (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 183). In children, "increased intracranial pressure resulting from obstructive hydrocephalus is the cause of the most common manifestations". [MSD Manual: CNS Tumors in Children](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/pediatric-cancers/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors-in-children)
3. **Adults vs children.** MSD lists the common childhood tumors as "cerebellar astrocytomas, juvenile pilocytic astrocytomas, and medulloblastomas" and ependymomas, and the common adult tumors as meningiomas, schwannomas and "gliomas of the cerebral hemispheres, particularly glioblastoma". [MSD Manual: Overview of CNS Tumors](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/central-nervous-system-tumors/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors) Your notes: in children most primary brain tumors are **infratentorial**, with **craniopharyngioma** the important exception; CNS tumors are the most common solid tumors of childhood and the second most common childhood cancer after leukemia; the most common benign one is **pilocytic astrocytoma** and the most common malignant one is **medulloblastoma** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 187). CNS tumors are "the most common solid cancer in children < 15 years old". [MSD Manual: CNS Tumors in Children](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/pediatric-cancers/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors-in-children)

## Adult tumors

| Tumor | Who and where | Picture to recognize | Key fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype** (your notes: glioblastoma multiforme, grade IV astrocytoma) | Middle-aged or older adults; cerebral hemisphere white matter | Ring-enhancing mass; can cross the corpus callosum as a "butterfly glioma". Histology: **pseudopalisading necrosis** with microvascular proliferation; **GFAP** positive | Most common and most lethal primary malignancy in adults, median survival about 1 year; often **EGFR** overexpression; spreads along white matter tracts; temozolomide is given with radiation (El-Husseiny p. 183) |
| **Meningioma** | "Median age of 67"; the only brain tumor more common in women; anywhere there is dura, most often the **convexities and parasagittal** region (your notes call it the 2nd most common primary adult CNS tumor, p. 184) | Extra-axial mass with a "dural tail". Histology: meningothelial whorls, psammoma bodies | From arachnoid cap cells; most are benign, CNS WHO grade 1; often progesterone receptor positive, and can grow in pregnancy |
| **Hemangioblastoma** | Cerebellum most often | Thin-walled capillaries packed together with little tissue between | From blood vessels; with retinal angiomas think **von Hippel-Lindau**; can make **erythropoietin**, causing polycythemia (El-Husseiny p. 184) |
| **Vestibular schwannoma** (acoustic neuroma) | Adults; internal auditory canal into the cerebellopontine angle | Mass at the cerebellopontine angle on gadolinium MRI | One-sided sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and vertigo; **bilateral** = neurofibromatosis type 2. Your notes: schwannomas can grow on any cranial nerve **except II** (no Schwann cells), and are **S-100** positive, like melanoma, because both come from neural crest (pp. 184-185) |
| **Oligodendroglioma** | Adults; frontal lobe white matter most often; often presents with **seizures** | Calcifications can be seen. Histology: "fried egg" cells, "chicken wire" capillaries | IDH mutation plus 1p/19q codeletion; slow growing, long survival, but tends to recur after surgery (p. 185) |
| **Pituitary adenoma** (WHO now also calls it pituitary neuroendocrine tumor, PitNET) | Adults; sella | Bitemporal hemianopia from pressure on the middle of the chiasm; your notes call it the most common cause of bitemporal hemianopia (p. 185) | Prolactin is the most common hormone made; prolactinomas are treated with dopamine agonists |
| **Primary CNS lymphoma** | HIV with a very low CD4 count; your notes: the most frequent CNS tumor in immunosuppressed patients (p. 186) | Ring-enhancing lesions | B-cell tumor; in HIV "frequently associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)"; high grade, poor prognosis |

- **Vestibular schwannoma:** [MSD Manual: Vestibular Schwannoma](https://msdmanuals.com/en-in/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/vestibular-schwannoma).
- **Oligodendroglioma:** [PathologyOutlines: Oligodendroglioma, IDH mutant and 1p/19q codeleted](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumoroligodendrogliomaidhmutant.html).
- **Pituitary adenoma:** [MSD Manual: Pituitary Tumors](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/pituitary-tumors).
- **Primary CNS lymphoma:** [MSD Manual: Cancers Common Among Patients With HIV](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/human-immunodeficiency-virus-hiv-infection/cancers-common-among-patients-with-human-immunodeficiency-virus-hiv-infection).

- **Recent change (names and grades):** your notes use "glioblastoma multiforme (grade IV astrocytoma)" and grade astrocytomas I to IV by atypia, mitoses, necrosis and vascular proliferation (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 183). The WHO 2021 classification writes grades in **Arabic numerals** ("Arabic numerals are employed (rather than Roman numerals)"), calls the tumor **glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4**, and lists "Pituitary adenoma/PitNET" (pituitary neuroendocrine tumor). [Louis DN et al. Neuro-Oncology 2021;23(8):1231-1251](https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214) Details below.
- **Glioblastoma is IDH-wildtype by definition (WHO 2021 classification, called CNS5).** CNS5 has only three adult-type diffuse gliomas: "Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant"; "Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant, and 1p/19q-codeleted"; and "Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype". IDH-mutant astrocytomas are graded 2, 3 or 4, and grades are now written in Arabic numerals. [Louis DN et al. The 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System: a summary. Neuro-Oncology 2021;23(8):1231-1251, doi:10.1093/neuonc/noab106](https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214) "IDH-mutant astrocytomas are no longer referred to as glioblastomas"; an IDH-wildtype diffuse astrocytic tumor with a TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or gain of chromosome 7 with loss of chromosome 10 is called glioblastoma. [Smith HL, Wadhwani N, Horbinski C. Major features of the 2021 WHO classification of CNS tumors. Neurotherapeutics 2022;19:1691-1704](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s13311-022-01249-0)
  - So the old "secondary glioblastoma" (IDH-mutant, grown from a lower-grade tumor) is now **astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4**, and the terms "primary" and "secondary" glioblastoma are no longer used (standard teaching). MSD Gliomas still uses the older wording ("primary glioblastomas"), so expect older question banks to use it too. IDH-mutant tumors "tend to have a better prognosis" than IDH-wildtype ones. [MSD Manual: Gliomas](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/gliomas)
  - Necrosis or microvascular proliferation in an IDH-wildtype diffuse astrocytic tumor also makes it glioblastoma (standard teaching; WebPath shows the histology). Glioblastoma is GFAP positive, an astrocyte marker (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 183).
- **Meningioma symptoms** depend on location: convexity tumors can cause "focal seizures" and cognitive deficits. [MSD Manual: Meningiomas](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/meningiomas) "Rarely, CNS radiation therapy causes gliomas, meningiomas, or peripheral nerve sheath tumors years after therapy." [MSD Manual: Overview of CNS Tumors](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/central-nervous-system-tumors/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors)
- **Vestibular schwannoma:** "Schwann cell–derived tumor of the eighth cranial nerve" that "almost always" arises from the vestibular division; early "unilateral tinnitus, dizziness, and dysequilibrium", later facial "numbness or weakness"; about 8% of intracranial tumors. "Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are a common feature of neurofibromatosis type 2." [MSD Manual: Vestibular Schwannoma](https://msdmanuals.com/en-in/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/vestibular-schwannoma) Histology: "the more cellular 'Antoni A' pattern ... with palisading nuclei surrounding pink areas (Verocay bodies)" and the "'Antoni B' pattern with a looser stroma, fewer cells, and myxoid change". [WebPath, University of Utah: schwannoma](https://webpath.med.utah.edu/CNSHTML/CNS187.html) That schwannomas are S-100 positive is standard teaching.
- **Pituitary adenoma:** excess growth hormone gives gigantism before puberty or acromegaly after; excess ACTH gives Cushing syndrome; excess prolactin gives amenorrhea and galactorrhea, or erectile dysfunction and gynecomastia. Pituitary apoplexy (bleeding into the tumor) gives "sudden headache, ophthalmoplegia, and visual loss". [MSD Manual: Pituitary Tumors](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/pituitary-tumors)

## Childhood tumors

| Tumor | Where | Picture to recognize | Key fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pilocytic astrocytoma** | Cerebellum (posterior fossa) most often; optic pathway in NF1; can be above the tentorium | "Cystic mass with enhancing mural nodule". Histology: hair-like ("pilo") spindle cells with microcysts, Rosenthal fibers, eosinophilic granular bodies | Most common childhood brain tumor, benign, good prognosis (El-Husseiny p. 188); CNS WHO grade 1; usually a KIAA1549-BRAF fusion; surgery alone usually cures; GFAP positive as an astrocytic tumor (p. 189) |
| **Medulloblastoma** | Posterior fossa, most often the **vermis**, so **truncal ataxia**; can block the 4th ventricle (hydrocephalus) (El-Husseiny p. 188) | Solid mass with signs of raised pressure and cerebellar dysfunction. Histology in your notes: sheets of **small blue cells** with **Homer Wright rosettes** and many mitoses | Embryonal, the "most common malignant posterior fossa tumor in children"; peaks at 3-4 and 8-10 years; stage with MRI of the entire spine and CSF cytology |
| **Ependymoma** | Around the fourth ventricle (3rd most common childhood brain tumor, poor prognosis in your notes, p. 188); spinal cord in adults | Obstructive hydrocephalus. Histology: perivascular pseudorosettes, true ependymal rosettes | Spinal ependymomas can carry NF2 mutations |
| **Craniopharyngioma** | Suprasellar; the most common **supratentorial** tumor of childhood (El-Husseiny p. 189); peaks in children and again in middle age | Usually cystic and calcified (your notes: "tooth enamel-like" calcification); "motor oil" cyst fluid. Histology: wet keratin, palisading, stellate reticulum | From Rathke pouch remnants; CTNNB1 (β-catenin) mutation; vision loss, hormone deficits, poor growth |

- **Medulloblastoma:** [MSD Manual: Medulloblastoma](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/pediatric-cancers/medulloblastoma).
- **Ependymoma:** [PathologyOutlines: Ependymoma overview (De Los Santos Y, Cai C)](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumorependymoma.html); [MSD Manual: Gliomas](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/gliomas) (fourth-ventricle ependymoma "can manifest with obstructive hydrocephalus").
- **Craniopharyngioma:** [PathologyOutlines: Adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma (Lakis NS)](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumoradamcraniopharyngioma.html).

- **Medulloblastoma details:** presents with "vomiting, headache, nausea" and "unsteady walking or clumsiness"; molecular groups are WNT-activated, SHH-activated, group 3 and group 4. [MSD Manual: Medulloblastoma](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/pediatric-cancers/medulloblastoma) Arising in the midline cerebellum (vermis) in children, Homer Wright rosettes, "small round blue cells", and spread down the spinal canal ("drop metastases") are standard teaching; MSD states only that staging needs whole-spine MRI and CSF cytology.
- **Pilocytic vs medulloblastoma in the cerebellum:** a **cystic** cerebellar tumor in a child is most likely pilocytic astrocytoma (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 188); **solid**, malignant and spreading through CSF = medulloblastoma.
- **Medulloblastoma name:** your notes call it a primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET); MSD calls it embryonal and sorts it into molecular groups (WNT, SHH, groups 3 and 4), as in the table above. [MSD Manual: Medulloblastoma](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/pediatric-cancers/medulloblastoma)
- **Pinealoma (pineal germinoma):** causes Parinaud syndrome (can't look up, light-near dissociation, hydrocephalus) and early puberty in boys from beta-hCG (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 189; guide 27).
- **Stains in your notes:** neuronal tumors stain for **synaptophysin**; glial tumors (astrocytomas, ependymomas, oligodendrogliomas) stain for **GFAP** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 189). **Correction:** your notes also put meningioma and pituitary adenoma on the GFAP list. Neither is glial: your notes themselves say meningioma comes from arachnoid cells (p. 184), and pituitary adenomas are neuroendocrine tumors (PitNET, above). Do not pick GFAP for them (standard teaching).
- **Craniopharyngioma vs pituitary adenoma:** both can cause bitemporal hemianopia. A **child** with **calcification**, cysts and short stature points to craniopharyngioma. [PathologyOutlines: Adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumoradamcraniopharyngioma.html)

## Neurocutaneous syndromes (your notes, El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 180-182)
Skin plus nervous system findings in one case = think of these.

| Syndrome | Gene and inheritance | Key findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sturge-Weber** | **GNAQ** activating mutation; sporadic, not inherited | **Port-wine stain** in the V1/V2 area; same-side **leptomeningeal angioma** causing seizures and intellectual disability; episcleral hemangioma raising eye pressure (early **glaucoma**); **"tram-track"** calcification |
| **Tuberous sclerosis** | **TSC1** (hamartin) or **TSC2** (tuberin); autosomal dominant | Cortical **tubers** and subependymal hamartomas (seizures, intellectual disability); cardiac **rhabdomyoma**; facial angiofibromas; **ash-leaf** spots; renal **angiomyolipoma** (80-90% with bilateral angiomyolipomas have TS) |
| **Neurofibromatosis 1** (von Recklinghausen) | **NF1** on chromosome **17**; autosomal dominant, full penetrance | **Café-au-lait** spots, neurofibromas (mostly Schwann cells), **optic gliomas**, **Lisch nodules** (iris hamartomas), sphenoid dysplasia, scoliosis, pheochromocytoma |
| **Neurofibromatosis 2** | **NF2** (merlin) on chromosome **22**; autosomal dominant | **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas**, juvenile cataracts, meningiomas, ependymomas ("2 ears, 2 eyes") |
| **Von Hippel-Lindau** | **VHL** on chromosome **3**; autosomal dominant | **Hemangioblastomas** (retina, brainstem, cerebellum, cord), cavernous hemangiomas, **bilateral renal cell carcinoma**, **pheochromocytoma** |

- **Recent change (wording):** your notes use the old term "mental retardation". The current term is **intellectual disability** (intellectual developmental disorder); DSM-5-TR notes that US Rosa's Law (Public Law 111-256) replaced the old term in federal law (DSM-5-TR, Intellectual Disability, diagnostic criteria note, p. 38).

## Clinical vignette
A 6-year-old boy has had morning headaches and vomiting for 3 weeks and now walks unsteadily. MRI shows a cerebellar mass that is mostly a cyst with a small enhancing nodule in its wall. What will the biopsy show?

**Answer:** pilocytic astrocytoma, with Rosenthal fibers and eosinophilic granular bodies.
**Why:** a cystic cerebellar mass with an enhancing mural nodule in a child is the textbook pilocytic astrocytoma; its histology is a "biphasic, astrocytic neoplasm with piloid processes, Rosenthal fibers and eosinophilic granular bodies", and surgery alone usually cures it. [PathologyOutlines: Pilocytic astrocytoma](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumorpilocyticastrocytoma.html) His headache and vomiting come from raised pressure due to blocked CSF flow. [MSD Manual: CNS Tumors in Children](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/pediatric-cancers/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors-in-children)
**Trap:** medulloblastoma, also a cerebellar tumor of children, but solid, malignant and able to spread through CSF.

## Common traps
- Several ring-enhancing masses in an adult with a smoking history = metastases (lung), not glioblastoma. Over 80% of brain metastases are multiple. [MSD Manual: Overview of CNS Tumors](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/central-nervous-system-tumors/overview-of-central-nervous-system-tumors)
- Ring-enhancing lesion in HIV with a very low CD4 count and EBV in CSF = primary CNS lymphoma. Toxoplasmosis is the other ring-enhancing lesion to weigh; telling them apart by number of lesions and response to toxoplasma treatment is standard teaching.
- Psammoma bodies are not only a meningioma clue; papillary thyroid, serous ovarian and mesothelioma also have them (standard teaching).
- Bilateral vestibular schwannomas = NF2; optic pathway pilocytic astrocytoma = NF1. [MSD Manual: Vestibular Schwannoma](https://msdmanuals.com/en-in/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/vestibular-schwannoma); [PathologyOutlines: Pilocytic astrocytoma](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumorpilocyticastrocytoma.html)
- Calcification shows up in oligodendroglioma and craniopharyngioma; use age and site (frontal lobe adult vs suprasellar child) to choose.
- Cerebellar tumor with polycythemia = **hemangioblastoma** (erythropoietin); with retinal angiomas, think VHL (El-Husseiny p. 184).
- S-100 positive = schwannoma or melanoma (neural crest) (p. 185).
- Port-wine stain plus seizures = Sturge-Weber; ash-leaf spots plus seizures = tuberous sclerosis (pp. 180-181).

## Practice questions
1. A 62-year-old man has 2 months of morning headaches and personality change. MRI shows a mass with a thick enhancing ring that crosses the corpus callosum into both frontal lobes. What will the histology show, and what drug is given with radiation?
   - **Answer:** glioblastoma: pseudopalisading tumor cells around necrosis, with microvascular proliferation; temozolomide.
   - **Why:** a ring-enhancing "butterfly glioma" in an older adult is glioblastoma, treated with temozolomide plus radiation. [MSD Manual: Gliomas](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/gliomas) The histology picture is from [WebPath, University of Utah](https://webpath.med.utah.edu/CNSHTML/CNS139.html). That the butterfly pattern means spread across the corpus callosum is standard teaching.
   - **Trap:** primary CNS lymphoma, a B-cell tumor seen with very low CD4 counts in HIV (MSD HIV cancers page). That it can also cross the corpus callosum is standard teaching.
2. A 52-year-old woman has a first focal seizure. MRI shows a smooth mass over the left frontal convexity, attached to the dura, with a "dural tail". The tumor has grown during a past pregnancy. What will the histology show?
   - **Answer:** meningioma: meningothelial whorls and psammoma bodies.
   - **Why:** meningiomas come from arachnoid cap cells, show a dural tail, contain whorls and psammoma bodies, and are often progesterone receptor positive, which explains growth in pregnancy. [PathologyOutlines: Meningioma](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumormeningiomageneral.html) Convexity meningiomas can cause focal seizures. [MSD Manual: Meningiomas](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/meningiomas)
   - **Trap:** glioblastoma. A glioma grows inside the brain (intra-axial); a dural tail means outside it (extra-axial).
3. An 8-year-old girl is the shortest in her class and keeps bumping into door frames on both sides. Imaging shows a calcified, partly cystic mass above the sella; at surgery the cysts contain dark fluid like motor oil. Where did the tumor come from?
   - **Answer:** craniopharyngioma, from remnants of Rathke pouch.
   - **Why:** craniopharyngiomas are suprasellar, usually cystic and calcified, with "motor oil" fluid, and cause visual disturbances, hormone deficits and growth failure. [PathologyOutlines: Adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumoradamcraniopharyngioma.html) Bumping into things on both sides is bitemporal hemianopia from chiasm compression. [MSD Manual: Pituitary Tumors](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/intracranial-and-spinal-tumors/pituitary-tumors)
   - **Trap:** pituitary adenoma, which is an adult tumor and is not usually calcified (standard teaching).
4. A 24-year-old has hearing loss and ringing in **both** ears. MRI shows a mass at each cerebellopontine angle. Which gene is affected?
   - **Answer:** NF2 (neurofibromatosis type 2), with bilateral vestibular schwannomas.
   - **Why:** vestibular schwannomas grow from Schwann cells of the vestibular part of cranial nerve VIII into the cerebellopontine angle, and bilateral tumors are "a common feature of neurofibromatosis type 2". [MSD Manual: Vestibular Schwannoma](https://msdmanuals.com/en-in/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/vestibular-schwannoma) The NF2 gene on chromosome 22 encoding merlin is standard teaching.
   - **Trap:** NF1, which gives café-au-lait spots, neurofibromas and optic gliomas, not bilateral acoustic tumors.
5. A 35-year-old has headaches and an unsteady gait. MRI shows a cerebellar mass; his hemoglobin is high, and an eye exam shows retinal angiomas. His father had kidney cancer in both kidneys. What is the tumor, and which chromosome carries the gene?
   - **Answer:** hemangioblastoma in von Hippel-Lindau disease; VHL gene on chromosome 3.
   - **Why:** cerebellar hemangioblastomas can make erythropoietin; with retinal angiomas and bilateral renal cell carcinoma in the family, this is VHL, an autosomal dominant tumor suppressor disorder on chromosome 3. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 182, 184)
   - **Trap:** pilocytic astrocytoma, a childhood cerebellar tumor without polycythemia.
