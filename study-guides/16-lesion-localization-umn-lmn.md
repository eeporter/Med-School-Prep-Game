# Localizing nerve lesions: upper vs lower motor neuron

## In one line
First decide upper (brain and cord) vs lower (anterior horn to nerve) motor neuron from tone, reflexes and wasting; then use the side of the face, crossed signs and a sensory level to find the exact spot.

## Core mechanism
1. The **upper motor neuron (UMN)** runs from cortex down the corticospinal tract. It normally holds lower circuits in check, so a lesion releases them: high tone, brisk reflexes, clonus and an up-going toe (Babinski sign). [StatPearls: How to Localize Neurologic Lesions](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/)
2. The **lower motor neuron (LMN)** is the anterior horn cell and its axon in the root, plexus and nerve. It is the muscle's only supply, so a lesion causes early wasting, fasciculations, low tone, low reflexes and a normal plantar response. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/)
3. The corticospinal tract crosses in the **lower medulla** (the pyramidal decussation). A UMN lesion above the crossing weakens the **opposite** body; a UMN lesion in the spinal cord, below the crossing, weakens the **same** side. UMN signs always appear **below** the level of the lesion; LMN signs appear **at** the level and always on the same side (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 14, 16, 19).
4. Why spastic and brisk? The UMN has an overall **inhibitory** effect on the stretch reflex that sets tone and tendon reflexes, so removing it gives clasp-knife spasticity and hyperreflexia. The LMN is the reflex's efferent limb, so cutting it gives flaccidity and areflexia (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 17, 19).

## Compare and contrast
| Feature | Upper motor neuron lesion | Lower motor neuron lesion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tone | Increased (spasticity) | Decreased |
| Reflexes | Brisk, clonus | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar response | Extensor (Babinski: big toe up, other toes fan; normal in infants, gone by age 1) | Normal (flexor) |
| Side and level | Opposite side if above the decussation, same side if in the cord; always **below** the level | Always same side, **at** the level only |
| Wasting | None early (disuse later) | Early and obvious |
| Fasciculations | None | May be present (irritated anterior horn cells) |
| Where | Cortex, internal capsule, brainstem tracts, spinal cord tracts | Anterior horn, root, plexus, peripheral nerve |

[MSD Manual table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/distinguishing-upper-from-lower-motor-neuron-lesions), [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/); El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 19

## Localization rules
| Level | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cortex | Opposite face and arm/leg weakness (UMN type) plus cortical signs (speech: Broca = output, Wernicke = comprehension) | Left MCA stroke: right face and arm weakness, aphasia |
| Internal capsule | Opposite lower face, arm and leg weakness (UMN type) without sensory loss or cortical signs ("pure motor hemiparesis", a lacunar stroke of the posterior limb; El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 129-130) | Left capsule: right hemiplegia and right lower-face weakness |
| Brainstem | **Crossed**: cranial nerve palsy on the same side + long-tract sign on the opposite side. The cranial nerve gives the level: midbrain = CN 3, 4; upper pons = CN 5; lower pons = CN 6, 7, 8; medulla = CN 9, 10, 12 (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 81) | Left midbrain (Weber): left oculomotor palsy + right hemiparesis; left pons: left LMN facial palsy + right hemiplegia |
| Spinal cord | Both sides below the lesion, with a sensory level (exception: a one-sided lesion, Brown-Séquard syndrome, gives same-side weakness and loss of touch, position and vibration, with opposite-side loss of pain and temperature; at the level itself there are same-side LMN signs and loss of all sensation, and a lesion above T1 can add a same-side Horner syndrome (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 29); [MSD Manual: Overview of Spinal Cord Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/spinal-cord-disorders/overview-of-spinal-cord-disorders)); cervical = quadriplegia, thoracic = paraplegia | Thoracic cord compression: spastic legs, sensory level on the trunk |
| Root, plexus, nerve | LMN signs in that root's or nerve's territory | Radial nerve palsy: wrist drop |
| Muscle | Weakness with **preserved** reflexes (distinguishes myopathy from neuropathy) | Polymyositis |

[StatPearls: How to Localize Neurologic Lesions](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/). The radial nerve and polymyositis examples are standard teaching, not on the cited page.

Your notes' rule for the spinal cord: any cord lesion gives **2 same-side signs** (motor signs, and loss of vibration and position sense) and **1 opposite-side sign** (loss of pain and temperature), because only the spinothalamic tract has already crossed (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 23, 29). Your notes call Weber syndrome the **medial midbrain syndrome**: same-side CN 3 palsy (eye down and out, dilated pupil, ptosis), opposite-side spastic weakness and opposite lower-face weakness (p. 84).

## Facial weakness: central vs peripheral
- The upper half of the facial nucleus (forehead) gets UMN input from **both** hemispheres; the lower half gets it only from the **opposite** hemisphere. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/), [MSD Manual: Facial Nerve Palsy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/facial-nerve-palsy)
- So a UMN lesion (stroke) weakens only the opposite lower face; the forehead still wrinkles. The hypoglossal nucleus is the other one that gets only crossed input (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 14).
- An LMN lesion (facial nerve, e.g. Bell palsy) weakens the whole face on the same side, forehead included: the patient cannot raise the eyebrow or close the eye. Bell palsy can also reduce tearing, cause hyperacusis and remove taste from the front two-thirds of the tongue (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 60).

## Clinical vignette
A 66-year-old has sudden weakness of the right arm and leg. On the left, the eye is turned down and out, the pupil is dilated and the eyelid droops. Where is the lesion?

**Answer:** the left midbrain (Weber syndrome).
**Why:** a cranial nerve 3 palsy on one side with weakness of the opposite body is a crossed sign of the brainstem; the oculomotor nerve and the corticospinal tract sit together in the midbrain. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/)
**Trap:** choosing the right cortex because the limbs are weak on the right.

## Common traps
- Weak limbs plus brisk reflexes = UMN, even if the cause is in the cervical cord.
- Crossed findings (cranial nerve one side, body the other) almost always point to the brainstem.
- Forehead spared = central facial palsy; forehead involved = peripheral.
- A cord lesion can give **LMN signs at its own level** (anterior horn cells damaged there) and **UMN signs below it** (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 16, 23).
- Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) gives **mixed UMN and LMN signs** with no sensory or bowel and bladder loss; often flaccid, wasted arms (LMN at the cervical level) with spastic legs (UMN below). Anterior spinal artery occlusion looks the same **plus** loss of pain and temperature, with vibration and position spared (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 32-33).
- In acute spinal cord injury, reflexes can be absent at first (spinal shock) before becoming brisk. [MSD Manual: Overview of Spinal Cord Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/spinal-cord-disorders/overview-of-spinal-cord-disorders)

## Practice questions
1. A 58-year-old man has 6 months of weakness in his right hand. The hand muscles are wasted, with visible twitching under the skin (fasciculations), and the right triceps and finger-flexor reflexes are absent. Sensation is normal. Which part of the motor pathway is most likely affected?
   - **Answer:** the lower motor neuron.
   - **Why:** wasting, fasciculations and hyporeflexia are LMN signs. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/)
   - **Trap:** weakness alone does not localize.
2. A patient cannot raise the left eyebrow or close the left eye, and the left mouth corner droops. Where is the lesion?
   - **Answer:** the left facial nerve (peripheral, LMN), as in Bell palsy.
   - **Why:** the forehead is involved, so the lesion is below the nucleus's bilateral cortical input. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/), [MSD Manual: Facial Nerve Palsy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/facial-nerve-palsy)
   - **Trap:** a right cortical stroke would spare the forehead.
3. A patient has spastic weakness of both legs, brisk knee reflexes, upgoing toes and loss of pinprick below the umbilicus. Where?
   - **Answer:** the thoracic spinal cord, at or above T10.
   - **Why:** UMN paraplegia with a sensory level = thoracic myelopathy. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/), [MSD Manual: Overview of Spinal Cord Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/spinal-cord-disorders/overview-of-spinal-cord-disorders) (Umbilicus = T10 dermatome is standard teaching.)
   - **Trap:** choosing a cortical lesion; bilateral leg weakness with a sensory level is cord.
4. A 45-year-old woman has 3 months of trouble climbing stairs and combing her hair. Shoulder and hip strength is reduced; sensation and deep tendon reflexes are normal. Where is the lesion most likely located?
   - **Answer:** skeletal muscle (myopathy).
   - **Why:** preserved deep tendon reflexes separate myopathy from neuropathy. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493159/)
   - **Trap:** peripheral neuropathy usually causes distal weakness, sensory loss and low reflexes.
