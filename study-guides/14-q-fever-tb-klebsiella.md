## In one line
Match the pneumonia to its exposure: farm animals and birth products with no rash = Q fever; weight loss, night sweats and an upper-lobe cavity = reactivation TB; an alcoholic or diabetic with "currant jelly" sputum in an upper lobe = Klebsiella.

## Compare and contrast
| Feature | Q fever | Tuberculosis | Klebsiella pneumonia |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Organism | *Coxiella burnetii*, rickettsia-like bacillus | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, acid-fast (lipid-rich wall), aerobic | *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, encapsulated gram-negative bacillus |
| Spread | Inhaled aerosols or dust from sheep, cattle, goats (birth products, urine, milk); no tick or insect | Airborne droplet nuclei, person to person | Aspiration of mouth flora, direct inoculation |
| Who | Farmers, abattoir and veterinary workers, people downwind of farms | Close contacts, immunocompromised | Alcohol use disorder, diabetes |
| Lungs | Interstitial pneumonitis | Primary: lower zones (Ghon focus). Reactivation: apical and posterior upper lobes, cavitation in 20-45% | Usually upper lobes (standard teaching); **lung abscess** and empyema are characteristic |
| Key clue | No rash (unlike rickettsiae); hepatitis | Caseating granulomas | "Currant jelly" sputum; capsule is the main virulence factor |
| Chronic / complication | Chronic Q fever (under 5% of infections), mainly culture-negative endocarditis, especially with damaged or prosthetic valves | Latent infection in about 90% of those infected | Carbapenem resistance, mainly from **carbapenemases (KPC)**; also ESBL plus porin (outer membrane) loss, and efflux pumps (standard teaching) |
| Diagnosis | Serology: phase II antibodies = acute; phase I and II = chronic | Smear is least sensitive (needs about 10,000 bacilli/mL); culture detects as few as 10/mL; NAAT (e.g. Xpert MTB/RIF) is about as sensitive as culture ([MSD Manual: Tuberculosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/mycobacteria/tuberculosis-tb)); IGRA preferred for latent TB in the US | Sputum and blood culture |
| Treatment | Doxycycline; endocarditis: doxycycline + hydroxychloroquine | Isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol | Guided by susceptibility |

## What your notes add
- **Q fever (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 168):** *Coxiella burnetii* spores are inhaled from cattle or sheep amniotic fluid; no arthropod vector. Think of it in a farm-animal worker with fever over 10 days, myalgia, retro-orbital headache with light sensitivity, a **normal white count** and raised liver enzymes; pneumonia is a main sign. It is the most common cause of **culture-negative endocarditis**. Mnemonic: Q fever is "Queer": no rash, no vector, and the organism survives outside the host in a spore-like form. Treat with doxycycline.
- **Klebsiella (pp. 147-148):** gram-negative, oxidase-negative rod with a large capsule (the main virulence factor); mucoid, lactose-fermenting colonies on MacConkey agar; lives in the colon and upper airway. Aspirated, it gives lobar pneumonia in alcoholics and diabetics, with frequent abscesses and thick, bloody **currant jelly** sputum that is not foul-smelling; *S. pneumoniae* is still the most common pneumonia in alcoholics. Mnemonic: the **4 As**: Aspiration pneumonia, Abscess in lung and liver, Alcoholics, di-A-betics. Pneumococcal sputum is "rusty" (oxidized blood), which replaces a standard-teaching label below.
- **TB immunology (pp. 90-91):** bacilli survive in macrophages by blocking phagosome-lysosome fusion (sulfatides); **cord factor** (serpentine cords) inactivates neutrophils, damages mitochondria and releases TNF. Th1 cells release interferon-γ to activate macrophages, and macrophage IL-12 drives more Th1 cells; granulomas form when bacilli resist killing. About 90% stay latent (tuberculin positive) and 10% progress. Reactivation favors the lung apices in people with weakened immunity, with caseous necrosis and cavitation from hypersensitivity.
- **Tuberculin skin test (p. 92):** read at 48-72 hours. Positive at **5 mm or more** in HIV, other immunosuppression or recent contacts; **10 mm** in IV drug users, people in poverty or immigrants from high-TB areas; **15 mm** in healthy people. IGRAs perform about as well (p. 93).
- **Recent change (name):** your notes list *Enterobacter aerogenes* (Microbiology, p. 330). It was moved to *Klebsiella* in 2017 and is now ***Klebsiella aerogenes***. [LPSN: Klebsiella aerogenes](https://lpsn.dsmz.de/species/klebsiella-aerogenes)

## Core mechanism: tuberculosis
1. Inhaled bacilli are taken up by alveolar macrophages, which are central to the immune response. [StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/)
2. The body walls them off in granulomas, which may have a caseous (cheese-like) necrotic center. [StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/)
3. The first lesion is the Ghon focus, usually in the lower zones; a calcified, healed primary lesion with nodes is called the Ranke complex. (The Ghon focus plus hilar node involvement is the "Ghon complex", which is standard teaching.) [StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/)
4. About 90% of infected people develop latent infection. Reactivation later favors the apical and posterior upper lobes and often cavitates. [StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/)

## TB drug toxicities
| Drug | Key adverse effect |
| --- | --- |
| Isoniazid (INH) | Hepatotoxicity; peripheral neuropathy, prevented with pyridoxine (vitamin B6) in at-risk patients. Your notes: a prodrug activated by bacterial catalase-peroxidase (*KatG*; resistance = less KatG) that blocks mycolic acid synthesis; cleared by acetylation (fast vs slow acetylators); also **sideroblastic anemia** from B6 loss and drug-induced lupus in slow acetylators (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 350-351) |
| Rifampin | Hepatotoxicity; harmless **orange** urine, tears and other body fluids (it stains contact lenses); lowers levels of many drugs, including oral contraceptives, because it induces cytochrome P450. Your notes' 4 Rs: RNA polymerase inhibitor, Ramps up P450, Red-orange fluids, Rapid resistance alone. Rifabutin is preferred in HIV because it induces P450 less (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 349-350) |
| Pyrazinamide | Hepatotoxicity; raised uric acid, can trigger **gout**. Your notes: a prodrug (pyrazinoic acid) that works only at acidic pH, so it is best at killing bacilli inside macrophage phagolysosomes (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 351) |
| Ethambutol | Eye damage: blurred vision, central scotoma and **red-green color loss** (optic neuritis), often reversible on stopping; "eye-thambutol" (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 351-352) |

[StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/), [CDC: Adverse Events During TB Treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/treatment/adverse-events.html)

Your notes give the four-drug regimen as **RIPE** (rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) and isoniazid as the prophylaxis drug, "the only agent used as solo prophylaxis against TB" (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 348, 350).

**Recent change (latent TB):** CDC now "preferentially recommend[s] short-course, rifamycin-based, 3- or 4-month latent TB infection treatment regimens" over 6 or 9 months of isoniazid alone; isoniazid regimens remain an alternative when a short course is not an option. [CDC: Treatment for Latent TB Infection](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/treatment/latent-tuberculosis-infection.html)

**Correction (ethambutol target):** your notes say ethambutol blocks arabinosyl transferase "thereby preventing peptidoglycan cell wall synthesis" (p. 351). Arabinosyl transferase builds **arabinogalactan**, the mycobacterial wall layer that anchors mycolic acids, not peptidoglycan (standard teaching).

## Clinical vignette
A 35-year-old sheep farmer develops fever, severe headache and a dry cough two weeks after lambing season. There is no rash. Liver enzymes are mildly raised and the chest X-ray shows interstitial infiltrates. Which test confirms the most likely diagnosis?

**Answer:** serology for *Coxiella burnetii* (phase II antibodies for acute Q fever).
**Why:** inhaled organisms from sheep birth products cause fever, pneumonitis and hepatitis without a rash. Phase II antibodies mark acute disease. [MSD Manual: Q Fever](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/rickettsiae-and-related-organisms/q-fever)
**Trap:** looking for a tick bite or rash, which belong to true rickettsial diseases.

## Common traps
- Q fever: no arthropod vector and no rash, unlike Rocky Mountain spotted fever.
- High phase **I** antibodies suggest chronic Q fever (think endocarditis).
- Primary TB in lower zones; reactivation TB in upper lobes.
- Q fever gives a **normal** white count (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 168).
- Rifampin is a P450 **inducer**; rifabutin is preferred in HIV for that reason (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 349).
- Klebsiella and TB both like the upper lobes; currant jelly sputum and alcohol use point to Klebsiella; weeks of weight loss and night sweats point to TB.

## Practice questions
1. A patient with a prosthetic heart valve and past farm work has fever, weight loss and repeatedly negative blood cultures. Which organism and which antibodies?
   - **Answer:** *Coxiella burnetii*; high antibodies to phase I (and phase II) antigens.
   - **Why:** chronic Q fever usually presents as culture-negative endocarditis; phase I antibodies mark chronic disease. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/rickettsiae-and-related-organisms/q-fever)
   - **Trap:** phase II alone means acute disease.
2. Why does *M. tuberculosis* stain acid-fast?
   - **Answer:** its cell wall has a very high lipid content.
   - **Why:** the lipid-rich wall retains the stain. [StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/) (Your notes name the lipid: acid-fastness comes from the high **mycolic acid** content of the wall; El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 88.)
   - **Trap:** TB is not reliably seen on a Gram stain.
3. A 58-year-old man with alcohol use disorder has fever and thick, bloody, mucoid sputum. Chest X-ray: right upper lobe consolidation. Most important virulence factor of the likely organism?
   - **Answer:** the polysaccharide capsule of *Klebsiella pneumoniae*.
   - **Why:** the capsule is the main virulence factor; currant jelly sputum and upper lobe disease in alcohol use disorder are classic. [StatPearls: Klebsiella](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK519004/)
   - **Trap:** choosing *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (rusty sputum; El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 148).
4. A patient on 4-drug TB therapy reports blurred vision and trouble telling red from green. Which drug?
   - **Answer:** ethambutol.
   - **Why:** ethambutol causes ocular toxicity. [StatPearls: TB](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441916/)
   - **Trap:** isoniazid causes neuropathy, not vision loss. (Red-green color loss: El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 351.)
