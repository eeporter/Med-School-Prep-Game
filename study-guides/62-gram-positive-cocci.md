## In one line
**Sort a gram-positive coccus with three bench tests:**
1. **Catalase:** positive = *Staphylococcus* (clusters); negative = *Streptococcus* / *Enterococcus* (chains or pairs).
2. **Staph: coagulase.** Positive = *S. aureus*. If negative, **novobiocin**: sensitive = *S. epidermidis*, resistant = *S. saprophyticus*.
3. **Strep: hemolysis.**
   - **Alpha (green):** **optochin** sensitive = *S. pneumoniae*; resistant = viridans.
   - **Beta (clear):** **bacitracin** sensitive / **PYR+** = GAS; resistant / **CAMP+** = GBS.
   - **Gamma (none):** *Enterococcus* (grows in **6.5% salt and bile**).

## Core mechanism: how the toxins work
- **Superantigens** cause TSS, staphylococcal food poisoning, SSSS and scarlet fever. They include:
  - staph enterotoxins, TSST-1 and exfoliatin;
  - strep pyrogenic exotoxins.
- **How a superantigen works** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 41):
  - an ordinary antigen activates a few helper T cells. A superantigen links **MHC class II on the antigen-presenting cell** to the **variable region of the TCR**, so it activates **huge numbers of T cells** at once;
  - the T cells release **IL-2**, and macrophages release **IL-1 and TNF**;
  - result: **fever, capillary leak, hypotension, shock, rash and multiorgan failure**.
- **Pore-forming toxins** punch holes in cell membranes and cause hemolysis on blood agar:
  - staph **alpha toxin** (p. 40);
  - strep **streptolysin O** (immunogenic, the basis of the **ASO** test) and **streptolysin S** (not immunogenic) (p. 50).

## Staphylococcus aureus (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 38-44)
- **Lab:**
  - catalase +, **coagulase +**;
  - golden colonies with beta-hemolysis;
  - **ferments mannitol** (p. 39).
- **Carriage:** the **nose** in up to about 25% of people (p. 39).
- **Virulence factors** (p. 40):
  - **coagulase**: turns fibrinogen into a fibrin wall, which **walls off** the infection, so staph pus stays localized as an abscess;
  - **protein A**: binds the **Fc portion of IgG** at the complement-binding site, so less C3b is made and **opsonization and phagocytosis** fail;
  - **clumping factor**: sticks to damaged tissue and clots;
  - leukocidin, staphylokinase and hyaluronidase help spread;
  - **alpha toxin**: forms pores.
- **Pus-forming disease** (pp. 41-42):
  - skin: folliculitis, furuncles, carbuncles, abscesses, wound infections;
  - **pneumonia after influenza**;
  - spread through the blood: **osteomyelitis** (the most common cause of blood-borne osteomyelitis in children; GAS is second), **septic arthritis**, **endocarditis**, meningitis.
- **Toxin disease** (pp. 42-44):
  - **food poisoning:**
    - a food handler contaminates food, the food sits at room temperature, and the bacteria make a **preformed, heat-stable enterotoxin**;
    - vomiting and cramps start **within about 6 hours**;
    - it does not spread from person to person;
    - classic food: **mayonnaise salads** (potato, macaroni), cream pastries;
  - **TSS:** TSST-1 from a **tampon or nasal packing** left in too long. Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, myalgia and **diffuse red rash**, then shock. **Palms and soles peel 1-2 weeks later**;
  - **SSSS:** **exfoliatin**, mostly in **infants and young children**. Superficial blistering, **positive Nikolsky sign** (guide 46). Your NBME notes add that exfoliative toxins bind **desmoglein 1**, splitting the skin at the stratum granulosum (NBME TC p. 461).
- **IVDU:** *S. aureus* is the most common cause of **tricuspid endocarditis** in people who inject drugs, with **septic emboli to the lungs** (p. 44).
- **Correction (food poisoning):** your notes call staph food poisoning "the commonest type of bacterial food poisoning" (p. 42). The CDC's 2011 US estimates rank **nontyphoidal *Salmonella*** (about 11% of foodborne illnesses), ***Clostridium perfringens*** (10%) and ***Campylobacter*** (9%) above ***S. aureus*** (3%). Norovirus causes the most illnesses overall (58%).
  - For exams, staph is still the classic **fast-onset (hours), vomiting, preformed toxin** picture.

## Coagulase-negative staphylococci (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 45-46)

***S. epidermidis***
- Skin flora. Novobiocin **sensitive**. Does not ferment mannitol.
- **Biofilm:** foreign bodies get coated with host fibrinogen and fibronectin. *S. epidermidis* sticks to these proteins and makes an **extracellular polysaccharide matrix**. The matrix blocks antibiotics, opsonization and neutrophils (p. 45).
- **Removing the device** is often needed for cure.
- Diseases (p. 46):
  - **infections of catheters, prosthetic valves and joints**;
  - the most common cause of **prosthetic valve endocarditis** (also NBME TC p. 72: "history of mechanical valve").
- **Contaminant or real?** Growth in **multiple blood cultures** means real infection.
- **Treatment of methicillin-resistant strains:** **vancomycin**, with rifampin and/or gentamicin for serious infection (p. 46).

***S. saprophyticus***
- Novobiocin **resistant**. Causes **UTIs in sexually active young women** (p. 46).
- **Correction (how common):** your notes say both "almost half of all UTIs in sexually active young women" and "second most common cause (first is *E. coli*)" (p. 46). MSD: ***E. coli* causes about 80%** of UTIs in patients with structurally normal urinary tracts, and ***S. saprophyticus* about 10-15%**. "Second most common" is the part to remember.

## Streptococci: grouping (El-Husseiny Micro p. 47)
- Gram-positive cocci in **chains**, **catalase negative**.
- **Lancefield groups** (A-O) are based on the **cell wall carbohydrate** (C carbohydrate) (pp. 47, 61).

## Group A Streptococcus (*S. pyogenes*) (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 49-55)
- **Lab:**
  - beta-hemolytic, **bacitracin sensitive**;
  - **PYR positive**. Many labs now use PYR instead of bacitracin because bacitracin is not very specific (p. 49).
- **Virulence factors** (pp. 49-50):
  - **M protein**: the most important. It **blocks phagocytosis**, and its types (about 80) define strains;
  - **hyaluronic acid capsule**: looks like host connective tissue, so it is **not immunogenic** (a disguise);
  - **streptokinase**: turns plasminogen into plasmin, so it **dissolves clots**;
  - hyaluronidase and DNases help spread;
  - **streptolysin O** (**ASO** titer above 200 is significant for rheumatic fever) and streptolysin S;
  - **pyrogenic exotoxins** (superantigens): **SPE A = scarlet fever (erythrogenic) toxin**. They also cause TSS and necrotizing fasciitis.
- **Diseases:**
  - **pharyngitis**: the most common GAS infection, with exudative tonsils and fever (p. 50);
  - **scarlet fever** (pp. 50-51):
    - pharyngitis from a toxin-making strain;
    - **strawberry tongue**;
    - a rash that starts on the neck, armpits and groin, then spreads, **sparing palms and soles**. It feels like **sandpaper**;
    - **circumoral pallor**;
    - the skin **peels** at the end of the first week;
  - skin and soft tissue: impetigo, erysipelas, cellulitis, **necrotizing fasciitis** (pp. 52-53; guide 46);
  - puerperal sepsis, acute endocarditis, TSS (pp. 52-53).
- **Penicillin:** your notes say no penicillin-resistant GAS has been found (p. 54). MSD agrees: "No isolate of GABHS has shown penicillin resistance clinically."

## After strep: rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 53-55; NBME TC p. 74)
- Both are **non-suppurative** (not caused by live bacteria in the organ). They start **1-4 weeks** after the strep infection (p. 53).
- **ARF follows throat infection only**; **glomerulonephritis** follows **throat or skin** infection (p. 53).
- **ARF mechanism:** antibodies to **M protein cross-react** with **cardiac myosin** and other heart proteins (**molecular mimicry**) (p. 54).
  - It causes a **pancarditis** (all layers of the heart). The **mitral valve** is most often scarred, then the aortic valve.
  - The same cross-reaction with the **basal ganglia** causes **Sydenham chorea** (NBME TC p. 74).
- **JONES** major criteria (NBME TC p. 74):
  - **J**oints (arthritis);
  - **O** = carditis (the O looks like a heart);
  - **N**odules (subcutaneous);
  - **E**rythema marginatum;
  - **S**ydenham chorea.
- **Recent change (Jones criteria):** the **2015 revised Jones criteria** use different thresholds for **low-risk** and **moderate/high-risk** populations.
  - Monoarthritis counts as a major criterion only in moderate/high-risk populations.
  - **"Carditis" now includes subclinical carditis** found by echocardiography.
  - **Echocardiography/Doppler is recommended for every suspected or confirmed case**, even without a murmur (CDC, reviewed Aug 2025).
  - Evidence of a preceding GAS infection is still needed in most cases. Chorea and slow, chronic carditis are exceptions.
- **Correction (how long to give prophylaxis):** your notes say "life-long penicillin prophylaxis is recommended following a single attack" (p. 54). The AHA 2009 guideline sets the length by heart involvement (summarized in Holland et al. 2018):
  - **ARF without carditis:** until **age 21 or 5 years** after the last attack, whichever is longer;
  - **carditis without lasting valve disease:** until **age 21 or 10 years**;
  - **carditis with lasting valve disease:** until **age 40 or 10 years**, whichever is longer. Sometimes it is lifelong.
- **Glomerulonephritis** (p. 55; guide 04): immune complexes and complement in the glomerulus. Cola-colored urine, facial edema, hypertension, RBC casts. Recurrence is uncommon, so **no prophylaxis** is needed.

## Group B Streptococcus (*S. agalactiae*) (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 56-57)
- **Lab:** beta-hemolytic, **bacitracin resistant**, **hippurate +**, **CAMP +** (it widens the zone of hemolysis made by *S. aureus*) (p. 56).
- **Where it lives:** the **vagina** (about 15-25% of women in your notes) and the gut. Babies are infected **during birth** (p. 56).
- **Disease:** **neonatal sepsis, pneumonia and meningitis**, mostly in the first 2 months (pp. 56-57). "Group **B** for **B**abies."
- **Prevention:** **intrapartum antibiotics**, with **penicillin first-line** and ampicillin as the alternative (p. 57).
- **Recent change (screening time):** your notes cite the 2002 rule of vaginal-rectal culture at **35-37 weeks** (p. 57). ACOG (2019) moved screening to **36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks**. This gives a 5-week window of valid results that covers births up to 41 0/7 weeks (AAFP report of the 2019 ACOG committee opinion).
  - For penicillin allergy:
    - **cefazolin** if the allergy is low-risk;
    - **clindamycin** if it is high-risk;
    - **vancomycin** if the strain is clindamycin-resistant.

## Viridans streptococci (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 57-58)
- **Lab:** alpha-hemolytic, **optochin resistant**. Mouth flora.
- **Mechanism:** they make **dextrans** from sucrose. Dextrans stick to **fibrin-platelet deposits on damaged valves** (p. 57). Without previous valve damage, they cannot stick.
- **Diseases:**
  - ***S. mutans*** sticks to teeth and causes **dental caries**;
  - ***S. sanguinis*** causes **subacute endocarditis** on damaged valves after dental work (p. 58).
- NBME TC p. 72: "dental surgery + history of MVP" = viridans.

## Streptococcus pneumoniae (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 58-60)
- **Lab:**
  - alpha-hemolytic, **optochin sensitive**;
  - **bile soluble** (lysed by bile);
  - **lancet-shaped diplococci** (pairs) (p. 58).
- **Virulence:**
  - the **polysaccharide capsule** blocks phagocytosis: "no virulence without capsule";
  - **IgA protease** (p. 58).
- **"MOPS":** the most common cause of **M**eningitis (adults), **O**titis media (children), **P**neumonia and **S**inusitis (p. 59).
  - Pneumonia is **lobar**, with shaking chills and **rusty sputum**.
  - Meningitis CSF shows **neutrophils, low glucose and high protein** (pp. 59-60).
- **Vaccines in your notes** (p. 59):
  - the **polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23)** does not engage helper T cells, so it gives weaker memory;
  - **conjugate vaccines** link the sugar to a **protein**, which recruits **T-cell help and memory**, so they work in infants;
  - your notes give PPSV23 for **adults over 65** and high-risk patients, and a 7-valent conjugate for children under 2.
- **Recent change (adult vaccine):** in October 2024 ACIP recommended a **conjugate vaccine for all adults aged 50 or older** who have not had one. The options are:
  - **one dose of PCV21 or PCV20**; or
  - **PCV15 followed by PPSV23 at least 1 year later** (Kobayashi et al., *MMWR* 9 Jan 2025).
  - The 7-valent child vaccine in your notes has also been replaced by newer, broader conjugates (standard teaching).

## Enterococci and *S. bovis* (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 60-62; NBME TC pp. 72, 425)
- **Enterococcus** (*E. faecalis*, *E. faecium*):
  - **Lab:** **gamma-hemolytic**, catalase −, **PYR +**, Lancefield group D. Grows in **6.5% NaCl and bile** (p. 61).
  - **Disease:** gut flora that cause **UTIs**, **biliary infections** and **subacute endocarditis after GI or GU procedures**. The classic patient is an **older man after cystoscopy** (p. 61). NBME TC p. 72: "abdominal surgery or on hemodialysis".
  - **Resistance:** enterococci are **penicillin G resistant** in your notes. Penicillin is combined with an **aminoglycoside for synergy**. **VRE** and linezolid-resistant strains are rising (p. 61).
- **Other source (endocarditis drugs):** the AHA and ESC 2015 guidelines support **ampicillin plus ceftriaxone** for *E. faecalis* endocarditis:
  - when the strain has **high-level aminoglycoside resistance**;
  - as an alternative to ampicillin + gentamicin when **kidney damage** is a concern (Marino et al. 2022).
- ***S. bovis* (now *S. gallolyticus*):**
  - **Lab:** a non-enterococcal group D strep that **grows in bile but not in 6.5% salt** (p. 61; NBME TC p. 425).
  - **Bacteremia or endocarditis** is linked to **colon cancer**. Your notes give about 25% (p. 61). Every patient needs a **colon workup** (p. 62): "*Bovis* in the blood = cancer in the colon". NBME TC p. 425 gives **colonoscopy** as the next step.
  - MSD's Lancefield table links *S. gallolyticus* (formerly *S. bovis* biotype I) to "colonic adenomas or carcinomas, endocarditis". A 2019 clinical image report adds that these patients have a higher colorectal cancer rate and colonoscopy "may be necessary" (McAuliffe et al. 2019).

## Clinical vignette
A 31-year-old man who injects heroin has 5 days of fever, cough and pleuritic chest pain. There is a holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that gets louder with inspiration. Chest CT shows several small peripheral nodules, some cavitating. Blood cultures grow gram-positive cocci in clusters that are catalase-positive and coagulase-positive.

- **Diagnosis:** ***S. aureus* tricuspid (right-sided) endocarditis** with **septic pulmonary emboli** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 44).
- **Why the right side:** injected bacteria hit the tricuspid valve first. Clots shed from it go to the lungs (p. 44). The murmur rising with inspiration points to the right side (standard teaching).
- **Why coagulase matters:** it confirms *S. aureus*. Coagulase-negative staph would point to *S. epidermidis*, which needs a prosthetic valve or device (pp. 39, 46).
- **Lung infarcts** are usually **hemorrhagic**, because the lung has two blood supplies (pulmonary and bronchial) (p. 44).

## Common traps
- **Catalase first, then coagulase, then novobiocin.** Mannitol fermentation also marks *S. aureus* (p. 39).
- **Optochin** separates pneumococcus from viridans. **Bacitracin / PYR** separates GAS from GBS. **Bile and 6.5% salt** separate enterococcus from *S. bovis* (pp. 47-61).
- **ARF needs pharyngitis.** Glomerulonephritis can follow skin infection too (p. 53).
- **Rheumatic fever prophylaxis length depends on the heart** (AHA 2009 via Holland 2018), not "lifelong after one attack".
- **Echo for every suspected ARF case** under the 2015 Jones criteria (CDC).
- **GBS screening at 36-37 weeks**, not 35 (ACOG 2019).
- **Staph food poisoning is fast (hours) because the toxin is preformed and heat-stable.** Reheating the food does not help (pp. 42-43).
- ***S. epidermidis* in one of several blood cultures** may be a contaminant. **Multiple positive cultures plus a device** means infection (p. 46).
- ***S. bovis* bacteremia → colonoscopy** (NBME TC p. 425; El-Husseiny Micro p. 62).
- **Adults 50 and over** now get a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (ACIP 2024).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. Twelve guests at a summer picnic develop nausea, forceful vomiting and abdominal cramps 2-4 hours after eating potato salad that sat in the sun all afternoon. None has a fever. The cook had an infected cut on her hand. The leftover salad was reheated and eaten the next day by two more people, who also became ill. Which property of the causative agent best explains the second outbreak?
   - **Answer:** **a preformed enterotoxin that is heat-stable.**
   - **Why:** *S. aureus* from a food handler grows in food left at room temperature and makes a heat-stable enterotoxin. Heating can kill the bacteria but not the toxin, and symptoms start within about 6 hours (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 42-43).
   - **Trap:** **spores that germinate in the gut and release toxin there**. That would cause later-onset illness, and the short incubation here means the toxin was already in the food (standard teaching).

2. A 19-year-old woman has fever of 40°C, vomiting, watery diarrhea, muscle aches and a diffuse red rash on day 4 of her menstrual period. Her blood pressure is 78/40 mm Hg. A tampon is found in the vagina. Which mechanism best explains her shock?
   - **Answer:** **a toxin that links MHC class II molecules to T-cell receptors outside the normal antigen-binding site, activating large numbers of T cells.**
   - **Why:** TSST-1 is a superantigen. Mass T-cell activation releases IL-2, and macrophages release IL-1 and TNF, causing capillary leak and shock (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 41, 43).
   - **Trap:** **endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide) binding Toll-like receptor 4**. That is the mechanism of gram-negative septic shock. *S. aureus* is gram-positive (standard teaching).

3. A researcher studies an *S. aureus* surface protein that binds the Fc region of IgG at the site where complement attaches. Which host defense does this protein most directly impair?
   - **Answer:** **opsonization by complement (C3b) and phagocytosis.**
   - **Why:** protein A holds IgG by its Fc end at the complement-binding site. Less C3b is made, so opsonization and phagocytosis fail (El-Husseiny Micro p. 40).
   - **Trap:** **conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin**. That is coagulase, which walls off the infection (p. 40).

4. A 68-year-old man had a mechanical aortic valve placed 3 months ago. He has low-grade fevers and fatigue. Three of four blood culture sets grow gram-positive cocci in clusters that are catalase-positive, coagulase-negative and novobiocin-sensitive. Which bacterial property best explains why this organism infects the prosthetic valve?
   - **Answer:** **production of an extracellular polysaccharide biofilm.**
   - **Why:** *S. epidermidis* binds host fibrinogen and fibronectin that coat the device, then builds a biofilm that shields it from antibiotics and phagocytes. Multiple positive cultures mean real infection, not contamination (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 45-46; NBME TC p. 72).
   - **Trap:** **coagulase**. This organism is coagulase-negative.

5. A 21-year-old sexually active woman has dysuria and frequency. Urine culture grows gram-positive cocci in clusters that are catalase-positive and coagulase-negative. Which laboratory result would identify this organism?
   - **Answer:** **resistance to novobiocin.**
   - **Why:** *S. saprophyticus* is novobiocin resistant; *S. epidermidis* is sensitive (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 45-46). It is the second most common cause of UTI in young women after *E. coli* (MSD: about 10-15%).
   - **Trap:** **sensitivity to optochin**, which separates pneumococcus from viridans streptococci (p. 58). Those are catalase-negative chains, not staphylococci.

6. A 9-year-old boy has fever, sore throat and tender anterior neck nodes. A throat swab grows colonies with a clear zone of hemolysis. The organisms are gram-positive cocci in chains and catalase-negative. Which test result best confirms the most likely organism?
   - **Answer:** **a positive PYR test.**
   - **Why:** *S. pyogenes* is PYR positive. Many labs now use PYR instead of bacitracin, which is less specific (El-Husseiny Micro p. 49).
   - **Trap:** **a positive CAMP test**, which identifies group B strep (p. 56).

7. A 13-year-old girl who moved from rural Pakistan had acute rheumatic fever with carditis 2 years ago. Echocardiography now shows moderate mitral regurgitation. She receives benzathine penicillin G every 4 weeks. According to the AHA guideline, until when should this prophylaxis continue at a minimum?
   - **Answer:** **until age 40 or for 10 years after the last attack, whichever is longer.**
   - **Why:** carditis with lasting valve disease calls for the longest course in the AHA 2009 guideline (Holland et al. 2018 table). Recurrent strep throat raises the risk of more valve damage (El-Husseiny Micro p. 54).
   - **Trap:** **until age 21 or 5 years after the attack**, which is for ARF without carditis (AHA 2009).

8. A 24-year-old woman at 36 weeks' gestation has a vaginal-rectal culture that grows group B *Streptococcus*. She has no drug allergies. She asks what will be done to protect her baby. Which plan is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **IV penicillin during labor.**
   - **Why:** intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis prevents early neonatal GBS sepsis, pneumonia and meningitis. Penicillin is first-line, and ampicillin is the alternative (El-Husseiny Micro p. 57). Screening at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks is the current timing (ACOG 2019 via AAFP).
   - **Trap:** **oral amoxicillin now to clear the colonization**. Your notes give prophylaxis during labor, and treating before labor does not prevent colonization at delivery (standard teaching).

9. A 58-year-old man with mitral valve prolapse has had low-grade fever, fatigue and weight loss for 6 weeks. He had two teeth extracted 2 months ago without antibiotics. Blood cultures grow alpha-hemolytic, optochin-resistant, catalase-negative cocci in chains. Which bacterial product most directly allowed the organism to colonize his valve?
   - **Answer:** **dextran made from sucrose, which binds fibrin-platelet deposits on the damaged valve.**
   - **Why:** viridans streptococci enter the blood after dental work. Dextrans let them stick to fibrin and platelets already deposited on damaged valve surfaces (El-Husseiny Micro p. 57; NBME TC p. 72).
   - **Trap:** **a polysaccharide capsule that blocks phagocytosis**, the main virulence factor of *S. pneumoniae* (p. 58). That organism is optochin sensitive.

10. A 64-year-old woman has fever, headache, confusion and neck stiffness. CSF shows 2,400 white cells/µL (90% neutrophils), glucose 20 mg/dL and protein 250 mg/dL. Gram stain shows lancet-shaped gram-positive cocci in pairs. Which bacterial structure is the organism's most important virulence factor?
    - **Answer:** **its polysaccharide capsule.**
    - **Why:** *S. pneumoniae* is the most common cause of adult bacterial meningitis. Its capsule blocks phagocytosis, and there is "no virulence without capsule" (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 58-60).
    - **Trap:** **M protein**, the main virulence factor of *S. pyogenes* (p. 49).

11. A healthy 55-year-old man asks about pneumococcal vaccination. He has never had a pneumococcal vaccine and has no chronic illness. According to current CDC recommendations, which is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **a single dose of a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV20 or PCV21) now.**
    - **Why:** in 2024 ACIP extended conjugate vaccination to all adults aged 50 or older (Kobayashi et al., *MMWR* 2025). Conjugate vaccines recruit T-cell help and memory (El-Husseiny Micro p. 59).
    - **Trap:** **PPSV23 at age 65**, which is the rule in your notes (p. 59) and is now out of date.

12. A 74-year-old man with chronic kidney disease (creatinine 2.4 mg/dL) develops fever and a new murmur 3 weeks after cystoscopy. Blood cultures grow gamma-hemolytic, catalase-negative, PYR-positive cocci that grow in 6.5% NaCl and bile. The isolate shows high-level gentamicin resistance. Which regimen is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **ampicillin plus ceftriaxone.**
    - **Why:** this is *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis after a GU procedure (El-Husseiny Micro p. 61). The AHA and ESC support ampicillin + ceftriaxone for high-level aminoglycoside resistance, and for patients at risk of kidney damage (Marino et al. 2022).
    - **Trap:** **ampicillin plus gentamicin**. Synergy is lost with high-level resistance, and gentamicin is toxic to the kidneys (p. 61; Marino 2022).

13. A 71-year-old woman has a fever and cough 6 days after an influenza diagnosis. Chest X-ray shows bilateral infiltrates with early cavities. Sputum Gram stain shows gram-positive cocci in clusters. Which organism is most likely?
    - **Answer:** ***Staphylococcus aureus.***
    - **Why:** staph pneumonia is a frequent complication of influenza (El-Husseiny Micro p. 42). Clusters fit staphylococci (p. 38).
    - **Trap:** ***Streptococcus pneumoniae***. It also follows influenza, but it is a lancet-shaped diplococcus, not clusters (p. 60).
