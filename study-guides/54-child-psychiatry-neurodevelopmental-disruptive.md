# Child psychiatry: ADHD, autism, tics, learning and behavior disorders

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the neurodevelopmental disorders chapter (pp. 35-98) and the disruptive, impulse-control and conduct disorders chapter (pp. 521-536), by printed page; Osmosis HY Pathology Vol. 2, chapter 96 (pp. 711-716); Pharmacology High Yield (PDF pages). Treatment facts come from the MSD Manual. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied. Where another source disagrees or something has changed, a marked note says so. Separation anxiety and selective mutism are in guide 48, enuresis and encopresis in guide 50, and DMDD in guide 47.

Abbreviations: **ADHD** = attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder; **ASD** = autism spectrum disorder; **ID** = intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability); **ODD** = oppositional defiant disorder; **CD** = conduct disorder; **IED** = intermittent explosive disorder; **CBIT** = comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics (includes habit reversal training); **ABA** = applied behavior analysis; **SPCD** = social (pragmatic) communication disorder.

## In one line
**Neurodevelopmental disorders** start in early development and affect learning, attention, social communication or movement (ADHD, ASD, ID, learning disorder, tics). **Disruptive disorders** are about behavior. **ODD** is anger and defiance toward authority. **CD** is violating other people's rights or major rules. **IED** is impulsive, out-of-proportion rage outbursts (pp. 35, 521).

## ADHD (pp. 68-72)
- **6 or more symptoms** of inattention and/or 6 or more of hyperactivity-impulsivity for **at least 6 months**. Adolescents and adults **17 and older need only 5** (p. 68).
- **Inattention examples:** careless mistakes, can't sustain attention, doesn't seem to listen, doesn't finish tasks, disorganized, avoids sustained mental effort, loses things, easily distracted, forgetful (pp. 68-69).
- **Hyperactivity-impulsivity examples:** fidgets, leaves seat, runs or climbs (in adults may be only restlessness), can't play quietly, "on the go", talks too much, blurts out answers, can't wait for a turn, interrupts (p. 69).
- **Several symptoms before age 12** (p. 69).
- **Present in 2 or more settings**, such as home and school (p. 69).
- **Not explained by another disorder** such as a mood, anxiety or psychotic disorder (p. 69).
- Presentations: **combined, predominantly inattentive, or predominantly hyperactive-impulsive** (p. 69).
- **Who gets it:** about **7.2% of children** worldwide and 2.5% of adults. Male to female ratio is about **2:1 in children** and 1.6:1 in adults. **Girls more often present with inattention** (pp. 71-72).
- **Treatment (MSD):**
  - **Preschool children:** start with **behavioral therapy**. Add medicine only if behavioral therapy is not enough or symptoms are moderate to severe.
  - **School-age children:** behavioral therapy **plus** medicine.
  - **Stimulants** (methylphenidate, amphetamine salts) are the most widely used. Common side effects are **appetite suppression and insomnia**, and **height growth slows** over 2 years of use.
  - **Atomoxetine** is a **selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor** (non-stimulant). It rarely causes liver toxicity and suicidal thinking.
  - **Alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine)** are less effective and not first-line.
- Her notes:
  - Stimulants (amphetamine, methylphenidate) plus behavioral therapy (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 712).
  - Bupropion (an NDRI, norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor) is listed for ADHD (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 62).

**Recent change (stimulant warnings):** on **11 May 2023** the FDA required an updated **boxed warning** for all prescription stimulants. The warning covers the risks of **misuse, abuse, addiction and overdose**. Clinicians should **assess the risk of misuse before prescribing** and monitor during treatment ([FDA Drug Safety Communication](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-updating-warnings-improve-safe-use-prescription-stimulants-used-treat-adhd-and-other-conditions)).

## Autism spectrum disorder (pp. 56-66)
- **A. All 3 social communication deficits:**
  - poor back-and-forth social and emotional reciprocity;
  - poor nonverbal communication (eye contact, gestures, facial expression);
  - trouble developing and understanding relationships (pp. 56-57).
- **B. At least 2 of 4 restricted, repetitive patterns:**
  - stereotyped movements or speech (hand flapping, **lining up toys**, **echolalia**);
  - **insistence on sameness** and rigid routines;
  - intense, narrow interests;
  - **over- or under-reaction to sensory input** (pp. 56-57).
- Symptoms start in **early development** but may not show fully until social demands rise (p. 57).
- Specify **with or without intellectual or language impairment** (p. 57).
- **Onset:** usually recognized at **12-24 months**. Some lose skills (regression) (p. 63).
- **Who gets it:** about **1-2%** in the U.S.; **male to female about 3:1**, with girls probably underrecognized (pp. 62-63).
- **ASD vs SPCD:** SPCD has the social communication deficits **without** restricted, repetitive behaviors. If ASD criteria are met, ASD wins (pp. 54-55, 66).
- **Workup and treatment (MSD):**
  - Screening tools include the **M-CHAT-R/F** for toddlers.
  - **Metabolic and genetic testing** looks for treatable or inherited disorders such as **fragile X**.
  - ASD has occurred with congenital rubella, CMV, PKU, **tuberous sclerosis** and fragile X.
  - **Seizures occur in 20-40%**, especially with IQ below 50.
  - **Vaccines do not cause autism**; the study that claimed it was withdrawn after its author falsified data.
  - Treatment is intensive behavioral and educational therapy (**ABA**).
  - **Atypical antipsychotics (risperidone, aripiprazole)** help ritualistic, self-injurious and aggressive behavior. **SSRIs** help ritualistic behavior.
- Her notes: Osmosis Vol. 2 pp. 712-713 (diagnosis and educational and behavioral therapy).

**Recent change (prevalence):** DSM-5-TR gives 1-2% (p. 62). The CDC's latest surveillance (published **17 Apr 2025**, 2022 data) found **1 in 31 (32.2 per 1,000) 8-year-olds** with ASD, with boys 3.4 times as often as girls ([CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm)). Know the **3-4:1 male ratio** and that rates keep rising.

## Intellectual disability (pp. 37-43)
- **Three requirements:**
  - deficits in **intellectual functions** (reasoning, problem solving, learning), confirmed by clinical assessment **and standardized IQ testing**;
  - deficits in **adaptive functioning** (communication, social participation, independent living) needing support;
  - onset during the **developmental period** (p. 37).
- **IQ:** about **2 standard deviations below the mean**, which is about **65-75 (70 ± 5)** (p. 41).
- **DSM severity** (mild, moderate, severe, profound) is set by **adaptive functioning, not IQ**, because adaptive functioning decides how much support is needed (pp. 38-41).
- **Prevalence:** about 10 per 1,000 (p. 43).
- **Causes (MSD):** chromosomal (for example **Down syndrome**), X-linked (**fragile X**), and **fetal alcohol syndrome**, a common and **preventable** cause.

**Other source (severity):** MSD says basing severity on IQ alone is inadequate and classifies severity by **IQ plus the level of support needed**. DSM-5-TR (her document) uses adaptive functioning. On exam questions, use DSM: **severity = adaptive functioning**.

## Specific learning disorder (pp. 76-81)
- Trouble with **reading** (slow, effortful or inaccurate; poor comprehension), **writing** (spelling, written expression) or **math** (number sense, calculation, reasoning).
- The trouble must last **at least 6 months despite targeted help**, with skills well below age level on standardized tests (pp. 76-77).
- It **starts in school years**. It is **not explained by ID**, vision or hearing problems, or poor teaching (p. 77).
- Prevalence is **5-15%** of school-age children (p. 81).
- Her notes: dyslexia (reading), dysgraphia (writing), dyscalculia (math); treatment is modified teaching such as one-on-one tutoring (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 715).

## Tic disorders and Tourette's (pp. 93-96)
- A **tic** is a sudden, rapid, repeated, **nonrhythmic** movement or sound (p. 93).

| Disorder | Tics | Duration | Onset | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tourette's disorder** | **Multiple motor AND 1 or more vocal** (not necessarily at the same time) | **More than 1 year** since first tic | **Before 18** | p. 93 |
| Persistent (chronic) motor or vocal tic disorder | Motor **or** vocal, **not both** | More than 1 year | Before 18 | p. 93 |
| Provisional tic disorder | Motor and/or vocal | **Less than 1 year** | Before 18 | p. 93 |

- Tics are **not caused by a substance (for example cocaine) or a medical condition (for example Huntington disease)** (p. 93).
- **Course:** first tics usually at **ages 4-6**, often **eye blinking**. Severity **peaks at 10-12** and declines in adolescence (p. 95).
- **Who gets it:** males 2-4 times as often as females (p. 95).
- **MSD:**
  - Many patients feel a **premonitory urge** and can **suppress tics briefly** (seconds to minutes).
  - **ADHD is the most common comorbidity**; OCD is also common.
  - **Coprolalia** (obscene words) may occur but is not required.
- **Treatment (MSD):**
  - **CBIT (habit reversal) should be strongly considered first.**
  - **Alpha-adrenergic agonists (clonidine, guanfacine)** are first-line for mild to moderate tics in patients with comorbid ADHD.
  - **Antipsychotics (aripiprazole, haloperidol, pimozide)** are second-line.
  - **Low-dose stimulants can sometimes treat ADHD in children with tics without making tics worse**; atomoxetine may be preferable.

**Other source (Tourette drug of choice):** her notes list **tetrabenazine** and deutetrabenazine (dopamine depleters) as "DOC" for Huntington chorea and Tourette syndrome, with haloperidol as a D2 blocker (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 56). Osmosis says antipsychotics or antiseizure drugs only in severe cases, plus CBT and habit reversal (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 716). MSD puts **CBIT first, then alpha agonists, then antipsychotics**, and does not mention tetrabenazine. On exam questions, follow MSD's order: CBIT first, then an alpha-2 agonist (especially with comorbid ADHD), then an antipsychotic.

## Disruptive, impulse-control and conduct disorders (pp. 521-536)
| | ODD | Conduct disorder | IED |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core | **Angry or irritable mood, arguing and defiance, or vindictiveness** | **Violates others' basic rights or major age-appropriate rules** | **Recurrent impulsive outbursts far out of proportion** to the trigger |
| Count and time | **4 or more of 8**, at least **6 months**, with at least 1 person who is not a sibling | **3 or more of 15** in the past **12 months** (1 in the past 6 months) | Verbal or non-injuring outbursts **twice a week for 3 months**, OR **3 damaging or injuring outbursts in 12 months** |
| Examples | Loses temper, touchy, angry and resentful, **argues with adults**, refuses rules, deliberately annoys, blames others, spiteful | **Aggression to people or animals** (bullying, fights, weapons, **cruelty to animals**, mugging, forced sex), **property destruction** (**fire setting**, vandalism), **deceit or theft** (breaking in, conning, shoplifting), **serious rule violations** (out at night or truant before 13, running away) | Not planned and not done for gain (money, power); age **6 or older** |
| Key facts | Starts in **preschool years**. Defiant symptoms predict CD; irritable mood predicts **depression and anxiety** | **Childhood-onset type** if a symptom appeared **before age 10**. If 18 or older, check criteria for antisocial PD | 1-year prevalence about 2.6% |
| Page | pp. 522, 524 | pp. 530-531, 534 | pp. 527-528 |

- **Prevalence:** ODD averages about 3.3% (p. 524). CD has a median 1-year prevalence of 4%, rising from childhood to adolescence (p. 534).
- **ODD vs CD:** ODD lacks serious aggression, destruction, theft or rule-breaking (MSD).
- **Treatment (MSD):**
  - **ODD:** rewards-based **behavior modification** and family-based approaches. ODD-like symptoms often resolve once ADHD is treated.
  - **CD:** psychotherapy, family therapy and behavior modification. Medicines may include **stimulants** such as methylphenidate. Mood stabilizers or short-term **risperidone** help aggression.
  - **Up to 50% of youth with CD develop antisocial PD.**
- Her notes: conduct disorder in children, antisocial PD in adults; treatment is therapy (CBT, social skills, anger management, parent management training), not medicines (Osmosis Vol. 2 pp. 713-714).

## Clinical vignette
An 8-year-old boy's teacher says he cannot stay in his seat, calls out answers, interrupts classmates and loses his homework. His parents report the same at home since kindergarten. He makes careless mistakes, is easily distracted, forgets chores and is "always on the go". Hearing, vision and thyroid tests are normal, and he has no mood symptoms.

**Diagnosis:**
- He has 6 or more symptoms in both groups for more than 6 months.
- They started before age 12 and occur in 2 settings.
- This is **ADHD, combined presentation** (pp. 68-69).

**Treatment:** behavioral therapy plus a **stimulant** such as methylphenidate (MSD).

## Common traps
- **ADHD: 6 symptoms (5 if 17 or older), 6 months, several before age 12, 2 or more settings** (pp. 68-69).
- **Preschooler with ADHD → behavioral therapy first** (MSD).
- **Stimulant side effects: appetite loss, insomnia, slowed growth. Atomoxetine = norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, not a stimulant** (MSD).
- **ASD = all 3 social deficits + 2 of 4 repetitive or restricted patterns.** Social deficits without repetitive behaviors = SPCD (pp. 56-57, 55).
- **ASD workup includes fragile X testing; vaccines do not cause autism** (MSD).
- **ID severity is based on adaptive functioning, not IQ** (DSM p. 41).
- **Tourette = multiple motor + at least 1 vocal tic, more than 1 year, onset before 18** (p. 93).
- **Tics: CBIT first; ADHD is the most common comorbidity** (MSD).
- **ODD = defiance toward authority; CD = violating others' rights; IED = impulsive rage out of proportion** (pp. 522, 527, 530).
- **CD before 18; antisocial PD at 18 or older needs CD before 15** (p. 748; guide 52).

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 3-year-old boy rarely makes eye contact, does not point to show his parents things, and does not respond to his name, though his hearing test is normal. He lines up his toy cars for hours, screams when his route to daycare changes, and repeats phrases from cartoons. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **autism spectrum disorder**.
   - **Why:** deficits in reciprocity, nonverbal communication and relationships, plus 2 or more restricted or repetitive patterns (lining up objects, echolalia, insistence on sameness) (pp. 56-57).
   - **Trap:** **social (pragmatic) communication disorder**, which has no restricted or repetitive behaviors (p. 55).

2. A 6-year-old boy with autism spectrum disorder has a long face, large ears and moderate intellectual disability. His maternal uncle has intellectual disability. Which test is most appropriate to identify an inherited cause?
   - **Answer:** **DNA testing for fragile X syndrome (FMR1)**.
   - **Why:** MSD recommends genetic testing in ASD to find inherited disorders such as fragile X. The X-linked pattern (maternal uncle) and facial features fit fragile X (facial features and inheritance are standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **a standard karyotype**, which does not detect the fragile X repeat expansion (standard teaching).

3. A 12-year-old girl with autism spectrum disorder has begun biting her hands until they bleed and hitting her mother during changes in routine, despite ongoing applied behavior analysis. Which medication is most appropriate for these behaviors?
   - **Answer:** **risperidone** (or aripiprazole).
   - **Why:** atypical antipsychotics help self-injurious and aggressive behavior in ASD (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **methylphenidate**, which targets inattention and hyperactivity, not self-injury.

4. A 4-year-old boy is "nonstop" at home and at preschool: he climbs furniture, can't wait his turn, interrupts and runs into the street. Symptoms have lasted 8 months. Which is the most appropriate first treatment?
   - **Answer:** **behavioral therapy (parent training in behavior management)**.
   - **Why:** for preschool children, MSD starts with behavioral therapy and adds medicine only if it fails or symptoms are moderate to severe.
   - **Trap:** **methylphenidate as first-line**, which fits school-age children together with behavioral therapy (MSD).

5. A 19-year-old college student has struggled since grade school with careless mistakes, losing his keys and phone, poor organization, avoiding long readings and being easily distracted. His parents confirm these problems began before age 10 at home and school. He has no other inattention symptoms and no hyperactivity. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **ADHD, predominantly inattentive presentation**.
   - **Why:** for people 17 and older, **5** symptoms are enough, starting before 12 and present in 2 or more settings (pp. 68-69).
   - **Trap:** **no diagnosis because he has fewer than 6 symptoms**; 6 are needed only before age 17 (p. 68).

6. A 10-year-old girl with ADHD needs medication. Her older brother was recently treated for misusing his prescribed amphetamine, and her parents want a non-stimulant. Which drug is most appropriate, and what is its mechanism?
   - **Answer:** **atomoxetine**, a **selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor**.
   - **Why:** it is a non-stimulant ADHD drug (MSD). Stimulants carry a boxed warning for misuse, abuse and addiction, and the risk should be assessed before prescribing (FDA, May 2023).
   - **Trap:** **guanfacine**, an alpha-2 agonist, which MSD calls less effective and not first-line.

7. A 9-year-old boy has blinked his eyes, jerked his head and shrugged his shoulders for 2 years. For the past 14 months he has also cleared his throat and sniffed repeatedly. He can hold the movements back briefly in class but then feels a strong urge. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **Tourette's disorder**.
   - **Why:** multiple motor tics and at least 1 vocal tic, lasting more than 1 year, starting before 18 (p. 93); premonitory urge and brief suppression are typical (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **persistent (chronic) motor or vocal tic disorder**, which has motor or vocal tics but not both (p. 93).

8. An 11-year-old girl with Tourette's disorder is teased at school for frequent facial grimacing and grunting. The tics are mild to moderate, and she has no ADHD or OCD. Her parents want treatment. Which is the most appropriate first step?
   - **Answer:** **comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics (CBIT, including habit reversal training)**.
   - **Why:** MSD says CBIT should be strongly considered first; antipsychotics are second-line.
   - **Trap:** **haloperidol**, a second-line antipsychotic with movement side effects (MSD).

9. A 9-year-old boy's teachers say he argues with every adult, refuses to follow class rules, deliberately annoys classmates and blames others for his mistakes. This has gone on for 10 months at school and at home. He has never been in physical fights, stolen, set fires or hurt animals. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **oppositional defiant disorder**.
   - **Why:** 4 or more of 8 defiant, angry or vindictive behaviors for at least 6 months (p. 522), without violating others' rights (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **conduct disorder**, which needs aggression, destruction, theft or serious rule violations (pp. 530-531).

10. A 15-year-old boy has set fire to a neighbor's shed, tortured the family cat, broken into cars and threatened classmates with a knife. His first such behavior began at age 8. Which adult diagnosis is he at greatest risk for?
    - **Answer:** **antisocial personality disorder**.
    - **Why:** this is conduct disorder, childhood-onset type (a symptom before age 10) (pp. 530-531). Up to 50% of affected youth go on to antisocial PD, and early onset worsens the outlook (MSD). Antisocial PD requires CD before 15 (p. 748).
    - **Trap:** **schizophrenia**, which is not the expected course of CD.

11. A 26-year-old man has had 4 episodes in a year of sudden rage, each time hitting someone or breaking something: he punched a coworker over a minor comment, smashed his phone after a slow internet connection, kicked in a door after a parking ticket and shoved a stranger who bumped him so hard that the man fell and cut his head. Each outburst lasts minutes, is unplanned, and he feels embarrassed afterward. Between episodes he is not depressed or manic and does not use substances. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **intermittent explosive disorder**.
    - **Why:** 3 or more outbursts with injury or property damage in 12 months, far out of proportion to the trigger, impulsive rather than planned (p. 527).
    - **Trap:** **antisocial personality disorder**, whose aggression is part of a pervasive pattern of disregard for others, with lack of remorse (p. 748).

12. An 8-year-old girl with normal intelligence, vision and hearing still reads single words slowly and guesses at many of them despite 7 months of extra reading help. Her math and verbal reasoning are at grade level. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **specific learning disorder with impairment in reading (dyslexia)**.
    - **Why:** slow, inaccurate word reading for at least 6 months despite targeted help, not explained by ID or sensory problems (pp. 76-77).
    - **Trap:** **intellectual disability**, which needs global deficits in reasoning and adaptive functioning confirmed by IQ testing (p. 37).
