# Acne, rosacea, skin infections, pressure injuries, hives and angioedema

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: Osmosis Dermatology High Yield Notes (skin and soft tissue inflammation and infections pp. 43-58; urticaria, erythema nodosum and hereditary angioedema pp. 59-63; printed footer page numbers). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **MRSA** = methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*; **GAS** = group A *Streptococcus* (*S. pyogenes*); **SSSS** = staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome; **TEN** = toxic epidermal necrolysis; **NSTI** = necrotizing soft-tissue infection; **LRINEC** = Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis; **HAE** = hereditary angioedema; **C1-INH** = C1 esterase inhibitor; **KOH** = potassium hydroxide; **PAS** = periodic acid-Schiff.

## In one line
For skin infections, the exam asks two things: **how deep** the infection is (epidermis = impetigo; upper dermis = erysipelas; deep dermis and fat = cellulitis; fascia = necrotizing fasciitis) and **which bug** fits the story (*S. aureus* when there is pus; GAS when the edge is sharp and spreading). For hives, ask **which mediator**: **histamine** (itchy wheals, which antihistamines help) or **bradykinin** (non-itchy swelling, which antihistamines and epinephrine do not help).

## Acne and its look-alikes (pp. 44-45, 49-50, 57-58)

### Acne vulgaris (pp. 44-45)
**Four steps (p. 44):**
1. **Androgens** make sebaceous glands overproduce sebum.
2. **Keratin plugs the follicle** (hyperkeratinization), forming a **comedo**.
3. The bacteria multiply in the plugged follicle, and their **lipase** turns sebum into irritating **free fatty acids**.
4. **Inflammation** follows: papules, pustules, nodules, scars.

**Drugs and conditions that worsen it (p. 44):** **lithium, glucocorticoids, anabolic steroids**; **polycystic ovary syndrome** (extra androgens).

**Treatment by severity (p. 45):**
- **Mild or comedonal:** **benzoyl peroxide** (kills the bacteria), **topical retinoid** (unplugs follicles), azelaic or salicylic acid.
- **Mild to moderate:** add **topical clindamycin** to benzoyl peroxide (they work together).
- **Severe:** **oral isotretinoin** lowers sebum, bacteria and inflammation.

**Recent change (the bacterium's name):** her notes call it *Propionibacterium acnes* (p. 44). MSD now uses ***Cutibacterium acnes*** ("formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*") ([MSD: Acne Vulgaris, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/acne-and-related-disorders/acne-vulgaris)). Exams may use either name.

**Other source (acne treatment):** MSD gives these details.
- **Benzoyl peroxide** "Should be used in all patients if tolerated." "Monotherapy, particularly with topical antibiotics, is not recommended." The benzoyl peroxide limits resistance.
- **Moderate acne:** **doxycycline and minocycline** are the preferred oral antibiotics (or the newer **sarecycline**), tapered once acne is controlled. Her notes say to avoid tetracycline because of sun sensitivity (p. 45). Sun sensitivity is a known tetracycline side effect (standard teaching), but MSD still lists these drugs first.
- **Hormonal options for women:** combined oral contraceptives and **spironolactone** (an androgen blocker). **Clascoterone** is a topical androgen-receptor blocker for age 12 and up.
- **Isotretinoin** is for severe acne or moderate acne that has not responded. It "is teratogenic", so patients who can become pregnant need **two forms of contraception** and **pregnancy tests before and monthly during treatment**. Other effects: dry eyes and lips, joint pain, **depression**, **high lipids**.

### Rosacea (pp. 57-58)
- **Who:** onset at 30-50 years; fair skin, Celtic or Northern European ancestry (p. 57).
- **Look:** persistent **central facial redness**, flushing, **telangiectasias**, papules and pustules **without comedones** (p. 57).
- **Late changes:** thick bulbous nose (**rhinophyma**), mostly in men (p. 57).
- **Eyes:** dry, burning, gritty eyes and blepharitis; ocular rosacea is common (p. 57).
- **Triggers:** heat, sun, alcohol, spicy food, stress (pp. 57-58).
- **Treatment (pp. 57-58):** topical **metronidazole**, **azelaic acid**, oral **doxycycline**; avoid triggers; sunscreen; laser for the vessels.

**Other source (rosacea):** MSD says "Sunlight exposure is thought to be the most common trigger" and lists more follicle mites (*Demodex folliculorum*) among proposed associations. It adds topical **ivermectin**, **brimonidine** or **oxymetazoline** gel for redness, minocycline foam, "Doxycycline 40 mg once/day", and isotretinoin for hard cases ([MSD: Rosacea, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/acne-and-related-disorders/rosacea)).

### Hidradenitis suppurativa (pp. 49-50)
- **What it is:** painful, recurring, pus-draining lumps and tunnels (**sinus tracts**) in the **armpits, groin and under the breasts**, from clogged follicles (p. 49). It is also called **acne inversa**.
- **Look:** "double comedones"; scarring (p. 49).
- **Risk:** **obesity, smoking**, friction; more common in women (p. 49).
- **Staging:** **Hurley stages I-III** (single abscesses, then recurrent abscesses with tracts, then diffuse connected tracts) (p. 50).
- **Treatment:** oral antibiotics, steroids, anti-androgens, **TNF inhibitors**, drainage and surgery, stopping smoking and losing weight (p. 50).
- **Long-term danger:** chronic lesions can become **squamous cell carcinoma** (p. 49).

**Recent change (HS biologics):** her notes list TNF inhibitors (p. 50). MSD now says "adalimumab, secukinumab, or bimekizumab are also efficacious" (the last two block IL-17). For Hurley stages I-II, MSD uses tetracyclines (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline) until the lesions resolve, adding **clindamycin and/or rifampin** if the response is incomplete ([MSD: Hidradenitis Suppurativa, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/en-nz/professional/dermatologic-disorders/acne-and-related-disorders/hidradenitis-suppurativa)). MSD describes follicular occlusion and rupture rather than an apocrine gland problem (her notes, p. 49, mention both).

## Bacterial skin infections (pp. 45-53, 58)

### By depth
| Infection | Layer | Usual bug | Key look |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Impetigo** | Epidermis | *S. aureus*, GAS | **Honey-colored crusts**; bullous form from *S. aureus* |
| **Ecthyma** | Through the epidermis into the dermis | Same | Deeper, painful "punched-out" sores |
| **Erysipelas** | **Upper dermis and lymphatics** | **GAS** | **Raised, shiny, sharply bordered** plaque; legs or face |
| **Cellulitis** | Deeper dermis and fat | GAS, *S. aureus* | **Spreading redness with blurry borders** |
| **Folliculitis / furuncle / carbuncle** | Hair follicle | ***S. aureus***; *Pseudomonas* after **hot tubs** | Pustules on hair-bearing skin |
| **Necrotizing fasciitis** | **Fascia** | Mixed bacteria (type I) or **GAS** (type II) | **Pain out of proportion**, crepitus, shock |

From pp. 45-53; MSD.

### Impetigo and ecthyma (pp. 50-51)
- **Who:** contagious; **children**, daycare, warm humid weather, eczema (p. 50).
- **Nonbullous** (most common): red bump → blister → **yellow (honey-colored) crust** (p. 50).
- **Bullous:** painless bullae that leave a thin brown crust (p. 50).
- **Complication:** **post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis** (p. 50).

**Other source (impetigo):** from [MSD: Impetigo and Ecthyma, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bacterial-skin-infections/impetigo-and-ecthyma).
- *S. aureus* causes most nonbullous cases and **all bullous cases**, through "exfoliative toxins produced by staphylococci."
- Nephritogenic GAS can cause "acute postinfectious glomerulonephritis" in children aged 2-4; MSD does not mention rheumatic fever.
- Treatment: topical **mupirocin** (ozenoxacin is an alternative). Oral **dicloxacillin or cephalexin** for 7 days; clindamycin, TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) or doxycycline if MRSA is suspected.
- Her notes list "penicillins" (p. 51). Because *S. aureus* is the usual cause, MSD's oral choices are the penicillinase-resistant dicloxacillin or cephalexin.

### Erysipelas (pp. 47-48)
- Bright red, raised, **sharply demarcated**, painful rash, often called a **"forest fire"** rash; usually on the **legs**, also the face (p. 47).
- Fever and chills first (p. 47).
- Usually ***S. pyogenes*** (p. 47).
- High ASO (anti-streptolysin O) titer (p. 47).
- Treatment: oral penicillins or macrolides; IV for severe disease (p. 48).

**Other source (erysipelas):** MSD says erysipelas involves "upper dermis and above, including the lymphatics". Penicillin is first-line (penicillin V by mouth, penicillin G by vein), with cephalexin as an option. "MRSA is not common among patients with erysipelas" ([MSD: Erysipelas, updated May 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bacterial-skin-infections/erysipelas)).

### Cellulitis (pp. 45-46)
- **What it is:** non-necrotizing infection of the dermis and subcutaneous fat, usually on **one leg**, entering through a skin break (cracked skin between the toes, ulcers, eczema) (pp. 45-46).
- **Look:** warm, swollen, painful redness with **unclear borders** (p. 46).
- **Purulent** cellulitis (with furuncles or abscesses) points to *S. aureus* (p. 46).
- **Ultrasound** shows "cobblestone" fat (p. 46).
- **Treatment:** antibiotics, **vancomycin for MRSA**; elevation; drain any abscess (p. 46).

**Correction (wording):** her notes say cellulitis is "typically caused by streptococci (S. aureus, S. pyogenes)" (p. 45). *S. aureus* is a **staphylococcus**, not a streptococcus. MSD: "The most common causes of cellulitis are" **group A β-hemolytic streptococci** and ***Staphylococcus aureus*** ([MSD: Cellulitis, updated May 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/bacterial-skin-infections/cellulitis)).

**Other source (cellulitis treatment):** "Most cellulitis is nonpurulent." For mild cases, "Oral therapy is usually adequate ... typically with dicloxacillin or cephalexin". Add MRSA coverage (TMP-SMX, doxycycline, linezolid or clindamycin by mouth; IV vancomycin if serious) for purulent cellulitis or high risk. Treating **tinea pedis** lowers the chance of it coming back (MSD).

### Folliculitis (pp. 48-49)
- Pustules centered on hair follicles, never on palms or soles (p. 48).
- **Causes (p. 48):** *S. aureus* (including **sycosis barbae**, chin pustules after shaving); ***Pseudomonas aeruginosa* after hot tubs and pools**; HSV; fungi (tinea barbae).
- **Deep forms (p. 48):** **furuncle** (a boil) → **carbuncle** (several boils joined).
- **Treatment (p. 49):** topical **mupirocin** or clindamycin; oral antibiotics if extensive; TMP-SMX, clindamycin or a tetracycline for MRSA.

### Necrotizing fasciitis (pp. 51-53)
- **What it is:** infection spreading fast along the **fascial planes**, with toxin-driven tissue death (p. 51).
- **Types (pp. 51-52):**
  - **Type I:** polymicrobial (aerobes plus anaerobes such as *Bacteroides*, *Clostridium*), often after surgery or in diabetes. **Fournier gangrene** of the perineum is an example.
  - **Type II:** **GAS** (± *S. aureus*).
  - **Type III:** **saltwater *Vibrio vulnificus***.
  - **Type IV:** fungal.
- **Risk (p. 52):** diabetes, immune suppression, IV drug use, trauma or surgery, childbirth.
- **Clues (p. 52):**
  - **Pain out of proportion** to how the skin looks; the skin may look normal at first.
  - Later: purple skin, **woody hardness**, blood-tinged bullae, **crepitus** (gas).
  - Fever, fast pulse, low blood pressure.
- **Tests (pp. 52-53):** CT shows **gas in the fascial planes**; high WBC and CK, low sodium. LRINEC score ≥ 6 raises suspicion and ≥ 8 is strongly predictive.
- **Treatment (p. 53):** **emergency surgical debridement**; broad IV antibiotics (carbapenem or a β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor **plus vancomycin or linezolid plus clindamycin**); fluids and pressors; hyperbaric oxygen.

**Other source (NSTI):** from [MSD: Necrotizing Soft-Tissue Infection, updated May 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bacterial-skin-infections/necrotizing-soft-tissue-infection).
- The key clue is that "pain develops out of proportion to clinical findings."
- "Treatment should not be delayed while awaiting imaging results."
- The LRINEC score has poor sensitivity and should not be used to rule the disease out.
- "Clindamycin and linezolid are also often administered to inhibit toxin production."
- *Vibrio vulnificus* infection follows water injuries in warm coastal areas, especially in people with **chronic liver disease**, diabetes or weak immunity.
- Mortality in treated patients is about 20-30%.
- MSD does not mention hyperbaric oxygen (her notes list it, p. 53).

### Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (p. 58)
- **Who:** children **under 6** (p. 58).
- **How:** *S. aureus* releases an **exfoliative toxin** that breaks the junctions between cells near the skin surface, so the skin peels (p. 58).
- **Look:** tender red skin that peels in large moist sheets; **Nikolsky sign positive**; fever (p. 58).
- **Biopsy:** a split **high in the epidermis, in the granular layer** (p. 58).
- **Treatment:** antibiotics (p. 58).

**Other source (SSSS):** exfoliative toxins A and B "cleave desmoglein-1", and "The mucosal surfaces are spared". That separates SSSS from **TEN**, which involves the mouth and eyes, is triggered by drugs, and splits **below** the epidermis. Treat with nafcillin or oxacillin, or vancomycin where MRSA is common. "Glucocorticoids are contraindicated" ([MSD: SSSS, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bacterial-skin-infections/staphylococcal-scalded-skin-syndrome)). Desmoglein 1 sits mainly in the upper epidermis and is not needed in the mouth, which explains why the mucosa is spared (standard teaching).

## Nail fungus: onychomycosis (pp. 54-55)
- **Causes (p. 54):** mostly dermatophytes, especially ***Trichophyton rubrum***; also molds and *Candida*.
- **Patterns (p. 54):**
  - **Distal lateral subungual:** most common; starts at the tip.
  - **Proximal subungual:** starts at the cuticle; a **sign of severe immune suppression**.
  - **White superficial.**
- **Diagnosis (p. 55):** **KOH** prep, PAS stain of nail clippings, culture.
- **Treatment (p. 55):** **oral terbinafine** for dermatophytes; itraconazole for yeasts and molds; topical efinaconazole; griseofulvin if tinea capitis is also present.

**Other source (onychomycosis):** MSD says about 65% of cases are from dermatophytes such as *T. rubrum*. Proximal subungual infection "is a marker of immunosuppression". "Confirmation to establish a fungal etiology is required prior to treatment." Azoles give "lower clinical as well as mycological cure rates compared to terbinafine" ([MSD: Onychomycosis, updated Oct 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/nail-disorders/onychomycosis)).

## Pressure injuries (pp. 56-57)
- **Cause:** pressure, often with **friction and shear**, cuts blood flow over **bony prominences** (sacrum, heels, hips) (p. 56).
- **Risk (p. 56):** immobility (stroke, hip fracture), poor sensation, poor blood flow, **malnutrition**, moisture from incontinence, diabetes.
- **Stages (p. 56):**
  - **1:** intact skin with **red skin that does not blanch**.
  - **2:** partial-thickness loss; a shallow red bed or a serum-filled blister.
  - **3:** full-thickness loss with **fat visible**.
  - **4:** full-thickness loss with **muscle, tendon or bone exposed**.
  - **Unstageable:** the base is hidden by slough or eschar.
  - **Deep tissue injury:** damage under intact skin.
- **Care:** **turn bed-bound patients at least every 2 hours**; special mattresses; debridement; dressings; negative-pressure therapy (pp. 56-57).

**Recent change (name):** MSD: "'Pressure injury' has replaced 'pressure ulcer' as the preferred terminology" under NPIAP (National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel) guidance, because early damage may not yet be an ulcer ([MSD: Pressure Injuries, updated Feb 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/pressure-injury/pressure-injuries)). Her notes use "pressure ulcer" (p. 56). The stages are the same.

**Other source (pressure injuries):** "The sacrum is the most common location, followed by the heels." Protein intake of 1.25-1.5 g/kg/day is suggested. Topical agents such as **silver sulfadiazine** (listed in her notes, p. 56) "should be used cautiously". Systemic antibiotics are kept for cellulitis, bacteremia or osteomyelitis. **Osteomyelitis** is present "in up to 32% of patients", so suspect it when a wound will not heal (MSD).

## Hives, angioedema and erythema nodosum (pp. 59-63)

### Urticaria (hives) (pp. 62-63)
- **Mechanism:** a hypersensitivity reaction makes **mast cells degranulate**; mediators leak fluid from **superficial** vessels, producing **wheals** (p. 62).
- **Look:** itchy, raised, red wheals with pale centers. Each lesion fades in **under 24 hours** while new ones appear (p. 62).
- **Acute vs chronic:** **acute** < 6 weeks; **chronic** ≥ 6 weeks (p. 62).
- **Causes ("5 Is", p. 62):** **I**nfection, **I**njection (drugs, insect venom), **I**nhalation (pollen), **I**ngestion (foods, drugs), **I**nternal disease (autoimmune). Physical triggers: cold, pressure, stroking. **Dermatographism** (wheals where the skin is stroked) is the most common physical type.
- **Treatment (p. 63):** avoid triggers; **antihistamines**; for severe cases, steroids or leukotriene blockers; monoclonal antibodies for refractory disease.

**Other source (testing and treatment):** from [MSD: Urticaria, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/approach-to-the-dermatologic-patient/urticaria).
- "Usually, no testing is needed for an isolated episode of urticaria." For unusual, recurrent or persistent cases, MSD advises referral for **allergy skin testing**.
- Her notes list **patch testing** for urticaria (pp. 59, 63). Patch testing is the test for **type IV contact allergy** (MSD: Contact Dermatitis). Hives are a mast cell (often IgE) reaction, so the allergy test is a **skin prick test** (standard teaching).
- **Second-generation H1 antihistamines** are the cornerstone. If they do not work, raise the dose "up to 4-fold". H2 blockers and montelukast are adjuncts with limited evidence.
- Oral steroids only in short courses ("should not be used long term").
- **Urticarial vasculitis** suggests itself when wheals are painful, last **> 48 hours**, do not blanch, or leave purpura; biopsy shows leukocytoclastic vasculitis.

**Recent change (chronic hives):** MSD lists **omalizumab** (anti-IgE), **dupilumab** (anti-IL-4/IL-13, age 12 and up) and **remibrutinib** (an oral BTK, Bruton tyrosine kinase, inhibitor) for hives that persist despite antihistamines, with cyclosporine as another option. Her notes say "monoclonal antibodies and immunosuppressants" (p. 63).

### Hereditary angioedema (pp. 61-62)
- **Cause:** **autosomal dominant** deficiency of **C1-inhibitor** (p. 61). C1-INH normally restrains complement and the contact system (factor XII → kallikrein), so **bradykinin** builds up (p. 61).
- **Look:** recurrent **painless, non-itchy, non-pitting swelling** of the face, lips, tongue, limbs and genitals, with **no hives** (p. 61).
- **Gut attacks:** severe colicky **abdominal pain**, vomiting; can mimic a surgical emergency (p. 61).
- **Airway:** laryngeal swelling can kill; without treatment, death by asphyxia occurs in about 25% (p. 61).
- **Course:** attacks last up to **72 hours** and start in childhood. Triggers include minor trauma and mood or temperature changes, but often there is no clear trigger (p. 61). Dental and airway procedures need preventive treatment (MSD, below).
- **Tests:** complement testing (p. 61).
- **Treatment of attacks (p. 61):** **C1-INH concentrate**, **ecallantide** (kallikrein inhibitor), **icatibant** (bradykinin B2-receptor blocker); fresh-frozen plasma if none is available.
- **Prevention (p. 62):** **danazol** (an androgen that raises C4) or C1-INH. **Avoid ACE inhibitors and estrogen.**

**Other source (HAE):** from [MSD: Hereditary and Acquired C1 Inhibitor Deficiency, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/immunology-allergic-disorders/allergic-autoimmune-and-other-hypersensitivity-disorders/hereditary-and-acquired-c1-inhibitor-deficiency?ruleredirectid=742autoredirectid=24953).
- Diagnosis: "Low levels of C4, even between episodes" plus a low C1-INH level or function. "Pruritus, urticaria, and bronchospasm do not occur."
- "Glucocorticoids, epinephrine, and antihistamines are usually not effective in bradykinin-mediated angioedema."
- Give short-term prevention (C1-INH preferred) before dental or airway procedures.
- **ACE inhibitors** raise bradykinin because ACE normally breaks it down (standard teaching). MSD lists estrogen-containing drugs and tamoxifen as triggers.

**Recent change (HAE drugs):** MSD now lists **sebetralstat** (an oral kallikrein inhibitor) with C1-INH, ecallantide and icatibant as first-line for attacks. For long-term prevention it lists **lanadelumab, berotralstat, garadacimab, donidalorsen** and plasma-derived C1-INH. Attenuated androgens such as **danazol** (her notes' first choice, p. 62) are now used "in settings where other agents are unavailable" and "should not be used in children."

### Erythema nodosum (pp. 59-60)
- **What it is:** inflammation of the **subcutaneous fat** (**panniculitis**), probably a **type IV** delayed reaction (p. 59).
- **Look:** red, **painful nodules on the shins**, often after fever and joint pain; they fade like bruises over about 2 weeks (pp. 59-60).
- **Causes (pp. 59-60):** 30-50% unknown.
  - **Infections:** strep, TB, *Yersinia*, histoplasmosis, **coccidioidomycosis**.
  - **Sarcoidosis**, **inflammatory bowel disease**, Behçet disease.
  - **Drugs:** sulfonamides, **oral contraceptives**.
  - Cancers.
- **Workup (p. 60):** CBC, ESR, ASO titer, throat culture, tuberculin test, **chest X-ray**.
- **Treatment (p. 60):** treat the cause; rest, elevation, NSAIDs; potassium iodide for stubborn cases.

**Other source (erythema nodosum):** MSD: "The most common causes of erythema nodosum are streptococcal infections" (especially in children), sarcoidosis and inflammatory bowel disease; pregnancy is another cause. It "almost always resolves spontaneously, typically over approximately 3 to 6 weeks." Systemic steroids are a last resort because they can worsen a hidden infection ([MSD: Erythema Nodosum, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hypersensitivity-and-reactive-skin-disorders/erythema-nodosum)). Painful shin nodules plus **hilar lymph node enlargement** on chest X-ray suggests **sarcoidosis** (standard teaching).

## Clinical vignette
A 58-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes cut his calf on a fence 2 days ago. Now his leg is swollen, and the pain is far worse than the faint redness suggests. The skin is turning dusky purple with blood-tinged blisters, there is crackling under the skin, and his blood pressure is 82/50 mm Hg. This is **necrotizing fasciitis** (pp. 51-52). The next step is **emergency surgical exploration and debridement**, not waiting for a CT scan (MSD). Start broad IV antibiotics including vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem, and clindamycin to block toxin production (p. 53; MSD).

## Common traps
- **Sharp raised border = erysipelas (GAS); blurry border = cellulitis; pus = think *S. aureus*/MRSA.**
- **Impetigo → glomerulonephritis**, not rheumatic fever (MSD mentions only glomerulonephritis).
- **SSSS spares the mouth** (desmoglein 1, granular layer, child); **TEN involves the mouth** (drug, adult, below the epidermis).
- **Necrotizing fasciitis = pain out of proportion → surgery now.** Liver disease plus seawater or oysters → *Vibrio vulnificus*.
- **Hot tub folliculitis = *Pseudomonas*.**
- **Rosacea has no comedones**; acne does.
- **Isotretinoin is teratogenic**: two forms of birth control and pregnancy tests.
- **Proximal subungual onychomycosis → look for immune suppression (HIV).**
- **Pressure injury staging:** fat visible = stage 3; bone, tendon or muscle = stage 4; base hidden = unstageable.
- **Itchy wheals = histamine** (antihistamines work). **Non-itchy swelling with low C4 = bradykinin** (C1-INH deficiency; antihistamines and epinephrine fail; ACE inhibitors make it worse).
- **Patch test = contact dermatitis (type IV). Skin prick test = hives and IgE allergy.**

## Practice questions

1. A 17-year-old girl has severe nodular acne on her face and back that has not improved with 4 months of doxycycline plus topical benzoyl peroxide and a retinoid. Her dermatologist plans oral isotretinoin. Which is required before she starts the drug?
   - **Answer:** **a negative pregnancy test, with two forms of contraception**.
   - **Why:** isotretinoin "is teratogenic", so pregnancy tests before and monthly during treatment, and two methods of contraception, are required (MSD; p. 45 for its use in severe acne).
   - **Trap:** **a baseline serum potassium**, which is checked for **spironolactone**, another acne drug.

2. A 71-year-old woman has a 1-day history of fever and a painful, bright red, shiny, raised plaque on her right cheek. Its edge is sharply defined and can be felt as a ridge. Which organism is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** ***Streptococcus pyogenes* (group A β-hemolytic streptococcus)**.
   - **Why:** a raised plaque with a sharp edge is **erysipelas**, an infection of the upper dermis and lymphatics, usually from GAS (p. 47; MSD).
   - **Trap:** ***Staphylococcus aureus***, which is more typical of purulent cellulitis, abscesses and folliculitis.

3. A 4-year-old boy had crusted, honey-colored sores around his nose 3 weeks ago that cleared without treatment. Now he has puffy eyelids, dark cola-colored urine and a blood pressure of 128/84 mm Hg. Which is the most likely cause of his new findings?
   - **Answer:** **post-streptococcal (postinfectious) glomerulonephritis** after a group A streptococcal skin infection.
   - **Why:** impetigo with nephritogenic GAS can be followed by immune-complex glomerulonephritis in young children (p. 50; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **acute rheumatic fever**, which follows strep **throat** infection, not skin infection (standard teaching).

4. A 2-year-old girl has a fever and is irritable. Her skin is diffusely red and tender, especially around the mouth and in the skin folds, and large areas peel off with gentle rubbing. Her lips, mouth and eyes are not involved. Biopsy shows a split high in the epidermis at the granular layer. The responsible toxin cleaves which protein?
   - **Answer:** **desmoglein 1**.
   - **Why:** this is SSSS. *S. aureus* exfoliative toxins A and B "cleave desmoglein-1" in the upper epidermis, and mucosa is spared (p. 58; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **desmoglein 3**, the main target in pemphigus vulgaris, which typically starts in the mouth.

5. A 64-year-old man with diabetes has 1 day of fever and rapidly spreading, exquisitely painful swelling of the scrotum and perineum. The skin is dusky, crepitus is felt, and CT shows gas tracking along the fascia. Cultures later grow *Escherichia coli*, *Bacteroides* and streptococci. He is taken to surgery and given IV vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam and clindamycin. What is the main reason clindamycin is added?
   - **Answer:** **to stop the bacteria from making toxins (it blocks bacterial protein synthesis)**.
   - **Why:** this is Fournier gangrene, a type I (polymicrobial) necrotizing infection of the perineum (p. 51). MSD: "Clindamycin and linezolid are also often administered to inhibit toxin production." Clindamycin binds the 50S ribosomal subunit (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **to cover anaerobes such as *Bacteroides***: piperacillin-tazobactam already covers them, so this is not the main reason for adding clindamycin.

6. A 62-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis cut his hand while shucking oysters on the Gulf Coast. Within 24 hours he has spreading swelling, hemorrhagic bullae and septic shock. Which organism is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** ***Vibrio vulnificus***.
   - **Why:** necrotizing infection after a saltwater or seafood injury, especially with chronic liver disease (p. 52; MSD).
   - **Trap:** ***Pasteurella multocida***, which infects cat and dog bites (standard teaching).

7. An 84-year-old woman who has been bedbound since a stroke has a wound over her sacrum. It is a full-thickness skin defect in which yellow subcutaneous fat is visible at the base. No muscle, tendon or bone is exposed, and the base is not covered by eschar. What is the stage of this pressure injury?
   - **Answer:** **stage 3**.
   - **Why:** stage 3 is full-thickness skin loss into the subcutaneous fat without exposed muscle, tendon or bone (p. 56; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **stage 4**, which requires exposed muscle, tendon or bone.

8. A 43-year-old woman has had redness of her cheeks and nose for 3 years. It flares with red wine, hot drinks and sun. Exam shows central facial erythema, fine telangiectasias, and scattered papules and pustules. Which finding would best distinguish her condition from acne vulgaris?
   - **Answer:** **no comedones (blackheads or whiteheads)**.
   - **Why:** papulopustular rosacea looks "similar to acne, no comedones" (p. 57); comedones come from the follicular plugging of acne (p. 44).
   - **Trap:** **pustules**, which occur in both rosacea and acne.

9. A 19-year-old man has had several episodes of painless, non-itchy swelling of his lips, hands and scrotum, each lasting 2-3 days, and one episode of severe crampy abdominal pain with vomiting. His father has similar episodes. He has never had hives. Serum C4 is low between attacks. Excess of which mediator causes his swelling?
   - **Answer:** **bradykinin**.
   - **Why:** hereditary angioedema from C1-inhibitor deficiency lets the kallikrein-bradykinin pathway run unchecked (p. 61); MSD calls it "bradykinin-mediated angioedema" with low C4.
   - **Trap:** **histamine**, the mediator of allergic angioedema with hives. This is why antihistamines and epinephrine "are usually not effective" in HAE (MSD).

10. A 35-year-old woman has had itchy wheals on her trunk and arms almost every day for 3 months. Each wheal fades within a day without bruising. No trigger is found. Standard-dose cetirizine taken daily helps only a little. Which is the most appropriate next step?
    - **Answer:** **increase the second-generation antihistamine dose (up to four times the standard dose)**.
    - **Why:** chronic urticaria (≥ 6 weeks). MSD advises "dose escalation of second-generation antihistamines up to 4-fold" before adding omalizumab or other drugs.
    - **Trap:** **a long-term daily oral prednisone course**: MSD says steroids "should not be used long term."

11. A 29-year-old woman has 1 week of fever, ankle pain, and painful red nodules on both shins. Chest X-ray shows enlarged lymph nodes at both lung hila. Which underlying condition is most likely?
    - **Answer:** **sarcoidosis**.
    - **Why:** erythema nodosum is a panniculitis triggered by infections, sarcoidosis, IBD and drugs (p. 59; MSD); shin nodules with bilateral hilar nodes point to sarcoidosis (standard teaching).
    - **Trap:** **pulmonary tuberculosis**, which can also cause erythema nodosum but usually gives an apical or cavitary lung lesion rather than symmetric hilar nodes alone (standard teaching).

12. A 40-year-old man has white discoloration that starts at the cuticle and spreads toward the tips of several fingernails. KOH preparation shows fungal hyphae. Which evaluation is most appropriate next?
    - **Answer:** **testing for immune suppression, including HIV**.
    - **Why:** proximal subungual onychomycosis is "a marker of immunosuppression" (MSD) and a "sign of severely immunocompromised state" (p. 54).
    - **Trap:** **a nail biopsy for melanoma**: a white streak from the cuticle with hyphae on KOH is fungal, not pigmented.
