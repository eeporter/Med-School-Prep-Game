# Acid-base disorders

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Nephrology (acid-base pp. 65-74). Each fact is also checked against StatPearls and the MSD Manual; where a different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Read the pH, find which value (PaCO2 or HCO3) explains it, then check whether the other value compensated as expected. If it did not, there is a second disorder.

## Core mechanism
1. pH depends on the ratio of bicarbonate (HCO3, controlled by the kidneys) to the arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2, controlled by the lungs). Normal: pH 7.35-7.45, PaCO2 35-45 mmHg, HCO3 22-26 mEq/L. [StatPearls: Acid Base Balance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507807/)
2. pH below 7.35 is acidemia; above 7.45 is alkalemia. [MSD Manual: Acid-Base Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/endocrine-and-metabolic-disorders/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/acid-base-disorders)
3. The primary disorder is the value that moved in the direction that explains the pH: low HCO3 in acidemia is metabolic acidosis; high PaCO2 in acidemia is respiratory acidosis; high HCO3 in alkalemia is metabolic alkalosis; low PaCO2 in alkalemia is respiratory alkalosis. MSD: "Changes in Pco2 reflect the respiratory component, and changes in HCO3− reflect the metabolic component." [MSD Manual: Acid-Base Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/endocrine-and-metabolic-disorders/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/acid-base-disorders)
4. The other system compensates in the same direction, but never fully back to normal: MSD says compensation "cannot return pH completely to normal and never overshoots." Renal compensation builds "over days". [MSD Manual: Acid-Base Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/endocrine-and-metabolic-disorders/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/acid-base-disorders) That the lungs respond within minutes to hours is standard teaching.
5. In metabolic acidosis, calculate the anion gap to split the causes (MSD: it "should always be calculated"; [MSD Manual: Acid-Base Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/endocrine-and-metabolic-disorders/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/acid-base-disorders)), then the delta ratio to catch a hidden second metabolic disorder. [StatPearls: AG and non-AG acidosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/)

## Your notes' two-step method (El-Husseiny Nephrology, pp. 65-66)
1. **pH:** low = acidosis, high = alkalosis.
2. **CO2 and HCO3:** in acidosis, a high CO2 means a respiratory part; if CO2 is not high, it must be metabolic (low HCO3). In alkalosis, a low CO2 means a respiratory part; if CO2 is not low, it must be metabolic (high HCO3).
3. **Opposite moves = combined disorder:** CO2 up with HCO3 down is combined respiratory and metabolic **acidosis** (the more commonly tested one); CO2 down with HCO3 up is combined **alkalosis**.
- **Timing:** the lungs compensate for a metabolic problem **quickly**; the kidneys compensate for a respiratory problem **slowly** (over days), so an **acute** respiratory disorder looks uncompensated (pp. 70-71).

## High-yield facts
- Anion gap (AG) = Na − (Cl + HCO3); normal about 8-12 mEq/L. [StatPearls: AG and non-AG acidosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/) Your notes: a gap over 12 **may** mean metabolic acidosis; a gap over **20 always** does (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 66).
- Winters formula (metabolic acidosis): expected PaCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3 + 8 ± 2. Measured PaCO2 higher means added respiratory acidosis; lower means added respiratory alkalosis. [StatPearls: Acid Base Balance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507807/) Your notes add: a **normal** PaCO2 in metabolic acidosis is not reassuring; it means the lungs failed to compensate, so there is also a respiratory acidosis (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 71).
- Respiratory acidosis: HCO3 rises about 1 mEq/L per 10 mmHg PaCO2 rise if acute, about 4 if chronic. [StatPearls: Respiratory Acidosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482430/)
- Respiratory alkalosis: HCO3 falls about 1-2 mEq/L per 10 mmHg PaCO2 drop if acute and about 4-5 once the kidneys have compensated over days; chronic respiratory alkalosis can bring the pH close to normal. [MSD Manual table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/primary-changes-and-compensations-in-simple-acid-base-disorders), [StatPearls: Alkalosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545269/)
- Metabolic alkalosis: expected PaCO2 = 40 + 0.6 × (HCO3 − 24). [StatPearls: Alkalosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545269/)
- **High anion gap causes in your notes (MUDPILES):** Methanol (formic acid), Uremia, Diabetic ketoacidosis, Propylene glycol, Iron tablets or Isoniazid, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol (oxalic acid), Salicylates (late) (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 67). **Other source:** StatPearls also lists the newer **GOLDMARK** mnemonic: Glycols, Oxoproline, L-lactate, D-lactate, Methanol, Aspirin, Renal failure, Ketoacidosis. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/)
- **Normal anion gap (hyperchloremic) causes in your notes (HARD-ASS):** Hyperalimentation, Addison disease, Renal tubular acidosis, Diarrhea, Acetazolamide, Spironolactone, Saline infusion (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 67). StatPearls agrees that diarrhea and renal tubular acidosis are the main ones. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/)
- **Salicylate overdose in your notes:** **respiratory alkalosis comes first**, because salicylate directly stimulates the medullary breathing center; a few hours later a high anion gap metabolic acidosis follows, as salicylate uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, boosts lipolysis and blocks the citric acid cycle, so ketoacids and lactate build up. The pH can land anywhere, even normal (El-Husseiny Nephrology, pp. 67-68).
- **Metabolic alkalosis in your notes** (El-Husseiny Nephrology, pp. 69, 73-74). Check **volume status and urine chloride**:

  | Cause | Blood pressure / volume | Urine Cl | Fixed by saline? |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Vomiting or NG suction (lost HCl) | Low (volume loss) | **Low** | Yes (saline-responsive) |
  | Thiazide or loop diuretics | Low | High while taking them, low after stopping | Yes |
  | Mineralocorticoid excess (Conn, Cushing) | **High** | High | **No** (saline-unresponsive) |

  **Contraction alkalosis:** volume loss turns on renin, angiotensin II and aldosterone. Angiotensin II increases proximal HCO3 reabsorption, and aldosterone increases distal H and K secretion, so the alkalosis and **hypokalemia** persist until you give **NaCl and K** (p. 69).
- Delta ratio = (AG − 12) / (24 − HCO3): 1-2 pure high-AG acidosis; below 1 adds a normal-AG acidosis; above 2 adds a metabolic alkalosis or a preexisting high HCO3. Beyond core Step 1, so learn it last. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/)

## Compare and contrast
| Primary disorder | pH | Primary change | Compensation | Expected size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | Low | HCO3 down | PaCO2 down (hyperventilation) | PaCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3 + 8 ± 2 |
| Metabolic alkalosis | High | HCO3 up | PaCO2 up (hypoventilation) | PaCO2 = 40 + 0.6 × (HCO3 − 24) |
| Respiratory acidosis | Low | PaCO2 up | HCO3 up | +1 per 10 mmHg acute, +4 chronic |
| Respiratory alkalosis | High | PaCO2 down | HCO3 down | −1 to 2 per 10 mmHg acute, −4 to 5 chronic |

## Clinical vignette
A 19-year-old with type 1 diabetes has 2 days of vomiting and deep, rapid breathing. Labs: pH 7.26, PaCO2 23 mmHg, HCO3 10 mEq/L, Na 140, Cl 100. What is the acid-base disorder, and is it mixed?

**Answer:** high anion gap metabolic acidosis from diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), appropriately compensated, with no second disorder.
**Why:** AG = 140 − (100 + 10) = 30. Winters: 1.5 × 10 + 8 = 23, range 21-25, and measured PaCO2 is 23. Delta ratio = (30 − 12) / (24 − 10) = 1.3, inside 1-2. Ketoacidosis is a high anion gap cause, and the delta ratio rule is from [StatPearls: AG and non-AG acidosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/); the Winters formula is from [MSD Manual: Acid-Base Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/endocrine-and-metabolic-disorders/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/acid-base-disorders).
**Trap:** stopping at "metabolic acidosis" without checking compensation and the gap.

## Common traps
- Compensation never overshoots. A pH on the "wrong" side of 7.40 for the disorder you picked means you picked the wrong primary disorder, or it is mixed.
- A normal pH does not mean a normal gas. Salicylate toxicity classically pairs respiratory alkalosis with a high-AG metabolic acidosis.
- Always compute the anion gap in metabolic acidosis, even when the cause seems obvious.
- Use Winters only for metabolic acidosis; each disorder has its own compensation rule.
- Metabolic alkalosis with **high blood pressure** and high urine chloride = mineralocorticoid excess; saline won't fix it (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 74).
- Vomiting causes **hypokalemia** mainly through aldosterone at the kidney, not from potassium lost in vomit (p. 69).

## Practice questions
1. pH 7.50, PaCO2 46 mmHg, HCO3 34 mEq/L after a week of vomiting. What is the primary disorder, and is compensation appropriate?
   - **Answer:** metabolic alkalosis, appropriately compensated.
   - **Why:** vomiting loses stomach acid, so HCO3 rises; the lungs hypoventilate to hold CO2. Expected PaCO2 = 40 + 0.6 × (34 − 24) = 46, which matches. [StatPearls: Alkalosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545269/)
   - **Trap:** the high PaCO2 is compensation, not a second respiratory acidosis.
2. Na 138, Cl 114, HCO3 14 mEq/L in a patient with profuse diarrhea. What is the anion gap, and which category of acidosis is this?
   - **Answer:** normal anion gap metabolic acidosis.
   - **Why:** stool is rich in bicarbonate, and chloride rises to replace it. AG = 138 − (114 + 14) = 10, inside 8-12. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/)
   - **Trap:** a low HCO3 alone does not tell you the category; always calculate the gap.
3. A patient with long-standing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has a stable PaCO2 of 60 mmHg. What HCO3 do you expect?
   - **Answer:** about 32 mEq/L.
   - **Why:** chronic respiratory acidosis; kidneys add about 4 mEq/L HCO3 per 10 mmHg rise. 24 + 2 × 4 = 32. [StatPearls: Respiratory Acidosis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482430/)
   - **Trap:** the acute rule (+1 per 10) predicts only 26 and makes a normal COPD gas look like an added metabolic alkalosis.
4. pH 7.47, PaCO2 20 mmHg, HCO3 14 mEq/L, Na 140, Cl 102 after an aspirin overdose. What is the acid-base disorder?
   - **Answer:** mixed high anion gap metabolic acidosis plus primary respiratory alkalosis.
   - **Why:** salicylates stimulate the respiratory center and also add acid. AG = 140 − (102 + 14) = 24. Winters predicts PaCO2 = 1.5 × 14 + 8 = 29 ± 2, but measured is 20, so there is extra hyperventilation. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448090/)
   - **Trap:** a near-normal pH tempts you to call it simple; the PaCO2 below the Winters range gives away the second disorder.
