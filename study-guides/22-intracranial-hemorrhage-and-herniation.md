# Bleeding in and around the brain, and brain herniation

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (pp. 164-170) and the Osmosis High-Yield Neurology notes (subarachnoid hemorrhage). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where a newer or different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Name the layer and the vessel. **Epidural** = artery (middle meningeal) between skull and dura, lens-shaped, lucid interval. **Subdural** = bridging veins between dura and arachnoid, crescent-shaped, often an older adult. **Subarachnoid** = between arachnoid and pia, usually a burst berry aneurysm, "worst headache of my life". **Intraparenchymal** = inside the brain, usually from high blood pressure in the putamen.

## Core mechanism
1. **Epidural hematoma:** blood between the skull and dura, "classically due to damage to the middle meningeal artery by a temporal bone fracture". Arterial pressure makes it expand fast, but there can be a symptom-free **lucid interval** first. Epidural hematomas are less common than subdurals. [MSD Manual: Traumatic Brain Injury](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi) Your notes add that the middle meningeal artery is a branch of the maxillary artery, and that noncontrast CT shows a bright (hyperdense) biconvex mass that does not cross suture lines. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 164)
2. **Subdural hematoma:** from laceration of cortical veins or "avulsion of bridging veins between the cortex and dural sinuses". Chronic subdurals affect older patients, especially those on antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs, with brain atrophy, or with alcohol use disorder. They can build up over several weeks, and the patient may not remember the head injury. [MSD Manual: Traumatic Brain Injury](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi) Why atrophy matters: a shrunken brain widens the gap to the skull, so the bridging veins are stretched and tear easily, even after minor trauma in older adults or people with alcohol use disorder. Shaken babies and other acceleration-deceleration injuries also tear them. Venous bleeding is slow, so symptoms build gradually. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 165)
3. **Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH):** bleeding between the arachnoid and pia. Head trauma is the most common cause overall; spontaneous SAH is from a ruptured saccular (berry) aneurysm in about 85%. [MSD Manual: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/subarachnoid-hemorrhage)
4. **Intraparenchymal (intracerebral) hemorrhage:** usually from **hypertension**. "Chronic arterial hypertension leads to formation of microaneurysms (Charcot-Bouchard aneurysms) in small perforating arteries", and the most common site is the **putamen**. Lobar bleeds in older adults usually come from **amyloid angiopathy**. [MSD Manual: Intracerebral Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/intracerebral-hemorrhage) Your notes give the order of sites as putamen (lenticulostriate vessels), then thalamus, pons and cerebellum, with hyaline arteriosclerosis of tiny arterioles as the first step. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 167)
5. **Herniation:** a growing mass pushes brain tissue past a rigid edge (tentorium, falx or foramen magnum), squeezing nerves, arteries and the brainstem. [MSD Manual: Brain Herniation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/brain-herniation)

## Compare and contrast: the four bleeds
| Feature | Epidural | Subdural | Subarachnoid | Intraparenchymal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Layer | Skull and dura | Dura and arachnoid | Arachnoid and pia | Brain tissue |
| Vessel | Middle meningeal artery | Bridging veins | Berry aneurysm (spontaneous); trauma overall | Small perforating arteries (Charcot-Bouchard) |
| Typical patient | Young, temporal bone fracture (standard teaching for "young") | Older adult, anticoagulants, atrophy, alcohol | Sudden "worst headache" | Long-standing hypertension; amyloid if lobar and older |
| Onset | Minutes to hours, maybe a lucid interval | Acute, or gradual over weeks (chronic) | Peaks within seconds | Sudden headache, often during activity; deficits sudden and progressive |
| CT | **Lens-shaped**, does **not** cross suture lines | **Crescent-shaped**, **crosses** suture lines but not the midline falx (midline point is standard teaching); chronic ones are darker (hypodense) | Blood in the subarachnoid space, lining the sulci | Bright blood in the brain, most often the putamen |

## Subarachnoid hemorrhage and berry aneurysms
- **Presentation:** headache "usually severe, peaking within seconds", often "the worst headache in their life". The neck is usually not stiff at first; meningismus and vomiting develop within 24 hours. [MSD Manual: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/subarachnoid-hemorrhage)
- **Diagnosis:** noncontrast CT within 6 hours has very high sensitivity. If CT is negative, lumbar puncture shows many red cells and **xanthochromia** (yellow fluid) with raised pressure; skip the LP if raised intracranial pressure is suspected. CT angiography has largely replaced catheter angiography for finding the aneurysm. [MSD Manual: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/subarachnoid-hemorrhage)
- **Vasospasm (your notes):** more than half of survivors develop vasospasm around the ruptured aneurysm, seen as new confusion or focal deficits **4-12 days** after the bleed; **nimodipine**, a calcium channel blocker, is given to prevent it. CT shows blood in the basal cisterns but cannot show vasospasm. Hydrocephalus (communicating or obstructive) is another complication. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 166) Osmosis gives the delayed-ischemia window as 4-11 days and also lists rebleeding, hyponatremia, seizures and neurogenic pulmonary edema. (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Subarachnoid hemorrhage")
  - **Recent change (how nimodipine helps):** nimodipine is now understood to prevent **delayed cerebral ischemia** rather than the vessel narrowing itself. StatPearls calls oral nimodipine "the only treatment with consistent, high-quality evidence for decreasing DCI", and says early studies showed it reduced DCI and improved outcomes "without affecting vasospasm". [StatPearls: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (Ziu E, Khan Suheb MZ, Mesfin FB; updated 1 Jun 2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441958/) For exam questions, "nimodipine after SAH" is still the answer; just know it works on the brain injury, not the spasm.
  - **Other source:** MSD puts peak vasospasm risk between **72 hours and 10 days** and says rebleeding is most common within 7 days. [MSD Manual: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/subarachnoid-hemorrhage) The windows overlap; for the exam, new deficits several days after SAH = vasospasm.
- **Outcome:** about 35% die after a first aneurysmal SAH, and another 15% die within weeks from a rebleed. Aneurysms are coiled during angiography or clipped surgically. [MSD Manual: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/subarachnoid-hemorrhage)
- **Berry aneurysms** occur in 3-5% of people in the US, usually at bifurcations around the circle of Willis. Risk factors: **autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease**, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, pseudoxanthoma elasticum, a first-degree relative with an aneurysm, hypertension and smoking. Septic emboli can cause **mycotic** aneurysms. An unruptured aneurysm can press on cranial nerves 3-6, giving double vision or eye pain. [MSD Manual: Brain Aneurysms](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/brain-aneurysms)
- **Berry aneurysms in your notes:** the **anterior communicating artery** (where it joins the anterior cerebral artery) is the most common site. They go with Ehlers-Danlos, **Marfan** and ADPKD; other risks are older age, hypertension, smoking and African-American ancestry. A growing **ACom** aneurysm can press on the optic chiasm (bitemporal hemianopia), and its rupture causes anterior cerebral artery ischemia (opposite leg weakness). A **PCom** aneurysm presses on the same-side third nerve: dilated "blown" pupil, ptosis, eye down and out. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 168)
- **Charcot-Bouchard vs berry (your notes):** Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms come with hypertension, sit in small vessels to the basal ganglia and internal capsule, and their rupture causes sudden **focal deficits**. Berry aneurysms sit on the circle of Willis, and their rupture causes sudden **headache and reduced consciousness** more than focal signs. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 169)

## Herniation syndromes
| Type | What moves where | Classic findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transtentorial (uncal) | Medial temporal lobe (uncus) under the tentorium | **Same-side dilated, fixed pupil** and third nerve palsy first; opposite-side weakness (same-side peduncle); **Kernohan notch**: the other peduncle is pressed, giving same-side weakness; posterior cerebral artery compression gives opposite-side homonymous hemianopia |
| Central | Both temporal lobes through the tentorial notch | Midposition fixed pupils, decerebrate posturing, then loss of all brainstem reflexes |
| Subfalcine (cingulate) | Cingulate gyrus under the falx | Traps the **anterior cerebral artery**, so the opposite **leg** becomes weak |
| Tonsillar | Cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum | Squeezes the brainstem; hydrocephalus, nystagmus, then sudden **respiratory and cardiac arrest** |
| Upward | Posterior fossa mass pushes up | Nausea, vomiting, occipital headache, ataxia, then drowsiness |

Source: [MSD Manual: Brain Herniation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/brain-herniation)

**Herniation in your notes** (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 190-191): uncal herniation is usually from a mass on the **same** side (tumor, epidural or subdural hematoma, bleed). It presses the same-side **CN III** (down-and-out eye, dilated pupil, ptosis), the same-side **posterior cerebral artery** (opposite homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing), and can push the opposite peduncle against the tentorium (weakness on the **same** side as the herniation). Stretching and tearing of basilar artery branches causes **Duret hemorrhages** in the midbrain and pons, usually fatal; your notes link these to downward central herniation as well. Tonsils through the foramen magnum compress the medulla and can stop the heart and breathing.

- **Cushing reflex** (triad): high systolic blood pressure with a wide pulse pressure, irregular breathing and a **slow** heart rate, from high intracranial pressure. [MSD Manual: Brain Herniation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/brain-herniation)
- **Lowering pressure fast:** raise the head of the bed to 30° and keep it midline; briefly hyperventilate, because low CO2 constricts brain vessels; give mannitol. [MSD Manual: Brain Herniation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/brain-herniation)

## High-yield facts
- **Diffuse axonal injury:** "rotational deceleration causes shear-type forces" that tear axons. Loss of consciousness lasts over 6 hours. Early CT can be normal; MRI shows small bleeds in deep white matter, the corpus callosum and the brainstem. Your notes add that it is the biggest cause of disability after traumatic brain injury, that the damage clusters at the **gray-white junction** (where tissue density changes most), that blocked axonal transport makes axonal swellings ("bulbs"), that most patients are in a coma (Glasgow Coma Score under 8), and that MRI is more sensitive than CT. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 170) [MSD Manual: Traumatic Brain Injury](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi), [MSD Manual: Common Types of TBI table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/common-types-of-traumatic-brain-injury)
- **Abusive head trauma (shaken baby):** violent shaking causes rapid acceleration and deceleration; diffuse axonal injury is typically the underlying injury, and infants should be checked for **retinal hemorrhages**, which may indicate abuse. [MSD Manual: Traumatic Brain Injury](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi) Your notes list shaken baby among the causes of subdural hematoma from torn bridging veins. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 165)
- **Basilar skull fracture:** fluid (CSF) leaking from the nose or ear, blood behind the eardrum, **Battle sign** (bruising behind the ear) and **raccoon eyes**. [MSD Manual: Common Types of TBI table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/common-types-of-traumatic-brain-injury)
- **Neonatal intraventricular hemorrhage (your notes):** bleeding into the ventricles of premature, low-birth-weight babies; the lower the birth weight, the higher the risk. It starts in the **germinal matrix**, a fragile, very vascular layer under the ependyma, because those capillaries are fragile and blood-flow autoregulation is immature. Signs: altered consciousness, bulging fontanelle, low blood pressure, seizures, coma. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 169)
- Intracerebral hemorrhage: cocaine and other sympathomimetics, too much anticoagulation and arteriovenous malformations are other causes; reverse anticoagulants when possible. About half of large bleeds are fatal within days. [MSD Manual: Intracerebral Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/intracerebral-hemorrhage)

## Clinical vignette
A 19-year-old is hit on the side of the head with a baseball bat. He is knocked out briefly, then wakes and talks normally for an hour. Then he becomes drowsy, his right pupil is dilated and fixed, and his left arm and leg are weak. What is the bleed, and where is the brain going?

**Answer:** a right epidural hematoma from the middle meningeal artery, causing right transtentorial (uncal) herniation.
**Why:** a temporal bone fracture tears the middle meningeal artery. Arterial blood expands quickly after a lucid interval. The uncus compresses the right third nerve (right pupil) and the right cerebral peduncle, which carries fibers for the left side of the body. [MSD Manual: Traumatic Brain Injury](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi), [MSD Manual: Brain Herniation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/brain-herniation)
**Trap:** calling it subdural. Subdurals are venous, crescent-shaped and cross sutures; the lucid interval and fast decline point to an artery.

## Common traps
- Lens = epidural = artery; crescent = subdural = veins. Subdurals cross suture lines; epidurals don't.
- An older adult on a blood thinner who is confused for weeks after a minor fall (or no remembered fall) = chronic subdural.
- "Worst headache of my life" with a normal CT: do the lumbar puncture and look for xanthochromia.
- New confusion or weakness several days after SAH (4-12 days in your notes; 72 hours to 10 days in MSD) = vasospasm; nimodipine is for prevention.
- Hypertensive bleed = putamen (deep); lobar bleed in an older adult = amyloid angiopathy.
- Kernohan notch: the weakness is on the **same** side as the mass, a false localizing sign (standard teaching for the term).
- Cushing reflex gives bradycardia, not tachycardia.

## Practice questions
1. A 45-year-old with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease has a sudden, severe headache that peaked within seconds, then vomits. CT 2 hours later shows blood lining the sulci. What is the most likely source, and what drug prevents the next big complication?
   - **Answer:** a ruptured berry (saccular) aneurysm; nimodipine to prevent the delayed ischemia from vasospasm (see the Recent change note above).
   - **Why:** ADPKD is a risk factor for brain aneurysms, and about 85% of spontaneous SAH is from an aneurysm. Vasospasm appears 4-12 days after the bleed in your notes (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 166); MSD gives 72 hours to 10 days. [MSD Manual: Brain Aneurysms](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/brain-aneurysms), [MSD Manual: Subarachnoid Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/subarachnoid-hemorrhage)
   - **Trap:** giving nimodipine to lower blood pressure; its purpose here is to prevent vasospasm.
2. An 80-year-old on apixaban has 3 weeks of worsening confusion and headache. CT shows a dark (hypodense) crescent over the left hemisphere that crosses suture lines. Which vessels bled?
   - **Answer:** bridging veins (chronic subdural hematoma).
   - **Why:** chronic subdurals come from bridging veins in older patients on anticoagulants or with atrophy, build up over weeks, and look hypodense on CT. [MSD Manual: Traumatic Brain Injury](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi/traumatic-brain-injury-tbi), [MSD Manual: Common Types of TBI table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/common-types-of-traumatic-brain-injury)
   - **Trap:** middle meningeal artery; that gives a lens-shaped epidural.
3. A 66-year-old with untreated hypertension for 20 years suddenly develops weakness on one side while shoveling snow. CT shows a deep bleed. What is the most likely location, and what lesion ruptured?
   - **Answer:** the putamen; a Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysm of a small perforating artery.
   - **Why:** chronic hypertension forms these microaneurysms, and the putamen is the most common site. [MSD Manual: Intracerebral Hemorrhage](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/intracerebral-hemorrhage)
   - **Trap:** amyloid angiopathy, which gives lobar (surface) bleeds in older adults.
4. A patient with a large right frontal tumor develops weakness of the left leg but not the arm. Which herniation, and which artery is pinched?
   - **Answer:** subfalcine (cingulate) herniation trapping the anterior cerebral artery.
   - **Why:** the cingulate gyrus slides under the falx and traps the anterior cerebral artery, which supplies the leg area of the cortex, giving opposite-leg weakness. [MSD Manual: Brain Herniation](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/brain-herniation)
   - **Trap:** uncal herniation, which hits the third nerve first (dilated pupil).
