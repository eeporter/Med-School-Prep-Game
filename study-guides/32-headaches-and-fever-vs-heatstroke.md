## In one line
Brain tissue feels no pain; headaches come from the meninges, vessels and trigeminal nerve. **Migraine** is one-sided, throbbing, with nausea and light sensitivity; **cluster** is brief, stabbing pain behind one eye with tearing and a runny nose, mostly in men; **tension** is a steady, band-like pain on both sides with no nausea.

## Core mechanism
1. "Brain tissues itself is not sensitive to pain"; pain comes from meninges, cranial nerves and extracranial structures (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 178).
2. **Migraine:** trigeminal afferents to the meninges release **substance P** and **calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)**, causing neurogenic inflammation with vasodilation and leaky vessels (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 243). MSD: the trigeminovascular system triggers "neuropeptide release [eg, CGRP], which causes painful inflammation in cranial vessels and the dura mater" ([MSD Manual: Migraine](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/migraine)).
3. **Triptans** are serotonin 5-HT1B/1D agonists: they block that neuropeptide release, constrict vessels and block brainstem pain pathways (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 243; MSD).
4. **Cluster:** the **posterior hypothalamus** switches on, which drives the trigeminal nerve and the parasympathetic pterygopalatine ganglion (tearing, runny nose); swelling in the cavernous sinus can hit the sympathetic fibers on the carotid, giving a **Horner syndrome** (Osmosis Neurology, p. 134).

## Compare: the three primary headaches
| Feature | Cluster | Migraine | Tension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sex | Male > female | Female > male | Female > male |
| Site | One side, behind one eye | One side | Both sides, band-like |
| Duration | 15 min to 3 h, repeated | 4-72 h | Over 30 min (typically 4-6 h) |
| Timing | Often during sleep, same time each day | Variable | With stress |
| Features | Excruciating periorbital pain, **tearing and runny nose**, may cause **Horner**; patient is **restless** | **Pulsating**, nausea, light and sound sensitivity; may have an **aura** | Steady pain; no nausea; does not limit daily activity |
| Acute treatment | **100% oxygen**, sumatriptan | NSAIDs, triptans, dihydroergotamine | Analgesics, NSAIDs, acetaminophen |
| Prevention | **Verapamil** | Lifestyle, beta blockers, amitriptyline, topiramate, valproate, botulinum toxin | Amitriptyline, behavioral therapy |

Source for the table: El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 178. MSD agrees that cluster "affects primarily men, typically beginning at age 20 to 40", that attacks "frequently occur at the same time each day, often awakening patients from sleep", and that patients are "agitated, restlessly pacing the floor" while migraine patients lie still ([MSD Manual: Cluster Headache](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/cluster-headache)).

- **Migraine mnemonic POUND** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 178): **P**ulsatile, **O**ne-day duration, **U**nilateral, **N**ausea, **D**isabling.
- **Aura** (Osmosis Neurology, p. 137): visual is most common; positive features such as a **scintillating scotoma** (zigzag lines spreading outward), or negative ones such as field loss; sensory tingling that marches from the hand up the arm to the face. Each aura lasts 5-60 minutes, and the headache follows within 60 minutes.
- **Migraine triggers** (Osmosis Neurology, p. 136): stress, hormone changes, irregular eating or sleep, weather, light, alcohol, tobacco, odors.
- **Cluster** criteria (Osmosis Neurology, p. 135): at least 5 attacks, 1-8 a day, up to 3 hours, with restlessness and at least one same-side autonomic sign. Episodic clusters last weeks, then remit for up to a year.

**Other source (tension headache and light sensitivity):** El-Husseiny says tension headache has "no photophobia or phonophobia" (p. 178). Osmosis lists photophobia or phonophobia under symptoms and allows light or sound sensitivity in its criteria (pp. 138-139). MSD says tension headaches are "not made worse by physical activity, light, sounds, or smells" ([MSD Manual: Tension-Type Headache](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/tension-type-headache)). For exams, marked light **and** sound sensitivity with nausea points to migraine.

## Drugs: what to know
- **Triptan toxicity** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 244): coronary vasospasm, so **contraindicated with coronary artery disease or Prinzmetal angina**; mild paresthesia; serotonin syndrome with other serotonergic drugs. MSD adds uncontrolled hypertension as a contraindication.
- **Medication overuse:** overusing triptans or analgesics causes rebound (medication overuse) headache (Osmosis Neurology, p. 137; [MSD Manual: Migraine](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/migraine)).
- **Oral contraceptives** "have been associated with stroke in women who have migraine with aura" (MSD).
- **Recent change (newer migraine drugs):** your notes stop at triptans, ergots and the older preventives. MSD now lists the **gepants** (oral CGRP receptor antagonists, ubrogepant and rimegepant) and **lasmiditan** (a 5-HT1F agonist) for acute attacks when triptans are contraindicated, because they have no known serious cardiovascular effects; it lists **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab) for prevention "if other medications are ineffective" ([MSD Manual: Migraine](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/migraine)). In 2024 the American Headache Society went further: it "encourages clinicians to use CGRP-targeting therapies as a first-line treatment" for prevention, "regardless of whether patients have experienced failure" of older preventives ([Practical Neurology report on Charles et al., Headache 2024](https://practicalneurology.com/news/american-headache-society-issues-new-migraine-prevention-guidance/2470423)). Galcanezumab is also used for cluster prevention when other drugs fail ([MSD Manual: Cluster Headache](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/cluster-headache)).

## Secondary headaches: think of these first (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 179)
- **Subarachnoid hemorrhage:** "the worst headache in my life" (see [22-intracranial-hemorrhage-and-herniation.md](22-intracranial-hemorrhage-and-herniation.md)).
- **Meningitis** (see [31-meninges-csf-and-lumbar-puncture.md](31-meninges-csf-and-lumbar-puncture.md)), **hydrocephalus**, **tumor**, **arteritis**.
- Osmosis: image when the headache comes with unusual neurologic signs, fever and a stiff neck, or is new in someone with HIV or cancer (p. 133).

## Fever vs heatstroke (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 179)
| | Fever | Heatstroke |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Cytokines released in inflammation or infection (they raise the hypothalamic set point, standard teaching) | The body cannot get rid of heat (exertion, hot weather) |
| Temperature | Usually below 40 °C | Usually above 40 °C |
| Complications | Febrile seizure (benign, usually self-limited) | Confusion and other CNS dysfunction, organ damage, ARDS, **rhabdomyolysis** |
| Treatment | Acetaminophen or ibuprofen for comfort; antibiotics if needed | **Rapid external cooling**, fluids and electrolytes |

- MSD: heatstroke means "temperature > 40° C and altered mental status"; CNS dysfunction is "the hallmark." Classic heatstroke builds over 2-3 days of heat exposure (typically in older or chronically ill people, standard teaching); exertional heatstroke strikes healthy active people suddenly. Sweating may be present or absent ([MSD Manual: Heatstroke](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/heat-illness/heatstroke)).
- **Why no antipyretics in heatstroke:** the set point is normal, so drugs that lower it do nothing (standard teaching). MSD: "Antipyretics are not indicated as they do not treat the underlying problem."
- Cooling: cold-water immersion is "the treatment of choice"; aim for below 39 °C within 30 minutes, then stop to avoid overshooting (MSD).

## Febrile seizures
- Children **6 months to 5 years** with fever above 38 °C that is not from a CNS infection (Osmosis Neurology, p. 98; [MSD Manual: Febrile Seizures](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/neurologic-disorders-in-children/febrile-seizures)).
- **Simple:** generalized, under 15 minutes, no repeat within 24 hours. **Complex:** any one of focal, 15 minutes or longer, or repeat within 24 hours (Osmosis Neurology, p. 98; MSD).
- About 20-35% recur; the risk of later epilepsy after simple febrile seizures is about 2-6%. Antipyretics have not been shown to prevent recurrence, and daily antiseizure drugs are not routinely given (MSD). Your notes agree that antipyretics are "for comfort" and "do not prevent future febrile seizures" (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 179).

## Clinical vignette
A 34-year-old man wakes at 2 a.m. most nights for 3 weeks with stabbing pain behind his right eye lasting 45 minutes. His right eye waters, his right nostril runs, and his right eyelid droops with a small pupil. He paces the room during attacks. Best acute treatment?

**Answer:** 100% oxygen by face mask (or a parenteral triptan).
**Why:** brief, nightly, one-sided orbital pain with same-side tearing, rhinorrhea and Horner signs in a restless man is cluster headache. Acute treatment is oxygen and sumatriptan; verapamil prevents attacks (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 178; [MSD Manual: Cluster Headache](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/cluster-headache)).
**Trap:** choosing propranolol or amitriptyline, which are migraine or tension preventives.

## Common traps
- Restless patient = cluster; lies still in a dark room = migraine.
- Triptans are out with coronary disease or Prinzmetal angina.
- Verapamil prevents cluster; it is not the acute drug.
- Heatstroke: cool the patient; antipyretics do not work.
- Antipyretics do not prevent febrile seizures.
- A first "worst headache of my life" is subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise.

## Practice questions
1. A 27-year-old woman has throbbing left-sided headaches lasting about a day, with nausea and light sensitivity, preceded by zigzag lines in her vision. She has coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal angina), and her doctor says sumatriptan is unsafe for her. What effect of sumatriptan is the reason?
   - **Answer:** it constricts blood vessels, including coronary arteries (5-HT1B/1D agonist).
   - **Why:** triptans constrict vessels (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 243) and can cause coronary vasospasm and are contraindicated in Prinzmetal angina (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 244; [MSD Manual: Migraine](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/migraine)).
   - **Trap:** NSAIDs are fine; a gepant or lasmiditan is an option when triptans are contraindicated (MSD).
2. A 45-year-old office worker has a pressing, band-like headache on both sides at the end of stressful days. No nausea; activity does not worsen it. Diagnosis and first treatment?
   - **Answer:** tension headache; an over-the-counter analgesic such as acetaminophen or an NSAID.
   - **Why:** bilateral, steady, no nausea, not limiting (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 178; [MSD Manual: Tension-Type Headache](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/headache/tension-type-headache)).
   - **Trap:** migraine is one-sided and pulsating with nausea.
3. A 19-year-old football player collapses at August practice. Rectal temperature is 41.5 °C and he is confused. Best treatment?
   - **Answer:** immediate cold-water immersion, aiming for below 39 °C within 30 minutes (a drop of 41.5 − 39 = 2.5 °C).
   - **Why:** exertional heatstroke (above 40 °C with CNS dysfunction) needs rapid external cooling ([MSD Manual: Heatstroke](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/heat-illness/heatstroke); El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 179).
   - **Trap:** acetaminophen; the hypothalamic set point is normal, so antipyretics do not help.
4. An 18-month-old with an ear infection and a temperature of 39.4 °C has a 3-minute generalized seizure and is back to normal an hour later. What do you tell the parents about fever medicine?
   - **Answer:** it can make the child comfortable but has not been shown to prevent another febrile seizure.
   - **Why:** this is a simple febrile seizure; recurrence is about 20-35% and antipyretics do not prevent it ([MSD Manual: Febrile Seizures](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/neurologic-disorders-in-children/febrile-seizures); El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 179).
   - **Trap:** starting a daily antiseizure drug, which is not routinely recommended.
