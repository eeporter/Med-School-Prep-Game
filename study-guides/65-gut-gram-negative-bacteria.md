## In one line
**Sort a gram-negative rod from stool or urine with three questions** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 125-126, 131-132):
1. **Does it ferment lactose?** On **MacConkey**, lactose fermenters are **pink**; on **EMB**, *E. coli* has a **green metallic sheen**. Fermenters: *Citrobacter*, *Klebsiella*, *E. coli*, *Enterobacter*, *Serratia* ("MacConKEE'S").
2. **Non-fermenter: oxidase?** Oxidase **positive** = ***Pseudomonas***. Oxidase **negative** = *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Proteus*, *Yersinia*.
3. **Oxidase-negative: H₂S?** **H₂S positive** = ***Salmonella*, *Proteus***. **H₂S negative** = ***Shigella*, *Yersinia***.
- **Comma-shaped, oxidase-positive** rods are a separate group: ***Campylobacter*** (grows at 42°C), ***Vibrio*** (grows in alkaline media), ***H. pylori*** (urease) (pp. 118-122).

## Core mechanism: four ways gut bacteria cause diarrhea
1. **Toxin raises cAMP (watery, no white cells in stool):**
   - **cholera toxin** and **ETEC LT** ADP-ribosylate **Gs**, locking adenylate cyclase on. cAMP rises, chloride and water pour out (pp. 123, 145);
   - **ETEC ST** turns on **guanylate cyclase** instead, raising **cGMP** (p. 145).
2. **Toxin kills cells by stopping protein synthesis:** **Shiga toxin** (*S. dysenteriae*) and **Shiga-like toxin** (EHEC) cut the **60S** ribosome. EHEC toxin also injures kidney endothelium (HUS) (pp. 133, 142, 145). Compare diphtheria toxin and *Pseudomonas* exotoxin A, which block **EF-2** instead (p. 142).
3. **Invasion (bloody, white cells in stool):** *Shigella*, EIEC and *Campylobacter* invade the colon lining (pp. 118, 133, 146).
4. **Invasion then spread through the body:** *Salmonella typhi* is carried inside **macrophages** to the liver, spleen and marrow (p. 138).
- **Infectious dose matters** (pp. 118, 122, 133): *Shigella* as few as **10-200** organisms (resists acid); *Campylobacter* about **500**; *Salmonella* and *V. cholerae* need about **10⁷** because stomach acid kills them. **Low stomach acid** (achlorhydria, long-term PPIs) lowers the dose needed (p. 122).

## Campylobacter jejuni (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 118-119)
- Comma- or S-shaped, oxidase positive, **grows at 42°C** ("likes the hot campfire") (p. 118).
- **Source:** mainly **undercooked poultry**; also unpasteurized milk and farm or pet animals (p. 118).
- **Disease** (pp. 118-119): fever, cramps, then **bloody diarrhea** with white cells; pain can **mimic appendicitis**.
- **Complications** (p. 119): **Guillain-Barré syndrome** (the most common infectious trigger; your notes say 10-30% of cases) and **reactive arthritis**.
- **How common:** your notes call it the most common cause of acute gastroenteritis in industrialized countries and say US cases exceed *Salmonella* plus *Shigella* (p. 118).
  - **Other source (CDC):** the CDC calls *Campylobacter* "the most common bacterial cause of foodborne illness" (about **1.87 million** US illnesses a year). It is not the most common cause of gastroenteritis overall: **norovirus** causes the most foodborne illness (58% in the CDC's 2011 estimates). (The older 2011 table in guide 62 ranked nontyphoidal *Salmonella* just above *Campylobacter*; the CDC's newer *Campylobacter* estimate is higher.)
  - CDC: Guillain-Barré follows only **0.2-1.7 per 1,000** infections, and *Campylobacter* causes **5-41%** of Guillain-Barré cases.
- **Treatment (CDC):** most recover without antibiotics. For severe illness, **azithromycin** or a fluoroquinolone; fluoroquinolone resistance is common in the US. Pharm HY lists azithromycin for *Campylobacter* (PDF 126).

## Helicobacter pylori (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 120-121; guide 18)
- Curved rod, **catalase, oxidase and urease positive**. Urease makes ammonia, which protects it from acid (p. 120).
- **Virulence:** **VacA** toxin; the ***cag* pathogenicity island** drives **IL-8**, which recruits neutrophils (p. 120).
- **Disease:** chronic gastritis, **duodenal ulcers**, **gastric adenocarcinoma** and **MALT lymphoma** (p. 120).
- **Tests:** **urea breath test** or **stool antigen**. Stop antibiotics and PPIs for **2-4 weeks** first, or the test can be falsely negative (p. 121).
  - **Other source (MSD, citing ACG 2024):** wait **at least 4 weeks after antibiotics** and **1 week after a PPI**; H2 blockers do not affect the test.
- **Recent change (treatment):** your notes give clarithromycin triple therapy first, with bismuth quadruple therapy if macrolide resistance is a concern (p. 121). MSD, citing the **ACG 2024** guideline:
  - **bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days** (PPI + bismuth + metronidazole + tetracycline) is the best first choice when clarithromycin resistance is over 15% or unknown;
  - clarithromycin triple therapy only where resistance is **known** to be under 15% and there was no earlier macrolide use;
  - **vonoprazan + amoxicillin** is an alternative;
  - confirm cure at least **4 weeks** after treatment.

## Vibrio (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 122-124)
- **Lab:** comma-shaped, oxidase positive, grows on **alkaline** media; "shooting star" motility (p. 122).
- **Cholera toxin** (p. 123): B subunit binds; A subunit **ADP-ribosylates Gs** → adenylate cyclase stays on → cAMP → chloride and water secretion.
- **Disease** (p. 123):
  - after 6-48 hours, sudden **massive watery diarrhea**: "**rice-water**" stools with mucus flakes, often with vomiting;
  - **no white or red cells** in stool, because it does not invade;
  - death from **hypovolemic shock**, acidosis and kidney failure (acute tubular necrosis);
  - **prompt oral rehydration** is the key treatment.
- **Correction (habitat):** your notes list cholera's habitat as "human colon" (p. 122). Cholera is an infection of the **small intestine**, as your notes say on p. 123. MSD: "an acute infection of the small bowel"; humans are the only known natural host.
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - **blood group O** and low stomach acid increase risk;
  - mild cases: **ORS**; severe cases: isotonic IV fluids;
  - **doxycycline** (one 300 mg dose for age 12 and older, including pregnancy) is first-line; it usually stops diarrhea within 48 hours;
  - oral cholera vaccines exist (the live vaccine is available in the US for travelers aged 2-64).
- ***V. vulnificus*** (p. 124):
  - lives in warm, salty coastal water; caught from **raw oysters** or wounds exposed to seawater;
  - people with **liver disease** or **iron overload (hemochromatosis)** are at highest risk;
  - can cause rapidly spreading **necrotizing fasciitis** with hemorrhagic bullae and septic shock.

## Pseudomonas aeruginosa (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 126-130)
- **Lab:** **oxidase-positive, non-lactose-fermenting**, motile, **aerobic**; blue-green pigments (**pyocyanin**, **pyoverdine**); **grape-like odor** (p. 126).
- **Virulence** (p. 127):
  - endotoxin;
  - **exotoxin A**: **ADP-ribosylates EF-2**, like diphtheria toxin, and drives the high death rate of *Pseudomonas* sepsis;
  - elastase, phospholipase C, pyocyanin (reactive oxygen species);
  - **mucoid capsule / biofilm**, which keeps it in **CF** lungs.
- **Who gets it** (pp. 127-129):
  - **catheters** (UTI), **ventilators**, **burns**, **neutropenia** (bloodstream infection);
  - **ecthyma gangrenosum** in neutropenic patients: necrotic skin lesions from bacteria invading vessel walls;
  - **CF**: chronic pneumonia;
  - **osteomyelitis and endocarditis** in people who inject drugs (*S. aureus* is still more common);
  - water exposures: swimmer's ear, **hot tub folliculitis** (guide 46).
- **Correction (otitis externa):** your notes call malignant otitis externa "swimmer's ear" (p. 128). These are two different diseases (MSD):
  - **acute otitis externa (swimmer's ear)**: a common outer-ear infection in healthy people;
  - **malignant (necrotizing) otitis externa**: a severe infection in people with **diabetes**, with **severe ear pain**, **granulation tissue** in the canal and spread to the **skull base**, often with cranial nerve palsy (p. 128; MSD).
- **Drugs** ("CAMPFIRE", p. 130): **C**arbapenems, **A**minoglycosides, **M**onobactams, **P**olymyxins, **F**luoroquinolones, th**IR**d- and fourth-generation cephalosporins (**ceftazidime, cefepime**), **E**xtended-spectrum penicillins (**piperacillin**, ticarcillin). Pharm HY: piperacillin and ticarcillin are effective against *Pseudomonas* (PDF 120).
  - **Other source (MSD):** one active antipseudomonal beta-lactam (for example **cefepime**) or a fluoroquinolone is usual; **two drugs** at first in critically ill or septic patients until results return.

## Shigella (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 132-134)
- **Lab:** **non-motile**, non-lactose-fermenting, **no H₂S** (p. 132).
- **Species:** *S. sonnei* is most common in the US; *S. flexneri* in developing countries; *S. dysenteriae* is most severe (pp. 132-133).
- **Spread:** humans only; **fecal-oral**, person to person; tiny infectious dose (p. 132-133).
- **Mechanism** (p. 133):
  - enters through **M cells** of Peyer patches, escapes the vacuole, and spreads **cell to cell**. It does **not** enter the blood;
  - **invasion** and the neutrophil response cause most of the disease. Shiga toxin adds damage, but toxin-negative strains still cause disease.
- **Disease** (p. 134): watery diarrhea, then fever, cramps and **bloody, mucoid stools** with **tenesmus** (bacillary dysentery).
- **After-effect:** **reactive arthritis** (arthritis, conjunctivitis, urethritis) can follow *Shigella* (NBME TC p. 386; MSD, especially with HLA-B27).
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - treat children, older adults, immunocompromised patients and moderate to severe disease: **azithromycin**, **ceftriaxone** or a fluoroquinolone (adults). Extensively drug-resistant strains are rising in the US;
  - **do not give loperamide** or other antidiarrheals; they can prolong illness;
  - *S. dysenteriae* type 1 can cause **HUS** in children; seizures can occur in children.

## Yersinia (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 134-135)
- ***Y. pestis* (plague):**
  - **bipolar "safety pin"** staining (p. 134);
  - **flea** from infected rodents → painful swollen node (**bubo**) in the groin, armpit or neck → sepsis (pp. 134-135);
  - **pneumonic plague** is **highly contagious** person to person (p. 135).
  - **Other source (MSD):** over 90% of US cases are in the rural **Southwest** (rodents include prairie dogs). Treatment: **streptomycin or gentamicin** (fluoroquinolones or doxycycline are alternatives). Contacts of pneumonic plague get **7 days** of doxycycline or ciprofloxacin. Pharm HY lists streptomycin as the drug of choice for plague (PDF 127).
- ***Y. enterocolitica*** (p. 135):
  - grows in the cold; motile at 25°C, not at 37°C;
  - from **puppy feces**, **unpasteurized milk** and **pork**;
  - bloody diarrhea in young children; in older children and young adults, **pseudoappendicitis** (right lower quadrant pain from **mesenteric adenitis** or terminal ileitis); reactive arthritis in adults.
  - **Other source (MSD):** mostly from raw or undercooked pork; also erythema nodosum. In **liver disease or iron overload**, bloodstream infection can be fatal in up to 50%.

## Salmonella (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 136-139)
- **Lab:** **motile** ("salmon swim"), non-lactose-fermenting, **H₂S positive**; ***S. typhi*** has the **Vi capsule** (p. 136).
- **Reservoir:** humans and animals, but ***S. typhi* lives only in humans** (p. 136).
- **Risk:** low stomach acid; **sickle cell disease** (pp. 136, 139).
- **Nontyphoidal gastroenteritis** (*S. Enteritidis*, *S. Typhimurium*) (p. 137):
  - from **poultry, eggs** and other animal foods;
  - fever, cramps, diarrhea (can be bloody); invades gut lining but does **not** live in macrophages;
  - treatment is **fluids and electrolytes**.
- **Typhoid (enteric) fever** (*S. typhi*, *S. paratyphi*) (pp. 137-138):
  - mostly in **travelers** to Asia, Africa and Latin America;
  - enters through **M cells**, survives **inside macrophages**, spreads to **liver, spleen and marrow** → bacteremia;
  - fever, abdominal pain, diarrhea **or constipation**, then **rose spots** and **hepatosplenomegaly**;
  - **Peyer patch** ulceration → **bleeding and perforation**;
  - **gallbladder carriage** → **chronic carriers** who spread it. Osmosis links chronic *S. typhi* infection with **gallbladder cancer** (p. 245).
  - **Other source (MSD):** fever rises in steps; **relative bradycardia**; rose spots on the chest and abdomen in **week 2**; perforation in **week 3**. Blood, stool and urine cultures; **bone marrow culture** is very sensitive. About 3% of untreated patients become carriers.
- **Bacteremia** (5-10% of infections, mainly immunocompromised) can seed bone, joints, lungs and meninges (p. 139).
- **Sickle cell disease** (p. 139): infarcted spleen and dead bone. Your notes: ***Salmonella* is the most common cause of osteomyelitis in sickle cell disease**, then *E. coli*, then *S. aureus*.
- **Vaccines** (p. 139): **oral live attenuated** (Ty21a) and **IM Vi polysaccharide**. MSD: oral not under age 6; IM not under age 2.
- **Treatment of typhoid:** Pharm HY calls ciprofloxacin the "first line drug for typhoid fever" (PDF 128).
  - **Recent change (CDC, Apr 2024):** "**Do not use fluoroquinolones for empiric treatment.**" "Ceftriaxone resistance is now common among travelers returning from **Iraq and Pakistan**". For travelers from those countries, or with no travel: **uncomplicated** illness → **azithromycin**; **complicated** illness → a **carbapenem**. Ceftriaxone and azithromycin "remain appropriate" for travelers from most other countries.
  - **Other source (MSD):** resistance is common and rising, especially in endemic areas. Preferred drugs are **ceftriaxone for 14 days** or a fluoroquinolone for 7-10 days; **azithromycin** for fluoroquinolone-resistant strains. **Carriers**: ciprofloxacin for 4-6 weeks; removing the gallbladder does not guarantee cure.

## Proteus (El-Husseiny Micro p. 140)
- **Swarming** motility on blood agar, **urease positive**, H₂S positive (p. 140).
- *P. mirabilis* causes about 90% of infections, mostly hospital UTIs (p. 140).
- **Urease** splits urea into ammonia, raising urine pH and forming **struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) stones** (NBME TC p. 299).

## Escherichia coli (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 141-146)
- **Lab:** lactose fermenter (pink on MacConkey, **green metallic sheen** on EMB) (p. 141).
- **UTI** (pp. 143-144):
  - causes about **80%** of community UTIs; **fimbriae** let it stick to the bladder lining;
  - women more than men (shorter urethra);
  - the urinary tract is the most common source of *E. coli* bacteremia.
- **Neonatal meningitis** (p. 144): second after group B strep; the **K1 capsule** is the main virulence factor. MSD agrees: usually K1 strains, especially in preterm infants.
- **Diarrhea types** (pp. 145-146):

| Type | Mechanism | Picture | Hook |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ETEC** | **LT** (↑cAMP, like cholera) and **ST** (↑cGMP) | Watery; **travelers' diarrhea**; infants in developing areas | **T** = travelers |
| **EHEC** (O157:H7) | **Shiga-like toxin** (60S); does **not** invade | **Bloody** diarrhea; **HUS**; undercooked **hamburger**; does **not ferment sorbitol** | **H** = hamburger, hemorrhagic |
| **EPEC** | Sticks tightly to the gut lining | Watery diarrhea in **infants** | **P** = pediatric |
| **EIEC** | **Invades** like *Shigella*, no Shiga toxin | Dysentery | **I** = invasive |

- **EHEC toxin:** your notes say the toxin is carried by a **temperate bacteriophage** (lysogeny) (p. 142).
- **Other source (MSD):** HUS develops in **2-7%** of EHEC cases. "**Antibiotics are not indicated** for enterohemorrhagic *E. coli* infections." HUS details are in guide 38.

## Gardnerella vaginalis: bacterial vaginosis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 149)
- Gram-variable rod. **Loss of lactobacilli** raises vaginal pH, and *Gardnerella* and other anaerobes overgrow (often after menses or antibiotics) (p. 149).
- **Signs** (p. 149):
  - thin **gray-white discharge**, **fishy odor**, stronger with **KOH (whiff test)**;
  - **clue cells** (vaginal cells covered with bacteria) on wet mount;
  - **no inflammation** (unlike trichomoniasis or *Candida*).
- **Treatment:** **metronidazole** or **clindamycin** (p. 149).
- **Other source (MSD, July 2026):** **Amsel criteria** need 3 of 4: thin gray discharge, **pH over 4.5**, positive whiff test, clue cells over 20% of epithelial cells. Regimens: **oral metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days**, metronidazole vaginal gel or clindamycin vaginal cream. BV in pregnancy is linked to preterm birth and membrane rupture.
- **Recent change (partner treatment):** your notes say BV is "associated with sexual activity, but not sexually transmitted" (p. 149).
  - A 2025 randomized trial (Vodstrcil et al., *NEJM* 2025, summarized by the California Department of Public Health): treating **male partners** with oral metronidazole plus clindamycin cream on the penis cut **recurrence from 63% to 35%**.
  - MSD reports that **ACOG** now says partner therapy "should be considered for male sexual partners of adult patients with recurrent, symptomatic bacterial vaginosis".

## Bacteroides fragilis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 151)
- The main **anaerobe** of the colon. Its **capsule** resists phagocytosis; its modified LPS is a weak endotoxin (p. 151).
- Causes **intra-abdominal abscesses** and peritonitis after **bowel perforation** (for example a ruptured appendix), surgery or trauma, usually **mixed** with *E. coli*, enterococci and streptococci (p. 151).
- Pharm HY pairs *Bacteroides* with **metronidazole** (PDF 123).

## Clinical vignette
A 58-year-old cook at a catering company feels well. Over 3 years, several customers who ate her food developed prolonged fever, abdominal pain and rose spots; blood cultures grew *Salmonella* Typhi. Stool cultures from her repeatedly grow the same strain. An ultrasound shows gallstones.

- **Diagnosis:** **chronic typhoid carrier** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 138).
- **Why she is well:** *S. typhi* lives in the **gallbladder** and is shed in stool without illness (p. 138). MSD: most US carriers are **older women with gallbladder disease**.
- **Why only humans:** *S. typhi* has **no animal reservoir**, so carriers keep it going (p. 136).
- **Treatment:** ciprofloxacin for 4-6 weeks cures 80-90%; removing the gallbladder does not guarantee cure (MSD).
- **Long-term risk:** chronic *S. typhi* infection is a risk factor for **gallbladder cancer** (Osmosis p. 245).

## Common traps
- **Non-lactose fermenters:** oxidase + = *Pseudomonas*; H₂S + = *Salmonella*, *Proteus*; H₂S − = *Shigella*, *Yersinia* (pp. 126, 132).
- ***Shigella* is non-motile; *Salmonella* is motile** (pp. 132, 136).
- **Cholera and ETEC: no white cells in stool.** *Shigella*, EIEC, *Campylobacter*: white cells and blood (pp. 118, 123, 134).
- **Cholera infects the small intestine**, not the colon (p. 123; MSD).
- **Cholera toxin → Gs** (on); **pertussis toxin → Gi** (off) (guide 64). Both raise cAMP.
- **Malignant otitis externa ≠ swimmer's ear** (MSD).
- **EHEC: no antibiotics** (MSD); no antidiarrheals in shigellosis (MSD).
- **Typhoid treatment:** no fluoroquinolone as empiric therapy. After travel to **Pakistan or Iraq**, ceftriaxone may fail: azithromycin if uncomplicated, a carbapenem if complicated (CDC 2024). Ceftriaxone or azithromycin after most other travel.
- ***Campylobacter*** is the most common **bacterial** cause of foodborne illness in the US (CDC); norovirus is the most common overall.
- **Sickle cell + osteomyelitis → *Salmonella*** (p. 139).
- **Raw oysters + cirrhosis or hemochromatosis → *V. vulnificus*** (p. 124).
- **BV: consider treating the male partner for recurrent BV** (MSD; ACOG).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 30-year-old woman has fever, cramps and diarrhea 2 days after eating undercooked eggs at a brunch. Stool culture grows a motile gram-negative rod that forms colorless colonies on MacConkey agar, is oxidase-negative and produces hydrogen sulfide. Which organism is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **nontyphoidal *Salmonella* (for example *S.* Enteritidis).**
   - **Why:** a motile, non-lactose-fermenting, oxidase-negative, H₂S-positive rod is *Salmonella*; eggs and poultry are classic sources (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 126, 136-137).
   - **Trap:** ***Shigella***. It is also a non-lactose fermenter but is non-motile and makes no H₂S (p. 132).

2. A 24-year-old man has fever, cramping and bloody diarrhea 3 days after a barbecue. Stool culture grows curved, oxidase-positive gram-negative rods that grow best at 42°C. Which food is the most likely source?
   - **Answer:** **undercooked chicken.**
   - **Why:** this is *Campylobacter jejuni*, which grows at 42°C and comes mostly from poultry (El-Husseiny Micro p. 118; CDC).
   - **Trap:** **raw oysters**. Those carry *Vibrio* species, which grow on alkaline media (pp. 122, 124).

3. A 40-year-old aid worker returns from a flooded region with sudden, profuse, painless watery diarrhea that looks like rice water. Stool microscopy shows no white or red blood cells. Which molecular action of the responsible toxin causes the fluid loss?
   - **Answer:** **ADP-ribosylation of the stimulatory G protein (Gs), keeping adenylate cyclase active and raising cAMP.**
   - **Why:** cholera toxin locks Gs on; cAMP drives chloride and water secretion in the small intestine (El-Husseiny Micro p. 123).
   - **Trap:** **ADP-ribosylation of the inhibitory G protein (Gi)**. That is pertussis toxin (guide 64).

4. A 9-year-old boy in a refugee camp has had severe rice-water diarrhea for 12 hours. He is thirsty and has dry mucous membranes but is alert, with a normal blood pressure, and he can drink. Which treatment is most important?
   - **Answer:** **oral rehydration solution.**
   - **Why:** death from cholera comes from fluid loss, so prompt rehydration matters most (El-Husseiny Micro p. 123). MSD treats mild cases with ORS and severe or hypotensive cases with IV fluids. Doxycycline shortens illness but does not replace fluids.
   - **Trap:** **loperamide to slow the diarrhea**. Antimotility drugs do not stop toxin-driven secretion (standard teaching).

5. A 55-year-old man with hereditary hemochromatosis and cirrhosis eats raw oysters on a summer beach trip. Within 24 hours he has fever, hypotension and rapidly spreading hemorrhagic blisters on his legs. Which organism is most likely?
   - **Answer:** ***Vibrio vulnificus*.**
   - **Why:** *V. vulnificus* lives in warm coastal water and concentrates in oysters. Liver disease and iron overload greatly raise the risk of sepsis and necrotizing skin infection (El-Husseiny Micro p. 124).
   - **Trap:** ***Vibrio cholerae***, which causes watery diarrhea, not bullous skin infection (p. 123).

6. A 72-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes has 3 weeks of severe right ear pain and discharge. The ear canal shows granulation tissue; the eardrum is intact. He now has weakness of the right side of his face. Which complication is most likely developing?
   - **Answer:** **osteomyelitis of the skull base.**
   - **Why:** this is malignant (necrotizing) otitis externa from *P. aeruginosa*, which spreads to the skull base and cranial nerves (El-Husseiny Micro p. 128; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **acute otitis media with eardrum perforation**. The eardrum is intact; this is an outer-ear infection that has invaded bone.

7. A 45-year-old woman receiving chemotherapy for leukemia has an absolute neutrophil count of 200/mm³. She develops fever and a painless skin lesion on her thigh that turns into a black, necrotic ulcer with a red rim. Blood cultures grow oxidase-positive gram-negative rods with a blue-green pigment. Which antibiotic is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **cefepime** (an antipseudomonal cephalosporin).
   - **Why:** ecthyma gangrenosum in a neutropenic patient means *P. aeruginosa* bacteremia (El-Husseiny Micro p. 128). Cefepime is a CAMPFIRE drug (p. 130); MSD uses an antipseudomonal beta-lactam such as cefepime, with a second drug at first if she is septic.
   - **Trap:** **ceftriaxone**. Ceftriaxone is a third-generation cephalosporin that does not cover *Pseudomonas*; ceftazidime is the third-generation exception (standard teaching).

8. Twelve children at a daycare center develop fever, cramps and small, frequent stools with blood and mucus. A non-motile, non-lactose-fermenting gram-negative rod that does not produce H₂S is isolated. A strain that lacks the toxin gene causes the same illness. Which mechanism is most responsible for the disease?
   - **Answer:** **invasion of the colonic lining through M cells, with cell-to-cell spread and a strong neutrophil response.**
   - **Why:** for *Shigella*, mucosal invasion is the essential step; toxin-negative strains still cause dysentery (El-Husseiny Micro p. 133).
   - **Trap:** **Shiga toxin inhibiting the 60S ribosome**. It adds damage but is not required (p. 133).

9. A 16-year-old girl has fever, diarrhea and right lower quadrant pain. At surgery the appendix is normal, but nearby mesenteric lymph nodes are enlarged. She often drinks raw milk from a farm. Stool culture held at 4°C grows a gram-negative rod that is motile at 25°C. Which organism is most likely?
   - **Answer:** ***Yersinia enterocolitica*.**
   - **Why:** it grows in the cold, is motile at 25°C but not 37°C, and causes "pseudoappendicitis" from mesenteric adenitis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 135; MSD).
   - **Trap:** ***Campylobacter jejuni***, which can also mimic appendicitis but grows at 42°C (pp. 118-119).

10. A 28-year-old man from rural New Mexico has 2 days of fever and a very painful, swollen lymph node in his groin after his dog brought home a dead prairie dog. Aspirate shows gram-negative rods that stain darker at both ends. Which antibiotic is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **gentamicin (or streptomycin).**
   - **Why:** this is bubonic plague from rodent fleas; bipolar "safety pin" staining is typical (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 134-135). MSD gives streptomycin or gentamicin as first-line; Pharm HY lists streptomycin (PDF 127).
   - **Trap:** **amoxicillin-clavulanate**, which treats *Pasteurella* from animal bites (guide 64).

11. A 26-year-old woman returns from 3 weeks in Pakistan with 9 days of fever that rose a little each day, abdominal pain and constipation. Her pulse is 78/min with a temperature of 39.6°C. Faint pink spots are on her abdomen, and her spleen is palpable. She is alert, her abdomen is soft without guarding, her stool has no blood, and she is drinking well. Blood cultures grow a motile, H₂S-positive gram-negative rod. Which treatment is most appropriate while susceptibility results are pending?
   - **Answer:** **oral azithromycin.**
   - **Why:** this is uncomplicated typhoid fever with relative bradycardia and rose spots (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 137-138; MSD). The CDC (2024): ceftriaxone resistance is now common in travelers returning from Pakistan and Iraq, so uncomplicated illness after travel there is treated empirically with azithromycin (a carbapenem if complicated).
   - **Trap:** **IV ceftriaxone**, which is still right after travel to most other countries but may fail after Pakistan (CDC). **Oral ciprofloxacin** is also wrong: Pharm HY calls it first-line (PDF 128), but the CDC says not to use fluoroquinolones empirically.

12. A 9-year-old boy with sickle cell disease has fever and a painful, swollen left tibia for 5 days. MRI shows osteomyelitis. Which organism is most likely?
   - **Answer:** ***Salmonella*.**
   - **Why:** infarcted bone and a nonfunctioning spleen predispose to *Salmonella* osteomyelitis; your notes rank it first in sickle cell disease, ahead of *E. coli* and *S. aureus* (El-Husseiny Micro p. 139).
   - **Trap:** ***Pseudomonas aeruginosa***, the classic cause of osteomyelitis after a nail puncture through the foot (MSD: deep foot puncture wounds).

13. A 6-day-old boy born at 33 weeks has fever, poor feeding and a bulging fontanelle. CSF shows gram-negative rods that ferment lactose on MacConkey agar. Which bacterial structure is the major virulence factor for this infection?
   - **Answer:** **the K1 capsule.**
   - **Why:** *E. coli* is the second most common cause of newborn meningitis, and K1-capsulated strains cause most cases (El-Husseiny Micro p. 144; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **Shiga-like toxin**, which belongs to EHEC and causes bloody diarrhea and HUS (p. 145).

14. A 35-year-old man has 4 days of watery diarrhea and cramps that began 2 days after arriving in Mexico. He has no fever or blood in the stool. The organism makes a heat-stable toxin. Which second messenger does that toxin increase in intestinal cells?
   - **Answer:** **cGMP, by activating guanylate cyclase.**
   - **Why:** ETEC causes travelers' diarrhea. Its ST raises cGMP, and its LT raises cAMP like cholera toxin (El-Husseiny Micro p. 145).
   - **Trap:** **cAMP**. That is the heat-labile toxin (LT), not the heat-stable one (p. 145).

15. A 4-year-old girl has bloody diarrhea and cramps 3 days after eating an undercooked hamburger. She has no fever. Stool culture grows an *E. coli* strain that does not ferment sorbitol. Her blood count and kidney function are normal so far. Which management is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **supportive care with fluids and close monitoring of blood counts and kidney function, without antibiotics.**
   - **Why:** this is EHEC O157:H7 (El-Husseiny Micro p. 145). MSD: "Antibiotics are not indicated", and HUS develops in 2-7% of cases, so she needs watching (guide 38).
   - **Trap:** **oral ciprofloxacin to shorten the illness**. Antibiotics are not indicated for EHEC (MSD).

16. A 32-year-old woman in a monogamous relationship has her fourth episode of bacterial vaginosis this year. Each episode cleared with oral metronidazole. Her male partner has no symptoms. Which additional step is most appropriate to lower her chance of recurrence?
   - **Answer:** **treat her male partner at the same time** (oral metronidazole plus clindamycin cream applied to the penis).
   - **Why:** a 2025 randomized trial cut recurrence from 63% to 35% with male partner treatment (Vodstrcil, *NEJM* 2025, via CDPH 2025). MSD reports that ACOG now says partner therapy should be considered for recurrent, symptomatic BV.
   - **Trap:** **no partner treatment, because BV is not sexually transmitted**. That is your notes' older view (p. 149); newer evidence supports partner treatment for recurrence.
