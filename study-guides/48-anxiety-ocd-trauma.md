# Anxiety, OCD-related and trauma-related disorders

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the chapters on anxiety disorders (pp. 215-262), obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (pp. 263-294) and trauma- and stressor-related disorders (pp. 295-321). Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers. Drug facts come from Pharmacology High Yield (PDF pp. 43-45, 59, 61) and are checked against the MSD Manual and the FDA-approved flumazenil label. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied; check exact wording in the book. Where another source disagrees or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **GAD** = generalized anxiety disorder; **OCD** = obsessive-compulsive disorder; **BDD** = body dysmorphic disorder; **PTSD** = posttraumatic stress disorder; **ASD** = acute stress disorder (in this guide only; not autism); **RAD** = reactive attachment disorder; **DSED** = disinhibited social engagement disorder; **CBT** = cognitive-behavioral therapy; **ERP** = exposure and response prevention; **SSRI** = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor; **SNRI** = serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor; **TCA** = tricyclic antidepressant; **BZD** = benzodiazepine; **GABA** = gamma-aminobutyric acid.

## In one line
For anxiety disorders, ask **what the fear is about** (a panic attack, being trapped, being judged, one object, many everyday worries, being apart from a parent) and **how long** it has lasted (most need **6 months**). For trauma disorders, ask **how long since the event**: symptoms lasting under 3 days do not meet criteria for acute stress disorder (p. 316), **3 days to 1 month is acute stress disorder**, and **more than 1 month is PTSD**.

| **Agoraphobia** | Fear of being somewhere that is **hard to escape or where help may not come** if panic-like or embarrassing symptoms start, in **2 or more of 5** situations: public transport, open spaces, enclosed places, lines or crowds, being outside home alone | 6 months | The places are avoided, need a companion, or are endured with intense fear. | **Separation anxiety disorder** | Excessive fear of **separation from attachment figures**, shown by **3 or more of 8** signs (distress at separation, worry that harm will come to the parent, refusing school or sleep away from home, nightmares, stomachaches or headaches at separation) | **4 weeks in children and adolescents; typically 6 months in adults** | Can be diagnosed in adults | p. 217 |
| **Selective mutism** | **Consistently not speaking in specific social settings** (such as school) while speaking normally elsewhere | **1 month**, not counting the first month of school | Not explained by not knowing the language or by a communication disorder | p. 222 |

**Two physiology facts the exam likes:**
- **Blood-injection-injury phobia causes fainting.** Most phobias cause a sympathetic surge, but this type often gives a **vasovagal response**: a brief rise in heart rate and blood pressure, then a **drop in heart rate and blood pressure**, with fainting or near-fainting (p. 226).
- **Panic attacks are a specifier, not a disorder.** A panic attack can occur in any anxiety disorder and in other disorders such as depression or PTSD; it cannot be coded on its own (p. 242). Panic **disorder** needs **unexpected** attacks (out of the blue, including waking from sleep) (p. 236).

## OCD and related disorders (pp. 265-286)
- **Obsessions** are repeated, **intrusive, unwanted** thoughts, urges or images that cause anxiety; the person tries to ignore, suppress or neutralize them (p. 265).
- **Compulsions** are repeated behaviors (washing, checking, ordering) or **mental acts** (counting, praying) that the person feels driven to do in response to an obsession or rigid rules, to reduce anxiety. They are not realistically connected to what they are meant to prevent, or are clearly excessive (p. 265).
- **How much:** time-consuming (DSM's example: **more than 1 hour a day**) or causing distress or impairment (p. 265).
- **Insight specifier (p. 266):** good or fair, poor, or **absent insight/delusional beliefs**. A person who is fully convinced the obsessive belief is true still has OCD (not a psychotic disorder). Only a few (4% or less) have absent insight (p. 266).
- **Tic-related specifier (p. 266):** up to **30%** of people with OCD have a lifetime tic disorder, most often **males with childhood onset** (p. 266).
- **Epidemiology (p. 267):** 12-month prevalence **1.2%**; mean onset **19.5 years**, a quarter by age 14; **males start earlier** (nearly a quarter before age 10); women slightly more affected in adulthood, males more in childhood.
- **Sudden childhood onset after strep throat:** DSM describes **PANDAS** (pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders associated with streptococcal infections): **sudden** obsessions, compulsions and/or tics after **group A streptococcal** infection, **without** the chorea, carditis or arthritis of rheumatic fever. Because other infections can do the same, the broader term **PANS** (pediatric acute-onset neuropsychiatric syndrome) is also used (p. 292).

| **Body dysmorphic disorder** | Preoccupation with **flaws in appearance that others cannot see or think are slight**, plus repetitive behaviors (mirror checking, grooming, skin picking, reassurance seeking) or mental comparing | Not better explained by weight concerns in an eating disorder. **Muscle dysmorphia** specifier: belief that the body is too small or not muscular enough (almost all males). On average insight is poor; a third or more are delusional | pp. 271-272 |

## Trauma- and stressor-related disorders (pp. 295-321)
**PTSD (adults, adolescents and children older than 6; pp. 301-303)**
- **A. The event:** actual or threatened **death, serious injury or sexual violence**, by experiencing it, **witnessing it in person**, **learning it happened to a close family member or friend** (if a death, it must have been violent or accidental), or repeated exposure to gruesome details at work (for example first responders). Watching it on TV or other media does not count unless it is part of the job (p. 301).
- **B. Intrusion (1 or more of 5):** intrusive memories, nightmares, **flashbacks**, distress at reminders, physical reactions to reminders (pp. 301-302).
- **C. Avoidance (1 or more of 2):** of memories, thoughts or feelings, or of external reminders (p. 302).
- **D. Negative thoughts and mood (2 or more of 7):** for example, not remembering key parts of the event, exaggerated negative beliefs, blaming oneself or others, persistent fear, guilt or shame, loss of interest, feeling detached, inability to feel positive emotions (p. 302).
- **E. Arousal (2 or more of 6):** irritability or angry outbursts, reckless behavior, **hypervigilance**, **exaggerated startle**, poor concentration, poor sleep (p. 302).
- **F. Duration: more than 1 month** (p. 302).
- **Specifiers (p. 303):** **with dissociative symptoms** (depersonalization or derealization) and **with delayed expression** (full criteria not met until at least **6 months** after the event).
- **Children 6 and younger (pp. 303-304):** a separate, simpler set. Intrusive memories may show up as **play reenactment**, and avoidance and negative mood are combined into one group needing only 1 symptom.
- **Who gets it:** US lifetime prevalence about 6-8% (DSM-5 criteria) (p. 308). It is **more common in women** (lifetime 8-11% vs 4.1-5.4% in men) (p. 311).

**Acute stress disorder (pp. 313-315)**
- Same kinds of event as PTSD.
- **9 or more of 14 symptoms** from any of 5 groups (intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance, arousal) (pp. 314-315).
- **Lasts 3 days to 1 month** after the trauma (p. 315). It cannot be diagnosed before day 3 (p. 316).
- It may progress to PTSD after 1 month, or it may resolve (p. 316).

**Adjustment disorders (pp. 319-320)**
- Emotional or behavioral symptoms that begin **within 3 months** of an identifiable stressor (any stressor, such as divorce, job loss or illness) (p. 319).
- Distress out of proportion to the stressor, or real impairment, but **not enough for another mental disorder**, and not normal grief or prolonged grief disorder (p. 319).
- Symptoms end **within 6 months after the stressor (or its consequences) ends** (p. 319). The "persistent (chronic)" specifier is for symptoms that last 6 months or longer because the stressor itself continues (p. 320).
- Subtypes by main feature: depressed mood, anxiety, mixed, disturbance of conduct, and so on (p. 319).

## Treatment (MSD and Pharmacology High Yield)
| Disorder | Psychotherapy | Drugs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GAD** | MSD: CBT "has been shown to be the most effective psychotherapeutic modality" | MSD: **SSRIs (for example escitalopram) and SNRIs (for example venlafaxine) are the preferred medications**; improvement usually takes **3-6 weeks**. Buspirone may work, but only after the dose is raised to the upper range. BZDs reduce anxiety right away but long-term use can cause physical dependence ([MSD: GAD, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/anxiety-and-trauma-and-stressor-related-disorders/generalized-anxiety-disorder)) |
| **Panic disorder** | CBT, including **interoceptive exposure** (deliberately bringing on the body sensations so the patient gets used to them) | MSD: SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs and MAOIs "are all effective"; BZDs are more likely to cause physical dependence and cause sleepiness, unsteadiness and memory problems ([MSD: Panic Attacks and Panic Disorder, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/anxiety-and-trauma-and-stressor-related-disorders/panic-attacks-and-panic-disorder)). Her notes: SSRI plus a BZD (PDF p. 59) |
| **Social anxiety disorder** | CBT with **exposure** | SSRIs, SNRIs and BZDs are effective; **beta-blockers** reduce the fast heart rate, trembling and sweating ([MSD: Social Anxiety Disorder, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/anxiety-and-trauma-and-stressor-related-disorders/social-anxiety-disorder)). Her notes: **propranolol is the drug of choice for performance anxiety** (PDF p. 59) |
| **Specific phobia** | MSD: **exposure therapy** is "the most extensively studied and most effective psychotherapy" | Only short-term help: a BZD (for example lorazepam) or a beta-blocker (for example propranolol) ([MSD: Specific Phobias, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/anxiety-and-trauma-and-stressor-related-disorders/specific-phobias)) |
| **OCD** | **Exposure and response prevention** (gradual exposure to the feared trigger while not doing the ritual), often with cognitive therapy | MSD: an **SSRI is usually preferred over clomipramine**; patients often need **higher doses than for depression**; augment with an antipsychotic such as aripiprazole or risperidone, especially with tics ([MSD: OCD, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/obsessive-compulsive-and-related-disorders/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd)). Her notes: clomipramine is the TCA for OCD (PDF p. 61) |
| **BDD** | CBT | SSRIs or clomipramine. Cosmetic, skin or surgical treatment is "usually unsuccessful and may intensify their preoccupation". | **Trichotillomania** | CBT, usually **habit reversal training** (first-line) | SSRIs or clomipramine; N-acetylcysteine or memantine worked in adults, but N-acetylcysteine was no better than placebo in a small study in children ([MSD: Trichotillomania, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/obsessive-compulsive-and-related-disorders/trichotillomania)) |
| **PTSD** | **Trauma-focused CBT** has the strongest evidence; also prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR (eye movement desensitization and reprocessing) | **SSRIs** improve symptoms; **prazosin** "appears helpful in reducing nightmares"; sedating drugs such as BZDs "should be used sparingly, if at all" ([MSD: PTSD, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/anxiety-and-stressor-related-disorders/posttraumatic-stress-disorder-ptsd)). Prazosin blocks **α1** receptors (standard teaching) |

### Anxiety drugs: how they work (Pharmacology High Yield)
- **Benzodiazepines** bind the BZD site on the **GABA-A receptor** (between the α and γ subunits) and **increase the frequency** of chloride channel opening, which hyperpolarizes the neuron. **Barbiturates** instead **increase the duration** of opening, and at high doses open the channel directly (PDF p. 43). Memory aid: "**fre**nzodiazepines increase **fre**quency, **bar**biturates increase **dur**ation" (standard teaching).
- **BZDs in liver failure:** most are metabolized by the liver; **lorazepam, oxazepam and temazepam** can still be used (PDF p. 44).
- **Z-drugs** (zolpidem, zopiclone, zaleplon) act at the BZD receptor but are not BZDs; used for trouble falling asleep (PDF p. 44).
- **Flumazenil** is a competitive BZD antagonist and the specific antidote, given IV (PDF p. 45). MSD: in BZD-dependent patients with a seizure disorder there is about a 1 in 5 chance it will **precipitate a seizure**, and it is not recommended in mixed overdoses with antiepileptic drugs or TCAs ([MSD: Benzodiazepines and Barbiturates, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/special-subjects/illicit-drugs-and-intoxicants/benzodiazepines-and-barbiturates)).
- **BZD withdrawal** can cause tremor, fast heart rate, confusion and **seizures**, including status epilepticus; treat by giving enough BZD to control symptoms, then tapering (MSD, same page).
- **Buspirone** is a **5-HT1A agonist**: **not sedating, not habit-forming**, but **slow to start working** (PDF p. 59). It is a partial agonist (standard teaching).

**Other source (flumazenil route):** her notes say flumazenil is not given orally "due to excessive absorption in GIT" (PDF p. 45). The FDA-approved label says only that it is "recommended for intravenous use only" and that clearance "occurs primarily by hepatic metabolism", with 99% of the drug metabolized ([Drugs.com: Flumazenil Injection prescribing information](https://www.drugs.com/pro/flumazenil-injection.html)). For the exam, remember: **IV only, short-acting, can cause seizures.**

## Clinical vignette
A 35-year-old paramedic was first on the scene of a highway crash 4 months ago in which a child died in front of him. Since then he has had intrusive images of the scene and nightmares several nights a week, and he drives a long detour to avoid that highway. He blames himself for not saving the child, feels cut off from his wife, and no longer enjoys fishing. He is irritable, constantly on guard, and jumps at sirens. He drinks no more than before, and his examination is normal. Witnessing a death in person, plus intrusion, avoidance, negative thoughts and mood, and arousal for **more than 1 month**, is **PTSD** (pp. 301-302). If the same picture had lasted only 3 days to 1 month, it would be acute stress disorder (p. 315). First-line treatment is **trauma-focused CBT**; **SSRIs** also help, and BZDs should be used sparingly, if at all (MSD).

## Common traps
- **Panic attack ≠ panic disorder.** Attacks can occur in any anxiety disorder; panic disorder needs **unexpected** attacks plus **1 month** of worry or avoidance.
- **Agoraphobia = fear of being trapped without help** (crowds, buses, open spaces, leaving home alone). **Social anxiety = fear of being judged.** Same avoidance, different reason.
- **GAD = 6 months, many worries, 3 of 6 symptoms** (1 in children). Its onset is the latest of the anxiety disorders (mean 35).
- **Separation anxiety needs only 4 weeks in children** (6 months in adults).
- **Blood-injection-injury phobia → vasovagal fainting** (bradycardia and hypotension), unlike the sympathetic surge of other phobias.
- **OCD with absent insight is still OCD**, not schizophrenia. Specify the insight level.
- **Sudden OCD or tics after strep throat in a child → PANDAS/PANS.** No chorea, carditis or arthritis.
- **BDD: do not send for cosmetic surgery**; treat with an SSRI and CBT.
- **Trauma timing: 3 days to 1 month = acute stress disorder; more than 1 month = PTSD.** Delayed expression = full criteria only after 6 months.
- **Adjustment disorder: within 3 months of any stressor; gone within 6 months after the stressor ends.** The stressor does not have to be life-threatening.
- **RAD = withdrawn child; DSED = child who goes off with strangers.** Both come from neglect; neither is autism or ADHD.
- **BZDs increase frequency, barbiturates increase duration** of chloride channel opening.
- **Performance anxiety → propranolol before the event**; generalized social anxiety → SSRI and CBT.
- **PTSD nightmares → prazosin (α1 blocker).**

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 31-year-old woman has had four sudden episodes in 3 months of racing heart, sweating, chest tightness, dizziness and fear that she is dying. Each starts without a trigger and peaks within minutes. For the past 2 months she has worried constantly about having another attack and now avoids driving alone. ECG, troponin and thyroid function tests are normal, and urine drug screen is negative. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **panic disorder**.
   - **Why:** recurrent unexpected panic attacks (4 or more symptoms peaking within minutes) followed by at least 1 month of worry or behavior change, with medical causes and substances excluded (pp. 235-236).
   - **Trap:** **generalized anxiety disorder**, in which worry is ongoing and about many everyday matters, not sudden attacks (p. 250).

2. A 44-year-old man has avoided shopping malls, crowded trains and movie theaters for 9 months. He fears that if he suddenly felt dizzy or needed a bathroom there, he could not get out or get help. He goes to the grocery store only if his wife comes with him. He is not worried about what other people think of him. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **agoraphobia**.
   - **Why:** fear of 2 or more typical situations (crowds, public transport, enclosed places) because escape or help might not be available, avoided or needing a companion, for 6 months or more (p. 246).
   - **Trap:** **social anxiety disorder**, where the fear is of being scrutinized and judged (p. 229).

3. A 38-year-old teacher has worried most days for the past year about money, her children's safety, her job and her parents' health. She cannot stop the worrying, feels tense and tired, and sleeps poorly. She has no panic attacks and does not use alcohol or drugs. She asks for a long-term medication. Which is the most appropriate first-line drug?
   - **Answer:** **escitalopram** (an SSRI; an SNRI such as venlafaxine is an equal alternative).
   - **Why:** this is GAD (6 months, many worries, 3 or more of 6 symptoms; pp. 250-251). MSD calls SSRIs and SNRIs the preferred medications; they take 3-6 weeks to work.
   - **Trap:** **alprazolam** (a BZD), which acts immediately but can cause physical dependence with sustained use (MSD).

4. A 27-year-old violinist is comfortable at parties and with coworkers. Before solo concerts only, she develops a pounding heart, sweaty hands and a tremor that ruins her bowing. She wants something to take before performances. Which drug is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **propranolol**, taken before the performance.
   - **Why:** this is social anxiety disorder, performance only (p. 230). Her notes call propranolol the drug of choice for performance anxiety (PDF p. 59); MSD: "For patients who experience distress when they need to perform in public, beta-blockers may be prescribed" to reduce the fast heart rate, trembling and sweating.
   - **Trap:** a **daily SSRI**, which is used for generalized social anxiety but is aimed at broader social fear; her fear is limited to performing, which is where MSD places beta-blockers.

5. A 19-year-old college student needs blood drawn. As the needle approaches she becomes pale and sweaty, and moments later she faints. A nurse notes that her heart rate rose briefly and then fell to 45/min, with a low blood pressure, before she recovered on lying flat. She has avoided vaccines and blood tests since childhood. Which mechanism best explains her fainting?
   - **Answer:** a **vasovagal response** (a brief rise, then a fall, in heart rate and blood pressure).
   - **Why:** blood-injection-injury phobia, unlike other phobias, often causes vasovagal fainting or near-fainting (p. 226).
   - **Trap:** a **sympathetic surge**, which is the typical response in animal, situational and natural environment phobias (p. 226).

6. An 8-year-old boy has refused to go to school for 6 weeks. On school mornings he has stomachaches and cries, saying something bad will happen to his mother while he is away. He will not sleep unless he is in his parents' bed and has nightmares about being lost. He plays happily with friends when his mother is nearby. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **separation anxiety disorder**.
   - **Why:** 3 or more of 8 separation-related signs (distress at separation, worry about harm to the parent, school refusal, refusal to sleep alone, nightmares, physical complaints) for at least 4 weeks in a child (p. 217).
   - **Trap:** **social anxiety disorder**, which would show fear of being judged by peers; he is comfortable with friends (p. 229).

7. A 24-year-old man spends 3 hours a day checking that the stove, door locks and windows are secure, because he fears his apartment will burn down or be robbed. He knows the fear is excessive but feels unbearably anxious if he does not check. He has never had hallucinations. Which psychotherapy is most effective for his condition?
   - **Answer:** **exposure and response prevention** (a form of CBT).
   - **Why:** this is OCD (obsessions and compulsions taking more than 1 hour a day; p. 265). MSD: ERP, which gradually exposes the patient to the trigger while he holds off the ritual, often with cognitive therapy, is the core psychotherapy.
   - **Trap:** **psychodynamic therapy** or simple **reassurance**; reassurance works like another compulsion (standard teaching).

8. A 7-year-old girl's parents say that over 3 days she suddenly began washing her hands dozens of times a day, refusing to touch doorknobs and blinking and grunting repeatedly. Two weeks ago she had a sore throat with fever. She has no chorea, no heart murmur and no joint swelling. Which is the most likely preceding infection?
   - **Answer:** **group A streptococcal pharyngitis**.
   - **Why:** sudden-onset obsessions, compulsions and tics after group A strep infection, without the chorea, carditis or arthritis of rheumatic fever, is described as PANDAS (p. 292).
   - **Trap:** **acute rheumatic fever with Sydenham chorea**, which would show involuntary jerky movements and often carditis or arthritis (p. 292; standard teaching).

9. A 23-year-old woman asks a plastic surgeon for a third nose operation. She spends 4 hours a day checking her nose in mirrors and photos and has stopped dating because her nose is "deformed". On examination her nose looks normal, and the two earlier surgeries had good results. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **refer for psychiatric treatment with an SSRI and CBT** rather than another operation.
   - **Why:** this is body dysmorphic disorder: preoccupation with a flaw others cannot see, plus repetitive checking (p. 271). MSD: cosmetic surgery is usually unsuccessful and may intensify the preoccupation; about 80% have suicidal thoughts.
   - **Trap:** **perform the revision rhinoplasty**, which does not treat the disorder.

10. A 14-year-old girl has had patchy hair loss on her scalp for a year, and her mother has found clumps of hair in her bed. She now has 2 months of early fullness, vomiting after meals and iron deficiency anemia. A firm, mobile epigastric mass is felt. Which is the most likely cause of the mass?
    - **Answer:** a **trichobezoar** (a ball of swallowed hair in the stomach).
    - **Why:** trichotillomania (repeated hair pulling with hair loss; p. 281); swallowing hair can form trichobezoars causing anemia, pain, vomiting and obstruction (p. 283).
    - **Trap:** **gastric carcinoma**, which would be very unusual at 14 and does not explain the hair loss.

11. A 26-year-old woman was assaulted in a parking garage 12 days ago. Since then she has had intrusive memories and nightmares of the attack, feels as if she is in a daze, cannot recall parts of the event, cannot feel happy about anything, avoids the garage, cannot sleep or concentrate, is constantly on guard, and startles at small noises. She has missed work all week. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **acute stress disorder**.
    - **Why:** 9 or more of 14 symptoms across intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance and arousal, lasting **3 days to 1 month** after the trauma (pp. 314-315).
    - **Trap:** **PTSD**, which needs symptoms for **more than 1 month** (p. 302).

12. A 34-year-old veteran has PTSD. After 6 months of trauma-focused CBT and sertraline his flashbacks and avoidance have improved, but he still wakes most nights from vivid nightmares of combat. Which drug is most appropriate to add for the nightmares?
    - **Answer:** **prazosin**.
    - **Why:** MSD: prazosin "appears helpful in reducing nightmares" in PTSD. It is an **α1-adrenergic blocker** (standard teaching).
    - **Trap:** **a nightly benzodiazepine**; MSD says sedating drugs such as BZDs should be used sparingly, if at all, in PTSD.

13. A 46-year-old man was laid off 5 weeks ago. Since then he has felt sad and discouraged, sleeps a little less, and has been snapping at his family. He still enjoys his hobbies, eats normally, has no thoughts of death and is actively applying for jobs. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **adjustment disorder with depressed mood**.
    - **Why:** emotional symptoms within 3 months of an identifiable stressor, with distress or impairment, not meeting criteria for another disorder (p. 319).
    - **Trap:** **major depressive disorder**, which needs 5 of 9 symptoms for 2 weeks (DSM-5-TR p. 183; see guide 47).

14. A 3-year-old boy was adopted 6 months ago from an orphanage where one caregiver looked after 15 infants. In the clinic waiting room he climbs into a stranger's lap, hugs the receptionist, and wanders out of the room without looking back for his adoptive mother. His language and motor skills are close to normal for age. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **disinhibited social engagement disorder**.
    - **Why:** a child raised with extremely insufficient care who readily approaches and is overly familiar with strangers and does not check back with the caregiver (pp. 298-299).
    - **Trap:** **reactive attachment disorder**, which comes from the same kind of neglect but shows a **withdrawn** child who rarely seeks or accepts comfort (p. 295).

15. A 52-year-old woman with panic disorder was given lorazepam in the emergency department and felt calmer within minutes. Lorazepam produces this effect by binding the GABA-A receptor. Which change in the receptor's chloride channel results?
    - **Answer:** **more frequent opening** of the chloride channel.
    - **Why:** BZDs bind between the α and γ subunits of the GABA-A receptor and increase the frequency of chloride channel opening, hyperpolarizing the neuron (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 43).
    - **Trap:** **longer duration of opening**, which is how barbiturates act (PDF p. 43).
