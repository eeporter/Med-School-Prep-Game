# Cranial nerve palsies and brainstem syndromes

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (Horner pathway pp. 27-28, cranial nerves pp. 50-79, gaze pp. 73-75, brainstem blood supply and syndromes pp. 80-84). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where a different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
A brainstem lesion gives **cranial nerve signs on the same side** and **long-tract signs on the opposite side** (the "crossed" pattern, guide 23). The cranial nerve tells you the level: **III and IV = midbrain; V = upper pons; VI, VII and VIII = lower pons; IX, X and XII = upper medulla** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 81). **Medial** lesions hit the motor tract (weakness); **lateral** lesions hit sensory and cerebellar paths (no weakness). For an isolated third nerve palsy, the **pupil** is the key: a blown pupil points to compression (aneurysm, herniation), while a spared pupil points to diabetic or hypertensive ischemia.

## Core mechanism
1. **Why crossed signs?** Your notes: a brainstem stroke almost always hits a cranial nerve plus a long tract; every long-tract sign is on the **opposite** side, except Horner syndrome from the descending hypothalamic fibers, which is always on the **same** side (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 81). Cranial nerve nuclei serve the same side, but the corticospinal tract and the body's sensory tracts have not yet crossed (or have just crossed) at brainstem level. MSD: "Ipsilateral facial sensory loss or motor weakness with contralateral body hemianesthesia or hemiparesis indicates a lesion at the pons or medulla." [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes)
2. **Medial vs lateral (the "rule of 4s", standard teaching):** the 4 **medial** cranial nerves are the ones that divide evenly into 12 (III, IV, VI, XII), and the corticospinal tract is medial too. So a **medial** brainstem lesion gives limb weakness plus one of these nerves. The 4 **lateral** cranial nerves are V, VII, IX and XI. A **lateral** lesion also hits the spinothalamic tract, sympathetic fibers, the vestibular nuclei and cerebellar paths: sensory loss, vertigo, ataxia and Horner, but no limb weakness. In the lateral pons the key nerve is **VII** (facial weakness); in the lateral medulla it is IX and X through the nucleus ambiguus (hoarseness, trouble swallowing).
3. **The third nerve and the pupil:** MSD lists aneurysms (especially posterior communicating) and transtentorial herniation as common causes of palsies that **affect** the pupil, and ischemia of the nerve or midbrain, usually from diabetes or hypertension, as the most common cause of palsies that **spare** it. [MSD Manual: Third Cranial Nerve Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/third-cranial-nerve-oculomotor-disorders) Your notes explain both sides: diabetes damages the vessels on the outside of the nerve, so the **inner motor fibers** starve first (ptosis, down-and-out eye, normal pupil); the **parasympathetic fibers on the outside** are the first hit by **compression** from a PCom aneurysm or uncal herniation (a "blown" pupil). Other causes in your notes: cavernous sinus thrombosis and midbrain stroke. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 53-54)

## The eye-movement nerves
Muscles: lateral rectus = VI, superior oblique = IV, all the rest = III ("LR6 SO4"; El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 52). Your notes on each nerve: **III** also lifts the lid (levator), constricts the pupil and drives accommodation, so a lesion gives ptosis, a down-and-out eye, a big pupil and lost near focusing (p. 52). **IV** depresses and intorts the adducted eye; a lesion makes the eye drift up, gives double vision looking down (stairs, reading), and the head tilts **away** from the bad side; "can't see the floor" (pp. 54-55). **VI** abducts; a lesion gives an inward-turned eye and double vision (p. 58).

| Nerve | What it does (MSD Cranial Nerves table) | Palsy looks like | Common causes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **III, oculomotor** | "Raises eyelids"; moves eyes "up, down, and medially"; pupil and lens | Ptosis, double vision; eye "slightly out and down"; pupil "normal or dilated" | Aneurysm (especially posterior communicating), transtentorial herniation (pupil affected); diabetes or hypertension (pupil spared) |
| **IV, trochlear** | Moves eye "in and down via the superior oblique muscle" | Vertical double vision, "one above and slightly to the side of the other"; trouble going down stairs | Closed head injury (common); often idiopathic; small-vessel infarction (eg, diabetes) |
| **VI, abducens** | Moves the eye "outward (abduction) via the lateral rectus muscle" | Horizontal double vision when looking toward the weak side; eye "slightly adducted" at rest | Raised intracranial pressure, including idiopathic intracranial hypertension; diabetes or hypertension; pontine stroke, aneurysm, cavernous sinus lesions |

Sources for the table: [MSD Manual: Cranial Nerves table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/cranial-nerves); [MSD Manual: Third Cranial Nerve Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/third-cranial-nerve-oculomotor-disorders); [MSD Manual: Fourth Cranial Nerve Palsy](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/fourth-cranial-trochlear-nerve-palsy); [MSD Manual: Sixth Cranial Nerve Palsy](https://msdmanuals.com/en-gb/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/sixth-cranial-nerve-palsy)

- **Fourth nerve head tilt:** "tilting the head to the side opposite the palsied muscle can compensate and eliminate the double images." So a right fourth nerve palsy tilts the head to the **left**. [MSD Manual: Fourth Cranial Nerve Palsy](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/fourth-cranial-trochlear-nerve-palsy)
- **Abducens nucleus vs nerve (your notes):** a sixth **nerve** lesion stops only that eye looking out. A sixth **nucleus** lesion stops **both** eyes looking to that side (lateral gaze palsy), because the nucleus also drives the opposite medial rectus through the MLF, and it causes a **same-side facial palsy**, because facial nerve fibers loop around the nucleus. The pontine gaze center (PPRF) controls gaze to its own side; the frontal eye field controls gaze to the opposite side, so a frontal lesion makes the eyes drift toward the lesion. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 45, 73-75)
- **Sixth nerve and raised pressure:** the sixth nerve is a classic "false localizing sign" when intracranial pressure rises, because its long course makes it easy to stretch (standard teaching; MSD lists raised pressure as a cause but does not use the phrase).
- **Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO):** a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF). The eye on the **same side as the lesion** "cannot adduct (or adducts weakly) past the midline", but it "adducts normally in convergence", and "nystagmus often occurs in the abducting eye". In young people it is "commonly caused by multiple sclerosis and often bilateral"; in older adults it is "typically caused by stroke and is unilateral". **One-and-a-half syndrome** = the horizontal gaze center plus the MLF on the same side. [MSD Manual: Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/internuclear-ophthalmoplegia) Your notes add how to tell INO from a third nerve palsy: INO has no ptosis, no big pupil and normal convergence (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 73).
- **Pupils that react to near but not to light** (your notes, El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 53): **Argyll Robertson pupil**, both sides, from a lesion of the pretectal area; classic in **tabes dorsalis** (late neurosyphilis), sometimes a pineal tumor. **Adie pupil**, one side, from damage to the ciliary ganglion; it reacts sluggishly to light and better to near.

## Horner syndrome
"Ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis due to dysfunction of cervical sympathetic output", from a break anywhere in "the cervical sympathetic pathway running from the hypothalamus to the eye". Central causes include brainstem ischemia and syringomyelia; peripheral causes include a **Pancoast tumor**, cervical adenopathy and **aortic or carotid dissection**. In the congenital form, "the iris does not become pigmented and remains blue-gray". [MSD Manual: Horner Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/autonomic-nervous-system/horner-syndrome)
- **Pathway in your notes:** 1st neuron, descending hypothalamic fibers to the lateral horn of **T1**; 2nd neuron, T1 to the **superior cervical ganglion**; 3rd neuron, along the **internal carotid** to the pupil dilator and the eyelid's superior tarsal (Müller) muscle. Sweat fibers to the face run along the **external** carotid. Causes by neuron: 1st, pontine hemorrhage, lateral medullary syndrome, cord lesions above T1 (Brown-Séquard, syringomyelia); 2nd, **Pancoast tumor** pressing the stellate ganglion; 3rd, **carotid dissection** (painful). Ptosis is mild because only the Müller muscle is lost; the pupil is small because the parasympathetics act unopposed. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 27-28) Many textbooks place this first synapse more broadly at C8-T2, the ciliospinal center of Budge (standard teaching); either way, a cord lesion above it causes Horner.
- **Sweating tells you the level:** the sweat fibers to the face leave the pathway after the superior cervical ganglion and travel with the **external** carotid (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 27). MSD lists Pancoast tumor and carotid dissection among the causes. [MSD Manual: Horner Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/autonomic-nervous-system/horner-syndrome) So a Pancoast tumor (preganglionic) gives facial anhidrosis, while a carotid dissection (postganglionic, along the internal carotid) usually spares facial sweating, because the facial sweat fibers leave with the external carotid (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 27).
- **Horner vs third nerve ptosis:** Horner ptosis is mild because only the small Müller (superior tarsal) muscle loses its nerve supply (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 28); the levator still works. The small pupil shows best in the dark (standard teaching). A third nerve palsy gives a **heavy** droop (levator palpebrae) with a **big** pupil if the pupil fibers are hit (standard teaching).

**Read more on EyeWiki:** [Horner Syndrome](https://eyewiki.org/Horner_Syndrome) (link for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

## Brainstem stroke syndromes

| Syndrome | Level and artery | Same side as the lesion | Opposite side | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Weber** (your notes: medial midbrain syndrome) | Cerebral peduncle (ventromedial midbrain); "most commonly" a branch of the posterior cerebral artery | Third nerve palsy (eye down and out, ptosis, pupil may be dilated) | Hemiplegia, plus lower face weakness (corticobulbar) | El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 84; StatPearls: Weber Syndrome; MSD Stroke Syndromes table (PCA) |
| **Parinaud** (dorsal midbrain) | Pineal region tumor, classically a **germinoma** | **Upward gaze** palsy (superior colliculus); pupils react to near but not light (pretectal area); hydrocephalus from aqueduct compression (headache, vomiting, papilledema); germinomas making beta-hCG can cause precocious puberty in boys | | El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 84 |
| **Claude / Benedikt** | Midbrain, deeper: red nucleus and superior cerebellar peduncle (Claude), red nucleus (Benedikt) | Third nerve palsy | Claude: ataxia and tremor; Benedikt: involuntary movements | MSD Third Cranial Nerve Disorders (names both as midbrain lesions of the third nerve fascicle); the extra signs are standard teaching |
| **Medial pontine** | Paramedian branches of the basilar artery | Sixth nerve: eye turned in, double vision | Spastic weakness (corticospinal); lost touch, position and vibration (medial lemniscus) | El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 83 |
| **Lateral pontine (AICA)** | Lateral lower pons; the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA), which also feeds the middle and inferior cerebellar peduncles | Facial paralysis of the lower motor neuron type, with lost taste on the front two-thirds of the tongue, tearing, salivation and corneal reflex, and hyperacusis (VII; facial nucleus signs point to AICA); hearing loss, vertigo, nausea and nystagmus (VIII); face pain and temperature loss (spinal V); Horner; ataxia (middle cerebellar peduncle) | Body pain and temperature loss | El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 83; Lee H et al. 2001 (Archives of Neurology); Radiopaedia: Lateral pontine syndrome |
| **Locked-in** | Pons (pontine infarct or hemorrhage), in the vertebrobasilar territory; ventral pons and basilar artery are standard teaching | Both sides: quadriplegia, no speech, chewing, swallowing or sideways eye movement | | MSD: Locked-In Syndrome; StatPearls: Locked-in Syndrome |
| **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg)** | Lateral medulla; PICA most often, or vertebral artery dissection | Face pain and temperature loss (spinal V), hoarseness, trouble swallowing and lost gag (nucleus ambiguus; specific to PICA), Horner, limb ataxia (inferior cerebellar peduncle), vertigo | Body pain and temperature loss | El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 82; Osmosis HY Neurology, p. 17; Guide 23 |
| **Medial medullary (Dejerine)** | Medial medulla; anterior spinal artery or vertebral artery | Flaccid tongue weakness; tongue points **toward** the lesion (XII) | Spastic hemiparesis (pyramid); loss of vibration and position sense (medial lemniscus) | El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 82; StatPearls: Medial Medullary Syndrome; Zhou and Cheng 2022 (tongue) |

- **Weber:** a cerebral peduncle lesion gives a same-side third nerve palsy and opposite-side hemiplegia (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 84). Claude syndrome (red nucleus and superior cerebellar peduncle) adds opposite-side ataxia and tremor, and Benedikt syndrome (red nucleus) adds opposite-side involuntary movements (standard teaching); MSD names both as "midbrain lesions that disrupt the oculomotor fascicle". [MSD Manual: Third Cranial Nerve Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/third-cranial-nerve-oculomotor-disorders) Weber "most commonly results from the occlusion of a branch of the posterior cerebral artery" [StatPearls: Weber Syndrome](https://www.statpearls.com/point-of-care/42986), and MSD lists "unilateral third cranial nerve palsy" among posterior cerebral artery stroke findings. [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes) The corticospinal tract running in the cerebral peduncle, above where it crosses, explains the opposite-side weakness (standard teaching).
- **Medial medulla:** "Key medial structures within the medulla include the pyramid, medial lemniscus, hypoglossal nucleus". Findings: "ipsilateral hypoglossal palsy with tongue deviation, contralateral hemiparesis" and "contralateral loss of deep sensation involving vibration and proprioception"; tongue "protrusion often reveals deviation toward the side of the lesion". [StatPearls: Medial Medullary Syndrome](https://www.statpearls.com/point-of-care/24830) A second source: "An infarct in the medulla typically damages the lower motor neuron" and "results in a tongue deviation toward the side of the lesion". [Zhou C, Cheng M. Am J Case Rep 2022;23:e936511](https://amjcaserep.com/abstract/full/idArt/936511) Your notes give the same triad (corticospinal tract, medial lemniscus, XII) and the reason for the tongue sign: the muscles on the damaged side are weak, so the tongue "licks the lesion" (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 79, 82).
- **Locked-in:** MSD defines it as "a state of wakefulness and awareness with quadriplegia and paralysis of the lower cranial nerves". Patients "cannot move their lower face, chew, swallow, speak, breathe, move their limbs, or move their eyes laterally", but "vertical eye movement is possible", so they answer by blinking. The usual cause is a pontine infarct or hemorrhage. [MSD Manual: Locked-In Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/coma-and-impaired-consciousness/locked-in-syndrome) StatPearls adds: "Cognition, vertical eye movement, blinking, and hearing are classically preserved." Consciousness is kept because "the reticular activating system, which lies dorsally, ... is not damaged by such ventral lesions". The most common cause is stroke, and it can also follow central pontine myelinolysis. [StatPearls: Locked-in Syndrome](https://www.statpearls.com/point-of-care/24399) MSD adds that these patients communicate "by vertical eye movements controlled above the pons". [MSD Manual: Hyponatremia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/electrolyte-disorders/hyponatremia)
- **Pons vs medulla, lateral side:** facial paralysis and hearing loss put a lateral lesion in the **pons** (AICA). AICA stroke "is known to be associated with hearing loss, facial weakness, ataxia, nystagmus, and hypalgesia"; the AICA supplies "the dorsolateral pons, middle cerebellar peduncle, inner ear, and anterior inferior cerebellum", and the inner ear's only supply, the internal auditory artery, usually comes from the AICA. [Lee H et al. Arch Neurol 2001;58:1287-1289](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/780050); [Radiopaedia: Lateral pontine syndrome](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/32269) Hoarseness and trouble swallowing (nucleus ambiguus) put it in the **medulla** (Wallenberg) (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 82; Osmosis HY Neurology, p. 17).

**Read more on EyeWiki:** [Wallenberg Syndrome](https://eyewiki.org/Wallenberg_Syndrome) (link for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

- **Facial nerve:** a lower motor neuron (Bell-type) lesion weakens the whole half of the face, forehead included; an upper motor neuron lesion spares the forehead. See guide 16.

## Clinical vignette
A 66-year-old man with hypertension wakes with weakness of his left arm and leg. His right eyelid droops, and his right eye rests down and out with a dilated pupil. Sensation is normal. Where is the lesion?

**Answer:** the right ventromedial midbrain (Weber syndrome), usually from a branch of the posterior cerebral artery.
**Why:** a same-side third nerve palsy with an opposite-side hemiplegia is Weber syndrome, a lesion of the cerebral peduncle (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 84). [StatPearls: Weber Syndrome](https://www.statpearls.com/point-of-care/42986)
**Trap:** uncal herniation (guide 22). Herniation also gives a blown pupil with weakness, but the patient is drowsy or comatose with a mass on imaging; here he is awake with a sudden small stroke.

## Common traps
- Third nerve palsy with a **dilated** pupil and a sudden severe headache = posterior communicating artery aneurysm until proven otherwise; MSD says neuroimaging is "done immediately". [MSD Manual: Third Cranial Nerve Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/third-cranial-nerve-oculomotor-disorders)
- MSD also notes that a posterior communicating aneurysm "occasionally" spares the pupil, so pupil sparing makes ischemia likely, not certain. [MSD Manual: Third Cranial Nerve Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/third-cranial-nerve-oculomotor-disorders)
- INO: name the side by the eye that **can't adduct**, not the eye with nystagmus. A young adult with bilateral INO = MS.
- Tongue points **toward** the lesion (lower motor neuron, XII). The jaw also deviates toward a weak trigeminal side, because the healthy pterygoids push it over (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 58, 79).
- Horner is the one brainstem long-tract sign on the **same** side (El-Husseiny p. 81).
- Can't look up, pupils react to near but not light, in a teenage boy = pineal germinoma (Parinaud) (p. 84).
- Lateral gaze palsy **plus** same-side facial palsy = abducens **nucleus**, not nerve (p. 75).
- Bilateral light-near dissociation = Argyll Robertson (neurosyphilis); one-sided = Adie (ciliary ganglion) (p. 53).
- Locked-in is **not** coma: the patient is awake and can answer with vertical eye movements or blinks.
- Wallenberg has **no** limb weakness; medial medullary syndrome has weakness but no hoarseness.

## Practice questions
1. A 64-year-old woman with long-standing diabetes has double vision and a drooping right eyelid that began over a day. The right eye rests down and out; both pupils are equal and reactive. She has no headache. What is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** ischemia of the third nerve (diabetic microvascular palsy).
   - **Why:** the most common cause of palsies that spare the pupil is ischemia, usually from diabetes or hypertension. [MSD Manual: Third Cranial Nerve Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/third-cranial-nerve-oculomotor-disorders) Your notes explain why: diabetes starves the inner motor fibers, while the pupil fibers on the outside of the nerve are spared (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 53-54).
   - **Trap:** a posterior communicating artery aneurysm, which usually blows the pupil and comes with headache.
2. After a fall from a bicycle, a 19-year-old sees one image above the other, worst when walking down stairs. He holds his head tilted to the left. Which nerve and which side are affected?
   - **Answer:** the right fourth (trochlear) nerve.
   - **Why:** fourth nerve palsy weakens the superior oblique, gives vertical double vision and makes stairs hard; closed head injury is a common cause, and tilting the head "to the side opposite the palsied muscle" compensates. Left tilt means the right side is weak. [MSD Manual: Fourth Cranial Nerve Palsy](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/neuro-ophthalmologic-and-cranial-nerve-disorders/fourth-cranial-trochlear-nerve-palsy)
   - **Trap:** picking the side the head tilts toward.
3. A 70-year-old has sudden weakness of the right arm and leg and lost vibration and position sense on the right. When he sticks out his tongue it points to the left. Pain and temperature sensation are normal. Which artery is blocked?
   - **Answer:** a paramedian branch of the anterior spinal artery (or the vertebral artery) supplying the left medial medulla.
   - **Why:** medial medullary syndrome damages the pyramid (opposite hemiparesis), the medial lemniscus (opposite vibration and position loss) and the hypoglossal nucleus (tongue points toward the lesion). [StatPearls: Medial Medullary Syndrome](https://www.statpearls.com/point-of-care/24830)
   - **Trap:** a left hemisphere stroke. A cortical lesion would not make the tongue point away from the weak limbs with this sensory split.
4. A 58-year-old has sudden vertigo, hearing loss in the left ear, weakness of the whole left face including the forehead, left arm clumsiness, and loss of pain and temperature on the right body. Which artery is involved?
   - **Answer:** the left anterior inferior cerebellar artery (lateral pontine syndrome).
   - **Why:** same-side VII and VIII signs, same-side face pain and temperature loss and limb ataxia, with opposite-side body pain and temperature loss, is the lateral pontine syndrome from AICA and basilar perforator occlusion. [Lee H et al. Arch Neurol 2001](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/780050); [Radiopaedia: Lateral pontine syndrome](https://radiopaedia.org/articles/32269) Hearing loss is the AICA clue because the inner ear's blood comes from it. The forehead is weak too because the facial nucleus or nerve itself is hit, a lower motor neuron lesion (guide 16).
   - **Trap:** Wallenberg (PICA). Facial paralysis and hearing loss point to the pons; hoarseness and trouble swallowing would point to the medulla.
5. A 14-year-old boy has headaches, morning vomiting and cannot look up. His pupils barely react to light but constrict when he focuses on a near object. He has new facial and pubic hair. Where is the lesion?
   - **Answer:** a pineal region germinoma pressing on the dorsal midbrain (Parinaud syndrome).
   - **Why:** superior colliculus compression stops upward gaze, pretectal compression gives light-near dissociation, aqueduct compression causes hydrocephalus, and beta-hCG from the germinoma causes early puberty. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 84)
   - **Trap:** Argyll Robertson pupils from neurosyphilis, which do not cause gaze palsy or hydrocephalus.
