# Eating, feeding and elimination disorders

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the chapters on feeding and eating disorders (pp. 371-398) and elimination disorders (pp. 399-405). Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers. Drug facts are from Pharmacology High Yield (PDF p. 88) and the MSD Manual. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied; check exact wording in the book. Where another source disagrees, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **AN** = anorexia nervosa; **BN** = bulimia nervosa; **BED** = binge-eating disorder; **ARFID** = avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder; **BMI** = body mass index (kg/m²); **CBT** = cognitive-behavioral therapy; **SSRI** = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor; **T3/T4** = triiodothyronine/thyroxine.

## In one line
**Weight decides between AN and BN.** Significantly low weight with fear of weight gain = **AN** (even if she binges and purges). Normal or higher weight with binges plus purging = **BN**. Binges with **no** purging = **BED**. Restricted eating **without** any body-image concern = **ARFID**.

## The eating disorders
| Disorder | What you need | Time rule | Severity | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Anorexia nervosa** | (1) Restricting intake leading to **significantly low body weight**; (2) **intense fear of gaining weight** or behavior that blocks weight gain; (3) distorted experience of weight or shape, self-worth driven by weight, or not recognizing how serious the low weight is | None | By BMI: **mild ≥ 17**, moderate 16-16.99, **severe 15-15.99**, **extreme < 15** | pp. 381-382 |
| **Bulimia nervosa** | Recurrent **binges** (a clearly larger amount than most people would eat in a set time, such as 2 hours, with **loss of control**) plus recurrent **compensatory behavior** (vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, fasting, excess exercise); self-worth driven by shape and weight; **not only during AN** | Both at least **once a week for 3 months** | By compensatory episodes per week: mild 1-3, moderate 4-7, severe 8-13, extreme 14+ | pp. 387-388 |
| **Binge-eating disorder** | Recurrent binges with **3 or more of 5**: eating very fast, eating until uncomfortably full, eating large amounts when not hungry, **eating alone from embarrassment**, feeling disgusted, depressed or guilty afterward; marked distress; **no regular compensatory behavior** | At least **once a week for 3 months** | By binges per week: 1-3, 4-7, 8-13, 14+ | pp. 392-393 |
| **ARFID** | Avoiding or restricting food (no interest in eating, dislike of texture/smell, fear of choking or vomiting) causing weight loss or poor growth, nutritional deficiency, dependence on tube feeds or supplements, or major psychosocial problems | None | | p. 376 |
| **Pica** | Eating **non-food, non-nutritive substances** (DSM examples include paper, hair, soil, chalk, paint, clay, starch and ice, p. 372) inappropriate for developmental level and not a cultural practice | At least **1 month** | | pp. 371-372 |
| **Rumination disorder** | **Repeated regurgitation** of food that is re-chewed, re-swallowed or spat out, not due to a medical condition such as reflux or **pyloric stenosis** | At least **1 month** | | p. 374 |

**AN subtypes (p. 381):** **restricting type** (weight loss by dieting, fasting or exercise, with no recurrent binging or purging in the last 3 months) and **binge-eating/purging type**. Someone who binges and purges **only while underweight** has AN binge-eating/purging type, **not** BN (p. 391).

**Amenorrhea is not a criterion** (it is not in criteria A-C, p. 381), though it is common (p. 383).

## What the body shows
**Anorexia nervosa (pp. 383-386)**
- **Exam:** emaciation, **low blood pressure, low temperature, slow heart rate**, **lanugo** (fine downy body hair), edema during refeeding, yellow skin from high carotene (p. 386).
- **Blood:** **leukopenia**, mild anemia, low platelets; high BUN from dehydration; **high cholesterol**; raised liver enzymes; sometimes low magnesium, zinc and phosphate (p. 385).
- **Thyroid:** T4 low-normal, **T3 low, reverse T3 high** (p. 385).
- **Sex hormones:** low estrogen in females, low testosterone in males (p. 385).
- **ECG:** **sinus bradycardia** is common; some have a **prolonged QTc** (p. 385).
- **Bone:** low bone mineral density, often not fully reversible (pp. 383, 385).
- **If she vomits:** metabolic alkalosis, low chloride and low potassium; laxative abuse can cause a mild metabolic acidosis (p. 385).
- **Mortality:** crude mortality about **5% per decade**, from medical complications or suicide (p. 384). Suicide risk is about **18 times** that of matched peers, and suicide is the second leading cause of death in AN (p. 386).

**Bulimia nervosa (p. 391)**
- Weight is usually **normal** (MSD: "Patients are typically of normal weight").
- **From vomiting:** **hypokalemia** (can cause arrhythmias), **low chloride**, and **metabolic alkalosis** (high bicarbonate) from losing stomach acid; laxatives or diuretics can instead cause metabolic acidosis.
- **Mildly raised amylase** (salivary type) and **enlarged parotid glands**.
- **Dental enamel erosion**, especially on the tongue side of the front teeth; more cavities.
- **Calluses or scars on the back of the hand** from the teeth when inducing vomiting. These are often called **Russell's sign** (standard teaching).
- About a quarter to a third have had suicidal thoughts, and a similar share have attempted suicide (p. 391).

## Who gets them (DSM-5-TR)
| | **Anorexia nervosa** | **Bulimia nervosa** | **Binge-eating disorder** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lifetime prevalence (US) | 0.6-0.8%; much higher in women (p. 384) | 0.28-1.0%; much higher in women (pp. 389-390) | **0.85-2.8%**, the most common of the three; women 2-3 times men for 12-month prevalence (p. 394) |
| Onset | Adolescence or young adulthood; rarely before puberty or after 40; often after a stressor such as leaving for college (p. 384) | Adolescence or young adulthood (p. 390) | |
| Other | Most remit within 5 years (p. 384) | | Linked to weight gain and obesity (p. 395) |

- **Pica:** about **5% of school-age children**; about **a third of pregnant women**, especially with food insecurity (p. 372). It can cause bowel obstruction from a bezoar, infections such as toxoplasmosis and toxocariasis, and **lead poisoning from paint** (p. 372).
- **Rumination:** in infants it usually starts at **3-12 months** and often goes away by itself, but it can be fatal from malnutrition (p. 375).

## Elimination disorders (pp. 399-405)
| | **Enuresis** | **Encopresis** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What | Repeated passing of **urine** into bed or clothes, on purpose or not | Repeated passing of **feces** into inappropriate places, on purpose or not |
| How often | **At least twice a week for 3 months in a row**, or causing distress or impairment | **At least once a month for 3 months** |
| Minimum age | **5 years** (or equal developmental level) | **4 years** (or equal developmental level) |
| Exclusions | Not due to a substance (diuretic, antipsychotic) or a medical condition (diabetes, spina bifida, seizures) | Not due to a substance (laxatives) or a medical condition, except through constipation |
| Subtypes | **Nocturnal only** (most common), diurnal only, both | **With constipation and overflow incontinence** (most children with encopresis) or without |
| Pages | p. 399 | pp. 402-403 |

- **Enuresis course:** **primary** (never dry) or **secondary** (wet again after being dry; usually starts at 5-8 years) (p. 400). Nighttime wetting affects about 5-10% of 5-year-olds, 3-5% of 10-year-olds and about 1% at 15 and older; more common in boys (p. 400).
- **Encopresis with constipation:** stool is often poorly formed, leakage can be constant, and the soiling **resolves once the constipation is treated** (p. 402). A rectal exam showing impaction supports it (p. 404). Soiling **without** constipation is usually linked to **oppositional defiant or conduct disorder** (p. 403).

## Treatment (MSD and Pharmacology High Yield)
| Disorder | Management |
| --- | --- |
| **Anorexia nervosa** | Outpatient psychotherapy is "the cornerstone of treatment"; **family-based (Maudsley) therapy for adolescents**. Feeding starts at about **30-40 kcal/kg/day** and is raised gradually. **Olanzapine** may help weight gain. Hospitalize for weight below about 75% of the healthy weight, rapid loss, symptomatic hypotension or extreme bradycardia ([MSD: Anorexia Nervosa, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/feeding-and-eating-disorders/anorexia-nervosa)) |
| **Refeeding syndrome** | A rare metabolic problem "characterized by hypophosphatemia and other electrolyte abnormalities", mostly in the most undernourished patients (MSD, same page). Low phosphate can cause weakness, heart failure, arrhythmias and confusion (standard teaching) |
| **Bulimia nervosa** | "Cognitive behavioral therapy is the treatment of choice." **Fluoxetine** is often used in adults; SSRIs alone are not ideal without psychotherapy ([MSD: Bulimia Nervosa, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/feeding-and-eating-disorders/bulimia-nervosa)). **Avoid bupropion** (seizure risk; see guide 47, MSD) |
| **Binge-eating disorder** | **CBT** is "the most studied and best-supported treatment"; **lisdexamfetamine** for moderate to severe BED; antidepressants such as SSRIs have short-term effectiveness; topiramate or orlistat may help ([MSD: Binge-Eating Disorder, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/feeding-and-eating-disorders/binge-eating-disorder)) |
| **Nocturnal enuresis** | MSD: "The most effective long-term strategy, when no organic causes are present, is a **bed-wetting alarm**." **Desmopressin** reduces wetting, but the effect usually does not last after stopping. **Imipramine** only for resistant cases, because of a rare risk of sudden death. About **14%** resolve on their own each year ([MSD: Urinary Incontinence in Children, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/urinary-incontinence-in-children/urinary-incontinence-in-children)) |

**Other source (enuresis):**
- Her notes call **desmopressin** (a selective V2 analog) the drug of choice for nocturnal enuresis (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 88). Among drugs that fits MSD, which prefers desmopressin over imipramine. But MSD names the **bed alarm**, not a drug, as the most effective long-term strategy. If a question asks for the best long-term treatment of uncomplicated bed-wetting, answer **alarm**; if it asks for a drug (for example for a sleepover), answer **desmopressin**.
- DSM-5-TR sets the minimum age at **5 years** (p. 399). MSD says enuresis "is usually not diagnosed until age 7". Use DSM's age 5 for diagnostic questions.

## Clinical vignette
A 17-year-old ballet student is brought in by her mother after fainting in class. She is 165 cm tall and weighs 41 kg (**BMI 15.1**). She has lost 9 kg in the past 3 months, eats about 600 kcal a day, runs 10 km daily, and says her thighs are "huge". She has not had a period for 5 months. Pulse is 42/min, temperature 35.6 °C, and she has fine hair on her back. She denies binging or vomiting. Low weight from restriction, fear of gaining weight and distorted body image = **anorexia nervosa, restricting type**; a BMI of 15-15.99 is **severe** (pp. 381-382). Bradycardia, low temperature and lanugo are typical (p. 386). Her extreme bradycardia and rapid weight loss justify admission, and feeding should start low and rise gradually to avoid **refeeding syndrome** (hypophosphatemia) (MSD).

## Common traps
- **Underweight + binging/purging = AN binge-eating/purging type, not BN.**
- **Amenorrhea is not required** for AN.
- **AN severity is by BMI** (≥ 17 mild, < 15 extreme); **BN severity is by compensatory episodes per week**; **BED severity is by binges per week**.
- **BN and BED both need binges at least weekly for 3 months.** BED has **no** compensatory behavior.
- **ARFID has no fear of fatness or body-image distortion.**
- **Vomiting → hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis; laxatives → metabolic acidosis.**
- **AN thyroid: low T3, high reverse T3, low-normal T4.** Do not treat it as hypothyroidism (standard teaching).
- **Refeeding syndrome = hypophosphatemia.** Start feeding low and go slow.
- **Bupropion is contraindicated in bulimia** (seizures).
- **Enuresis: age 5, twice a week for 3 months. Encopresis: age 4, once a month for 3 months.**
- **Bed alarm = best long-term enuresis treatment; desmopressin = fast but relapse when stopped.**
- **Encopresis usually means constipation with overflow; treat the constipation.**

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 19-year-old college student has a BMI of 16.4. She eats very little during the week and, about twice a week, eats a large amount of food in an hour and then makes herself vomit. She is terrified of gaining weight and believes her stomach is fat. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **anorexia nervosa, binge-eating/purging type**.
   - **Why:** significantly low weight, fear of weight gain and distorted body image meet AN (p. 381). Binging and purging that happen only while underweight are diagnosed as this AN subtype, not as BN (p. 391).
   - **Trap:** **bulimia nervosa**, which cannot be diagnosed when the behavior occurs only during AN (p. 387).

2. A 16-year-old girl with anorexia nervosa, restricting type, has a BMI of 15.8. She reports fatigue and cold intolerance. Which pattern of thyroid tests is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **low T3, high reverse T3, and low-normal T4.**
   - **Why:** this is the typical starvation pattern in AN (p. 385).
   - **Trap:** **high TSH with low T4** (primary hypothyroidism), which is not the AN pattern (standard teaching).

3. A 20-year-old woman with severe anorexia nervosa is admitted and started on oral nutrition. On day 3 she develops muscle weakness, shortness of breath, leg swelling and confusion. Which laboratory abnormality is most likely responsible?
   - **Answer:** **hypophosphatemia**.
   - **Why:** refeeding syndrome is characterized by hypophosphatemia and other electrolyte problems and occurs mainly in the most undernourished patients (MSD). Low phosphate impairs energy (ATP) production in muscle and heart (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **hyperkalemia**; refeeding shifts potassium into cells and tends to lower it (standard teaching).

4. A 21-year-old woman of normal weight has binged and then made herself vomit about three times a week for 6 months. She has painless swelling in front of both ears and calluses on the back of her right hand. Which set of serum findings is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **low potassium, low chloride and high bicarbonate (metabolic alkalosis).**
   - **Why:** vomiting loses stomach acid, causing metabolic alkalosis, with hypokalemia and hypochloremia (p. 391). Parotid swelling and hand calluses fit bulimia nervosa (p. 391).
   - **Trap:** **metabolic acidosis**, which is the pattern with laxative or diuretic abuse, not vomiting (p. 391).

5. A 22-year-old woman with bulimia nervosa has started cognitive-behavioral therapy and wants medication as well. She has mild depression. Which drug is most appropriate to add?
   - **Answer:** **fluoxetine**.
   - **Why:** CBT is the treatment of choice; fluoxetine is often used for bulimia in adults (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **bupropion**, which is contraindicated in bulimia because of seizure risk (MSD, guide 47).

6. A 40-year-old man with a BMI of 34 says that twice a week for the past year he eats large amounts of food quickly in the evening, even when not hungry, alone because he is embarrassed, and feels disgusted afterward. He does not vomit, fast or exercise to make up for it. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **binge-eating disorder**.
   - **Why:** recurrent binges with 3 or more associated features, marked distress, at least weekly for 3 months, and no compensatory behavior (pp. 392-393).
   - **Trap:** **bulimia nervosa**, which requires recurrent compensatory behavior (p. 387).

7. A 10-year-old boy eats only five foods (plain pasta, crackers, apples, chicken nuggets and milk) and gags on anything with a lumpy texture. He has fallen from the 50th to the 5th percentile for weight over 2 years. He does not worry about his body size or shape. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder**.
   - **Why:** food avoidance based on sensory features causing poor weight gain, with **no** disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced (p. 376).
   - **Trap:** **anorexia nervosa**, which needs fear of weight gain and a distorted body image (p. 381).

8. A 3-year-old boy living in an old apartment building has been seen repeatedly chewing flaking windowsill paint for the past 2 months. He is irritable, constipated and has a microcytic anemia. Which complication of his eating behavior is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **lead poisoning**.
   - **Why:** pica (eating non-food substances for at least 1 month, p. 371) can cause poisoning, such as lead from paint (p. 372).
   - **Trap:** **intestinal obstruction from a bezoar**, which is also possible (p. 372) but does not explain the anemia and irritability.

9. A 7-month-old girl brings up milk 15-30 minutes after most feeds, chews it and swallows it again, and seems content while doing it. She is gaining weight slowly. She had no vomiting in her first 2 months of life, and a trial of acid suppression did not help. She has done this for 6 weeks. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **rumination disorder**.
   - **Why:** repeated regurgitation that is re-chewed or re-swallowed for at least 1 month, not explained by reflux or pyloric stenosis (p. 374); in infants it typically starts at 3-12 months (p. 375).
   - **Trap:** **gastroesophageal reflux**, which the criteria require to be excluded (p. 374).

10. A 7-year-old boy has wet the bed 3-4 nights a week since toilet training. He is dry during the day. Urinalysis and examination are normal. His parents want the treatment most likely to keep him dry after it is stopped. Which is the most appropriate treatment?
    - **Answer:** **a bed-wetting alarm.**
    - **Why:** primary nocturnal enuresis (p. 399). MSD: the alarm is the most effective long-term strategy when no organic cause is present.
    - **Trap:** **desmopressin**, which reduces wetting but usually does not keep children dry after it is stopped (MSD).

11. A 6-year-old girl soils her underwear with soft stool several times a week for 4 months. She has hard, painful bowel movements every 5 days, and a large mass of hard stool is felt on rectal examination. Which is the most appropriate first step in management?
    - **Answer:** **clear the impaction and treat the constipation** (for example with an osmotic laxative and a toilet routine).
    - **Why:** this is encopresis with constipation and overflow incontinence (pp. 402, 404); the soiling resolves after the constipation is treated (p. 402). The specific regimen is standard teaching.
    - **Trap:** **a behavioral reward chart alone**, which does not treat the underlying constipation.
