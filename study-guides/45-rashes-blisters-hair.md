# Rashes, drug reactions, scaly plaques, blistering diseases and hair loss

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: Osmosis Dermatology High Yield Notes (dermatitis and eczema pp. 10-15; erythema multiforme and SJS/TEN pp. 16-20; hair pp. 21-23; papulosquamous disorders pp. 32-37; vesiculobullous diseases pp. 64-67; printed footer page numbers). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual, FDA pages or drug labels; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **AD** = atopic dermatitis; **EM** = erythema multiforme; **SJS** = Stevens-Johnson syndrome; **TEN** = toxic epidermal necrolysis; **BSA** = body surface area; **HSV** = herpes simplex virus; **HLA** = human leukocyte antigen; **IL** = interleukin; **TNF** = tumor necrosis factor; **JAK** = Janus kinase; **PDE4** = phosphodiesterase 4; **BP** = bullous pemphigoid; **PV** = pemphigus vulgaris; **EB** = epidermolysis bullosa; **IVIG** = intravenous immunoglobulin; **UVB/UVA** = ultraviolet B/A light.

## In one line
Most rashes on the exam are sorted by **which immune pathway** drives them (IgE and Th2 in eczema, T cells in contact dermatitis, lichen planus and SJS, Th17 in psoriasis, antibodies in blistering diseases) and by **where the skin splits**. A split **inside the epidermis** gives a **floppy blister that rubs off** (pemphigus). A split **below the epidermis** gives a **tense blister** (pemphigoid).

## Dermatitis and eczema (pp. 10-15)

### Atopic dermatitis (pp. 10-12)
- **Why it happens:** two problems together (p. 10).
  - A **leaky skin barrier**, classically **loss-of-function mutations in filaggrin**, lets antigens in.
  - The immune system leans toward **Th2** cells, which release **IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13**, drive IgE and recruit mast cells and eosinophils.

  MSD agrees: filaggrin mutation is a major predisposing factor, and inflammation "includes a Th2-dominant component" that "drives IgE production" ([MSD: Atopic Dermatitis, updated Sept 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/dermatitis/atopic-dermatitis-eczema)).
- **Atopic triad:** eczema, asthma and allergic rhinitis in the person or family (p. 10).
- **Where it shows up by age (p. 11):**
  - **Infants (0-2 years):** cheeks, scalp and **extensor** surfaces; weeping, crusted plaques.
  - **Children (2-16 years):** **flexural** creases (inside elbows, behind knees), wrists, ankles and neck; thickened (**lichenified**) skin.
  - **Adults:** localized lichenified plaques in the flexures.

  MSD gives the same pattern.
- **Complications (p. 11):** ***S. aureus*** skin infection (impetigo), and **eczema herpeticum**, which is HSV spreading fast over eczematous skin. MSD: eczema herpeticum is "an infection of the skin with herpes simplex virus (HSV)" that is more widespread than HSV in people without eczema.
- **Labs:** high serum IgE (p. 11).
- **Treatment (pp. 11-12):**
  - Thick, unscented creams or ointments right after bathing; avoid heat and dry air.
  - Topical steroids; **topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus); antihistamines for itch.
  - Oral cyclosporine; **dupilumab (an IL-4 receptor blocker)**.

**Other source (antihistamines):** MSD says oral antihistamines "can help relieve pruritus via their sedating properties" (hydroxyzine or diphenhydramine at bedtime). For non-sedating ones (loratadine, fexofenadine, cetirizine) "their efficacy has not been established."

**Recent change (more targeted eczema drugs):** besides dupilumab, MSD now lists:
- topical **crisaborole** (a PDE4 inhibitor) and topical **ruxolitinib** cream (a JAK inhibitor);
- **narrowband UVB**;
- the biologic **tralokinumab**;
- the oral JAK inhibitors **upadacitinib, abrocitinib and baricitinib**.

MSD says dupilumab "blocks the signaling of the proinflammatory Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13". Tralokinumab blocks **IL-13** (standard teaching).

### Contact dermatitis (pp. 12-13)
| | **Irritant** | **Allergic** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How | Direct damage to skin (soaps, solvents, water, friction); no prior sensitization needed | **Type IV (delayed, T-cell) hypersensitivity**; needs prior sensitization |
| Classic causes | Frequent handwashing, detergents | **Poison ivy, oak and sumac; nickel**; fragrances, dyes |
| Symptom clue | **Burning** | **Itching** |
| Test | History | **Patch test** |

From pp. 12-13. Her notes say irritant contact dermatitis is the most common form (p. 12).
- **How allergic contact dermatitis works (p. 12):** the allergen is a **hapten**, a small molecule that crosses the stratum corneum and binds skin proteins to become a full antigen. Dendritic cells carry it to lymph nodes and prime T cells. On re-exposure, memory T cells in the skin cause the rash.
- **Treatment (p. 13):** avoid the trigger; mild steroid or calamine for itch; high-potency topical steroids or oral steroids for allergic disease.

**Other source (contact dermatitis):** from [MSD: Contact Dermatitis, updated Apr 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/dermatitis/contact-dermatitis).
- Allergic contact dermatitis "is a type IV, T-cell–mediated, delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction."
- "Nickel sulfate is the most common contact allergen in most populations." Poison ivy's allergen is **urushiol**.
- The rash "typically takes ≥ 1 day after exposure to become noticeable."
- Treatment: mid- to high-potency topical steroids, and "Oral corticosteroids, given as a 7 to 14 day course" for severe or extensive disease.

### Seborrheic dermatitis (pp. 14-15)
- **What it looks like:** red plaques with **yellow, greasy scales** where sebaceous glands are dense: scalp (**dandruff** in adults, **cradle cap** in infants), eyebrows, **nasolabial folds**, ears, central chest (p. 14).
- **Why:** the skin reacts to ***Malassezia*** yeast. Oil production is **not** increased (p. 14).
- **Risk (p. 14):** **HIV**, **Parkinson disease**, stress, cold dry weather. MSD agrees that it is more common and more severe in HIV and in Parkinson disease ([MSD: Seborrheic Dermatitis, updated Apr 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/dermatitis/seborrheic-dermatitis)).
- **Treatment (p. 15):** antifungal shampoo (MSD: **ketoconazole 2% or 1%**), short courses of topical steroids, calcineurin inhibitors; for cradle cap, oil overnight and gentle brushing.

## Drug and infection reactions: EM and SJS/TEN (pp. 16-20)

### Erythema multiforme (pp. 16-18)
- **Cause:** usually an **infection**, and **HSV is the primary cause**; drugs are a rare cause (p. 16). MSD: "The most common cause of erythema multiforme is herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection"; mycoplasma is another trigger ([MSD: Erythema Multiforme, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hypersensitivity-and-reactive-skin-disorders/erythema-multiforme)).
- **Lesion:** **target lesions**: a dusky center, a pale swollen ring and a red outer edge (p. 17). They are symmetric, start on the **hands, feet and face**, and spread toward the trunk (p. 17). MSD adds that palms and soles are often involved.
- **Course:** usually goes away in 1-2 weeks (p. 17).
- **Treatment (pp. 17-18):** treat the cause. **For recurrent HSV-linked EM, give antivirals continuously for up to 6 months.** MSD suggests valacyclovir, famciclovir or acyclovir for 6 months.

**Recent change (EM is no longer the mild end of SJS):** her notes describe "erythema multiforme major" as often drug-related, with skin detachment and a Nikolsky sign (p. 17). MSD says EM was once seen as the mild end of a spectrum with SJS and TEN, but "it is now considered a different entity." Widespread purple spots and blisters mainly on the **trunk and face** are "more likely to have Stevens-Johnson syndrome rather than erythema multiforme." Exam rule: **targets on the hands after a cold sore = EM; dusky blistering trunk rash with mucosal erosions after a new drug = SJS.**

### Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis (pp. 18-20)
- **What it is:** cytotoxic (**CD8**) T cells kill keratinocytes that display a drug antigen, so the epidermis dies and peels off (p. 18). SJS and TEN are the same disease at different sizes (p. 18).
- **Size cutoffs (p. 19):** **SJS < 10% BSA**, overlap 10-30%, **TEN > 30%**. MSD uses the same cutoffs.
- **Drugs (p. 18):** **allopurinol, sulfa drugs, lamotrigine, carbamazepine**, nevirapine, oxicam NSAIDs. Infection: ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae***.
- **Story (p. 19):** flu-like illness (fever, sore throat) first, then burning skin and eyes, dusky red-purple spots that blister and peel, **painful mouth, eye and genital erosions**, and a **positive Nikolsky sign**.
- **Genetic risk (p. 18):** **HLA-B\*15:02** and HLA-A\*31:01 for carbamazepine.
- **Complications (p. 18):** sepsis, dehydration, kidney failure, and **eye scarring with vision loss**.
- **Care (p. 19):** stop the drug; burn unit or ICU; fluids; warm room (30-32 °C); infection control; pain control.

**Other source (SJS/TEN details):** from [MSD: SJS and TEN, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hypersensitivity-and-reactive-skin-disorders/stevens-johnson-syndrome-sjs-and-toxic-epidermal-necrolysis-ten).
- The prodrome starts "Within 1 to 3 weeks after the start of the offending medication."
- MSD adds **HLA-B\*58:01 for allopurinol** and **immune checkpoint inhibitors** to the drug list.
- **In children, infection (most often *M. pneumoniae*) is the most likely cause.**
- Adult mortality "can be as high as 25 to 35%."
- The **SCORTEN** score counts 7 risk factors on day 1: age ≥ 40, cancer, heart rate ≥ 120, BUN > 28 mg/dL, detachment ≥ 10%, bicarbonate < 20 mEq/L, glucose > 250 mg/dL.
- "Ophthalmology consultation and specialized eye care are mandatory" when the eyes are involved.

**Recent change (drug treatment of SJS/TEN):** her notes list **IVIG** (p. 19). MSD now says "Pharmacotherapy of SJS/TEN is controversial." IVIG trials "have reported conflicting results". **Cyclosporine** "has been shown to decrease the duration of active disease" and may "possibly decrease mortality". **Etanercept** "can additionally reduce disease-specific mortality", though evidence is limited. "The use of systemic glucocorticoids remains controversial." Stopping the drug and burn-unit care are still the core.

## Hair loss (pp. 21-23)
| | **Alopecia areata** | **Telogen effluvium** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pattern | **Smooth, round bald patches**; no scarring | **Diffuse thinning** all over; no scarring |
| Cause | **Autoimmune** (T cells attack the hair bulb) | A **stressor** (illness, surgery, childbirth, drugs, poor diet) pushes many hairs into the **resting (telogen) phase** at once |
| Clue | **"Exclamation point" hairs** (short broken hairs, narrower near the scalp) at the edge; **nail pitting** | Shedding starts **3-4 months** after the stressor (MSD); **positive hair-pull test** |
| Associations | Psoriasis, **vitiligo, thyroid disease** | Nail lines running side to side |
| Treatment | **Intralesional triamcinolone**, topical steroids, minoxidil | Remove the stressor; it usually corrects itself |

From pp. 21-22, except the 3-4 month timing (MSD).

**Other source (hair loss):** MSD says telogen effluvium shows up "3 to 4 months after a major stressor". Its pull test grasps about 40 hairs and is positive "If > 4 to 6 hairs come out with each pull". Her notes say grasp 50-60 hairs, positive if > 6-10 (p. 22). Hair loss "is usually temporary" ([MSD: Alopecia, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hair-disorders/alopecia)). The Alopecia Areata page says it "occasionally coexists with autoimmune vitiligo or thyroiditis", so thyroid testing is done when signs suggest it ([MSD: Alopecia Areata, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hair-disorders/alopecia-areata)).

**Recent change (JAK inhibitors for alopecia areata):** MSD: "Baricitinib, ritlecitinib, and deuruxolitinib may be used for the treatment of severe alopecia areata in adolescents and adults" ([MSD: Alopecia Areata, updated Sept 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hair-disorders/alopecia-areata)). Her notes say there is no cure and treatment is unreliable (p. 22). MSD also lists oral minoxidil and contact immunotherapy.

## Scaly plaques: papulosquamous disorders (pp. 32-37)

### Side-by-side
| | **Psoriasis** | **Lichen planus** | **Pityriasis rosea** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Look | **Salmon-pink plaques with silvery scale** | **Purple, flat-topped, polygonal, itchy papules** (the "P" words) with **Wickham striae** (white lacy lines) | **Herald patch**, then oval scaly patches in a **"Christmas tree"** pattern on the back |
| Where | **Extensor** surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, low back | **Flexor** wrists, ankles, shins; **mouth** | Trunk |
| Signs | **Auspitz sign** (pinpoint bleeding when scale is pulled off), **Koebner**, **nail pitting and oil spots** | **Koebner** | Often after a cold-like illness |
| Immune driver | **Th1/Th17 cells** (IL-17, IL-22, TNF) | **CD8 T cells** attack the basal layer | Possibly **HHV-7** |
| Histology | Thick epidermis, **parakeratosis**, **Munro microabscesses** (neutrophils in the top layer) | **Sawtooth** interface infiltrate of lymphocytes, **Civatte bodies** | Rarely biopsied |
| Course | Lifelong, relapsing | Skin lesions fade over months | **Resolves by itself** |

From pp. 32-36.

### Psoriasis (pp. 35-37)
- **Mechanism (p. 35):** T cells (CD4 Th1, **Th17**, CD8) gather in the epidermis and release IFN-γ, TNF-α, **IL-17** and IL-22, so keratinocytes divide too fast and keratinize poorly.
- **Triggers (p. 35):** skin injury, infection, stress, winter, and drugs: **lithium, beta-blockers, chloroquine**. MSD adds ACE inhibitors, indomethacin, terbinafine, interferon-alfa, immune checkpoint inhibitors and TNF inhibitors ([MSD: Psoriasis, updated Feb 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/psoriasis-and-other-papulosquamous-disorders/psoriasis)).
- **Types (p. 36):**
  - **Plaque**, 90%.
  - **Guttate**: small drop-like spots after **group A strep throat**.
  - **Inverse**: in skin folds.
  - **Pustular**: sterile pus.
  - **Erythrodermic**: the whole body is red; can be fatal; can follow **steroid withdrawal**.
- **Nails (p. 36):** pitting, **oil-spot** discoloration, onycholysis.
- **Psoriatic arthritis (pp. 35-36):** asymmetric, **DIP joints**, **"sausage digits"** (dactylitis), enthesitis; **rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP negative**; on X-ray, erosions with new bone growth.
- **Treatment (p. 37):**
  - **Topical:** steroids, **vitamin D analogs (calcipotriene)**, anthralin, coal tar; the combination of a steroid and calcipotriene works best.
  - **Systemic:** methotrexate, cyclosporine, **acitretin**, biologics (TNF inhibitors, **ustekinumab against IL-12/23**).
  - **Phototherapy.**

**Other source (psoriasis):** MSD says "Up to 30% of patients develop psoriatic arthritis" (5-30% in its key points); her notes say 10-15% (p. 35). MSD lists **narrowband UVB** and PUVA (psoralen plus UVA) as phototherapy, while her notes list UVA (p. 37). MSD warns that "Systemic glucocorticoids may precipitate exacerbations or development of pustular psoriasis" and "should not be used to treat psoriasis."

**Recent change (newer psoriasis drugs):** her notes list **alefacept**, a T-cell drug (p. 37). MSD's current list does not include it. MSD now lists:
- **IL-17 blockers:** secukinumab, ixekizumab, bimekizumab, brodalumab.
- **IL-23 blockers:** guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab.
- **Ustekinumab** (IL-12/23).
- **TNF inhibitors.**
- Oral **apremilast** (a PDE4 inhibitor) and **deucravacitinib** (a TYK2 inhibitor; standard teaching for the target).
- Topical **tapinarof** and **roflumilast**.

### Lichen planus (pp. 32-34)
- **Remember the Ps:** **P**lanar (flat-topped), **P**urple, **P**olygonal, **P**ruritic **P**apules and **P**laques (p. 33).
- **Wickham striae:** grey-white lacy lines on the papules and on the **inner cheeks** (pp. 32-33).
- **Koebner phenomenon:** new lesions appear where the skin is scratched (p. 33).
- **Mouth, esophagus and genitals** can be involved. **Long-lasting erosive mouth lesions can turn into SCC** (p. 32).
- **Histology:** **sawtooth** junction with a band of lymphocytes, and dead basal cells (**Civatte bodies**) (p. 33).
- **Drugs can cause a lichenoid reaction** (p. 32).

**Other source (lichen planus):** MSD links oral lichen planus with hepatitis, "particularly, hepatitis C–induced liver insufficiency". Drug causes include beta-blockers, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, thiazides and antimalarials. Long-standing oral erosions "rarely become cancerous". MSD lists **narrowband UVB** "as an alternative to oral therapies". For oral lichen planus it calls JAK and TYK2 inhibitors "Emerging strategies" that are "still under evaluation" ([MSD: Lichen Planus, updated Feb 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/psoriasis-and-scaling-diseases/lichen-planus)).

### Pityriasis rosea (pp. 34-35)
- A single oval **herald patch** on the trunk comes first. **1-2 weeks later**, many oval pink scaly patches follow skin lines in a **"Christmas tree"** pattern (p. 34). Itching; sometimes a mild cold first (p. 34).
- **Cause:** possibly **HHV-7** (p. 34).
- **Treatment:** usually none. Antihistamines for itch; it goes away by itself in 6-8 weeks (p. 35).

**Other source (pityriasis rosea):** from [MSD: Pityriasis Rosea, updated Nov 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/psoriasis-and-other-papulosquamous-disorders/pityriasis-rosea).
- MSD names HHV 6, 7 and 8.
- It "usually spontaneously remits within 5 weeks."
- **"Serologic testing for syphilis is indicated when the palms or soles are affected"**, when there is no herald patch, or when the pattern is unusual.
- **In pregnancy it "is associated with preterm birth or fetal demise"**, highest in the first 15 weeks, so pregnant patients should be offered acyclovir.

## Blistering diseases (pp. 64-67)

### Pemphigus vulgaris vs bullous pemphigoid
| | **Pemphigus vulgaris (PV)** | **Bullous pemphigoid (BP)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antibody target | **Desmoglein** (holds keratinocytes to each other: desmosomes) (p. 66) | **Hemidesmosome** protein **BPAg2 / BP180 (collagen XVII)** (holds the base of the epidermis to the basement membrane) (p. 64) |
| Split | **Inside the epidermis**, just above the basal layer: **acantholysis**; basal cells left standing like "**tombstones**" (pp. 66-67) | **Below the epidermis** (subepidermal); blister full of **eosinophils** (p. 65) |
| Blister | **Flaccid**, breaks easily, painful erosions (p. 66) | **Tense**, hard to break; itchy (p. 65) |
| Nikolsky sign | **Positive** (p. 66) | **Negative** (p. 65) |
| Mouth | **Oral mucosa is the most common site** (p. 66); MSD: "Often, oral lesions precede skin involvement." | Less common (see Other source) |
| Age | Adults (p. 66); MSD: "usually occurs in middle-aged patients" | **Older than 60** (p. 64) |
| Danger | Life-threatening (p. 66) | Usually less severe |
| Immunofluorescence | IgG **between keratinocytes** (net-like) (standard teaching) | **Linear** IgG and complement **along the basement membrane** (MSD) |

**Other source (blistering diseases):** from [MSD: Pemphigus Vulgaris, updated Feb 2024](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bullous-diseases/pemphigus-vulgaris) and [MSD: Bullous Pemphigoid, updated Feb 2024](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bullous-diseases/bullous-pemphigoid).
- **PV:** antibodies target "desmoglein 3 and sometimes desmoglein 1". "Often, oral lesions precede skin involvement." "Without treatment, pemphigus vulgaris is often fatal, usually within 5 years."
- **BP:** antibodies bind "BPAg1 [BP230], BPAg2 [BP180]". "Direct immunofluorescence shows linear IgG and complement deposits along the basement membrane zone."
- **BP and the mouth:** "Mucous membrane involvement is rare". Her notes mention oral and eye involvement (p. 65).
- **BP drug triggers:** MSD lists **dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors** (diabetes drugs such as sitagliptin; the example is standard teaching), furosemide, spironolactone, PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies and penicillin.
- **BP treatment:** high-potency topical clobetasol for localized disease, prednisone for widespread disease, or tetracycline or minocycline with nicotinamide.

**Recent change (rituximab first-line for pemphigus):** her notes list systemic steroids and immunosuppressants (p. 67). MSD now says that for mild disease "first-line treatment is typically rituximab" or prednisone, and that moderate to severe disease is treated with rituximab plus higher-dose prednisone. Rituximab is an anti-CD20 antibody that removes the B cells making the antibodies (standard teaching).

**Recent change (dupilumab for pemphigoid):** the FDA approved **dupilumab (Dupixent)** "for the treatment of adult patients with bullous pemphigoid" on **20 Jun 2025** ([Drugs.com approval history](https://www.drugs.com/history/dupixent.html)). In the ADEPT trial, "18.3 percent of patients treated with Dupixent experienced sustained disease remission" at 36 weeks vs 6.1% with placebo ([Drugs.com news, 24 Jun 2025](https://www.drugs.com/news/fda-approves-dupixent-bullous-pemphigoid-125520.html)). MSD lists rituximab, dupilumab, omalizumab and IVIG among the biologic options for BP.

### Epidermolysis bullosa (pp. 65-66)
- An **inherited** group of diseases in which **minor rubbing causes blisters**, because a **structural protein of the skin is faulty**: keratin, desmosome or junction proteins (p. 65).
- Nails are often lost; the mouth can be involved; scarring can cause contractures (pp. 65-66).
- Treatment is supportive wound care and infection prevention (p. 66).

**Other source (EB types):** MSD sorts EB by where the skin splits ([MSD: Epidermolysis Bullosa, updated Sept 2024](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/bullous-diseases/epidermolysis-bullosa)).
- **Simplex:** split within the epidermis; **KRT5/KRT14** (keratins 5 and 14); mainly autosomal dominant.
- **Junctional:** split in the lamina lucida; laminin and collagen XVII genes; autosomal recessive.
- **Dystrophic:** split below the lamina densa; **COL7A1 (type VII collagen)**; dominant or recessive.
- **Kindler syndrome:** FERMT1.

**Recent change (gene therapy for dystrophic EB):** on 19 May 2023 the FDA approved **Vyjuvek**, the first topical gene therapy, "for the treatment of wounds in patients 6 months of age and older" with dystrophic EB from COL7A1 mutations. It uses a "herpes-simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) vector" to deliver working COL7A1, which makes type VII collagen, and is applied to wounds once a week ([FDA press release, 19 May 2023](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-topical-gene-therapy-treatment-wounds-patients-dystrophic-epidermolysis-bullosa)). A label update on 15 Sep 2025 extended it to patients "from birth" and allows patients and caregivers "to apply beremagene geperpavec-svdt at home on their own" ([HCPLive, Sept 2025](https://www.hcplive.com/view/fda-approves-beremagene-geperpavec-svdt-vyjuvek-label-update-in-deb)). MSD's EB page still describes treatment as supportive.

## Clinical vignette
A 26-year-old woman started lamotrigine for bipolar disorder 2 weeks ago. She has had fever and a sore throat for 2 days, and now has burning eyes, painful crusted erosions on her lips and mouth, and dusky purple spots on her chest that blister. Light pressure next to a blister makes the top layer slide off (**positive Nikolsky sign**). Detached skin covers about 8% of her body: **Stevens-Johnson syndrome** (< 10%; p. 19). The first step is to **stop lamotrigine**. She needs burn-unit or ICU care, eye specialist care, fluids and wound care (p. 19; MSD). Her targets are on the trunk with mucosal erosions after a new drug, which separates this from HSV-linked erythema multiforme (MSD).

## Common traps
- **Eczema in infants is on the cheeks and extensor surfaces; in older children it moves into the flexures.**
- **Sudden punched-out painful vesicles in eczema = eczema herpeticum (HSV)**, not just impetigo.
- **Allergic contact dermatitis is type IV (T cells), not type I (IgE)**, and appears a day or more after contact.
- **EM = HSV + targets on hands and feet; SJS/TEN = drug + mucosa + skin peeling.** EM is no longer the mild end of SJS.
- **SJS < 10%, TEN > 30%.** Children: think *Mycoplasma*. Carbamazepine in Asian patients: **HLA-B\*15:02**. Allopurinol: **HLA-B\*58:01**.
- **Psoriasis is worsened by lithium, beta-blockers and antimalarials, and by stopping systemic steroids**; MSD says not to treat psoriasis with systemic steroids.
- **Guttate psoriasis follows strep throat.**
- **Lichen planus → check for hepatitis C.**
- **Pityriasis rosea with palm or sole lesions → test for syphilis.**
- **PV = desmoglein, inside the epidermis, Nikolsky positive, mouth first. BP = hemidesmosome, below the epidermis, tense, Nikolsky negative, older adults.**
- **Telogen effluvium appears months after the stressor**, not the next day.

## Practice questions

1. A 7-month-old boy has itchy, red, weeping, crusted plaques on both cheeks and on the outer surfaces of his arms and legs. His mother has asthma and his father has hay fever. A mutation that reduces which skin protein most likely contributes to his condition?
   - **Answer:** **filaggrin**.
   - **Why:** this is atopic dermatitis in the infant pattern (cheeks and extensor surfaces). Filaggrin loss weakens the skin barrier so antigens get in and drive Th2 inflammation (pp. 10-11; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **desmoglein 3**, the target of autoantibodies in pemphigus vulgaris.

2. A 9-year-old girl with long-standing atopic dermatitis develops fever and many painful, clustered, punched-out erosions and small vesicles over her eczema patches on the neck and face over 2 days. Which organism is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** **herpes simplex virus**.
   - **Why:** this is eczema herpeticum, HSV spreading rapidly over eczematous skin (p. 11; MSD).
   - **Trap:** ***Staphylococcus aureus***, which causes honey-crusted impetigo on eczema.

3. A 34-year-old woman has an itchy, red, vesicular rash in a band around her left wrist. It began 2 days after she started wearing a new metal bracelet. Which type of immune reaction is responsible?
   - **Answer:** **type IV (delayed, T-cell-mediated) hypersensitivity**.
   - **Why:** this is allergic contact dermatitis to nickel. Previously sensitized memory T cells respond to the hapten-protein antigen a day or more after contact (pp. 12-13; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **type I (IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity**, which causes hives within minutes.

4. A 30-year-old man started allopurinol 3 weeks ago. He now has fever, painful erosions of the lips, mouth and genitals, and dusky red skin that peels off in large sheets over 40% of his body. A skin biopsy shows full-thickness epidermal necrosis separated from the dermis. Which cell type directly kills his keratinocytes?
   - **Answer:** **cytotoxic CD8+ T cells**.
   - **Why:** this is toxic epidermal necrolysis (> 30% detachment). Cytotoxic T cells destroy keratinocytes that display the drug antigen (p. 18). Allopurinol is a classic trigger, linked to HLA-B\*58:01 (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **IgG autoantibodies against desmoglein**, the mechanism of pemphigus vulgaris, which splits inside the epidermis rather than killing it.

5. A 38-year-old man of Han Chinese ancestry has trigeminal neuralgia. His physician plans to start carbamazepine. Which test should be done before the first dose to lower his risk of a severe blistering skin reaction?
   - **Answer:** **HLA-B\*15:02 genotyping**.
   - **Why:** HLA-B\*15:02 strongly predicts carbamazepine-induced SJS/TEN; MSD says testing may be considered "particularly in certain Northern European or Asian populations" (p. 18; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **HLA-B\*58:01 genotyping**, which predicts **allopurinol** reactions.

6. A 23-year-old woman has had four episodes this year of round lesions on her palms and the backs of her hands. Each lesion has a dark center, a pale ring and a red rim. Each episode began a few days after a cold sore on her lip, and each cleared within 2 weeks. Her mouth and eyes have not been involved. Which treatment is most likely to prevent further episodes?
   - **Answer:** **daily oral antiviral therapy (for example valacyclovir) for about 6 months**.
   - **Why:** this is recurrent HSV-associated erythema multiforme; continuous antivirals prevent the triggering outbreaks (pp. 16-18; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **a long course of oral prednisone**, which does not prevent HSV reactivation and is not the recommended prevention.

7. A 19-year-old college student first noticed a single 4-cm oval, pink, scaly patch on her chest. Ten days later, dozens of smaller oval, scaly patches appeared on her back along the skin lines. Her palms and soles are clear, and she is not pregnant. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **reassurance; it usually clears by itself within weeks**.
   - **Why:** a herald patch followed by a Christmas-tree eruption is pityriasis rosea, which is self-limited (pp. 34-35; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **oral terbinafine**, for tinea corporis, which the herald patch can mimic. The many oval patches along skin lines point to pityriasis rosea instead.

8. A 47-year-old man has well-demarcated salmon-pink plaques with silvery scale on his elbows, knees and lower back, and pits in several fingernails. His rash flared after he started lithium. His dermatologist starts secukinumab. This drug neutralizes a cytokine made mainly by which T-cell subset?
   - **Answer:** **Th17 cells** (secukinumab blocks IL-17A).
   - **Why:** psoriasis is driven by Th17 and Th1 cells releasing IL-17, IL-22 and TNF (p. 35); MSD lists secukinumab among the IL-17 blockers. The IL-17A target and Th17 source are standard teaching.
   - **Trap:** **Th2 cells**, which make IL-4 and IL-13, the targets of dupilumab in eczema.

9. A 52-year-old woman has very itchy, flat-topped, purple, polygonal papules on the insides of her wrists and on her shins. There are lacy white lines on her inner cheeks. New papules have appeared along a scratch on her forearm. Screening for which infection is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **hepatitis C**.
   - **Why:** this is lichen planus (Ps, Wickham striae, Koebner phenomenon; pp. 32-33). MSD links oral lichen planus with hepatitis C.
   - **Trap:** **HIV**, which is linked to severe **seborrheic dermatitis**.

10. A 78-year-old man with type 2 diabetes treated with sitagliptin has had itchy, red, raised plaques on his trunk and thighs for a month. Now large, tense, fluid-filled blisters have formed on them. His mouth is clear and the Nikolsky sign is negative. A perilesional biopsy with direct immunofluorescence would most likely show which pattern?
    - **Answer:** **linear IgG and complement along the basement membrane zone**.
    - **Why:** this is bullous pemphigoid, with antibodies against hemidesmosome proteins (BP180, BP230). MSD lists DPP-4 inhibitors as a trigger (pp. 64-65; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **IgG between keratinocytes in a net-like pattern**, the pattern of pemphigus vulgaris.

11. A 44-year-old woman has had painful mouth sores for 2 months. She now has flaccid blisters on her chest and scalp that break easily and leave raw, painful erosions. Rubbing normal-looking skin next to a blister makes the top layer slide off. Biopsy shows a split just above the basal cell layer. Her autoantibodies most likely target which protein?
    - **Answer:** **desmoglein 3**.
    - **Why:** this is pemphigus vulgaris: oral lesions first, flaccid blisters, positive Nikolsky sign, intraepidermal acantholysis. MSD: antibodies against "desmoglein 3 and sometimes desmoglein 1" (p. 66).
    - **Trap:** **BP180 (collagen XVII)**, the hemidesmosome target in bullous pemphigoid.

12. A 31-year-old woman notices large amounts of hair in her brush and shower drain. Her scalp hair is thinning evenly all over without bald patches or scarring. Three months ago she was in the ICU for 10 days with septic shock. A gentle pull on a group of hairs removes many hairs with white club-shaped roots. These hairs were in which phase of the hair cycle?
    - **Answer:** **the resting (telogen) phase**.
    - **Why:** this is telogen effluvium. A major stressor pushes many hairs into telogen at once, and they shed about 3-4 months later (p. 22; MSD). The club-shaped root of a telogen hair is standard teaching.
    - **Trap:** **the growth (anagen) phase**, which is shed within days to weeks in anagen effluvium from chemotherapy.
