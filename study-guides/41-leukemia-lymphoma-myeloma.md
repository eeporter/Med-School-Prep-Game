# Blood cancers: leukemias, myeloproliferative neoplasms, lymphomas and plasma cell disorders

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials for USMLE Step 1, Hematology & Oncology (acute leukemia pp. 62-64, chronic leukemia pp. 65-66, myeloproliferative disorders pp. 68-70, lymph nodes and lymphoma pp. 71-77, plasma cell disorders pp. 78-79, histiocytic disorders p. 80, chronic venous insufficiency pp. 81-82, massive transfusion p. 83; p. 67 is a diagram page with no text). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Sort every blood cancer by **what the cell looks like** and **where it lives**. **Blasts** (immature cells) piling up in the marrow = **acute leukemia**. **Mature** cells in the blood = **chronic leukemia** or a **myeloproliferative neoplasm**. A **mass in a lymph node or tissue** = **lymphoma**. **Plasma cells** making one antibody = **myeloma** and its relatives. Then match the **marker** (CD number, stain) and the **translocation**.

## Acute leukemia (pp. 62-64)
- **Definition in your notes:** more than **20% blasts** in the marrow (p. 62).
- Blasts **crowd out** normal cells, so the patient arrives quickly with the three marrow-failure signs: **anemia** (tiredness), **low platelets** (bleeding) and **low neutrophils** (infection) (p. 62).
- Blasts usually spill into the blood (high WBC, white blood cell count) and can infiltrate liver, spleen, nodes and skin (**leukemia cutis**) (p. 62).

**Recent change (blast cutoff):** MSD's current AML (acute myeloid leukemia) workup starts at "≥ 10% myeloid blasts or blast equivalents in the bone marrow or peripheral blood". Several genetically defined AML types now need only **10%** blasts, while AML with *BCR::ABL1* still needs **20%**, and some types with 10-19% blasts are called **MDS/AML** ([MSD: Acute Myeloid Leukemia, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/leukemias/acute-myeloid-leukemia-aml)). For a Step 1 question, "more than 20% blasts" is still the safe number for acute leukemia; know that a defining gene change can lower it.

### ALL vs AML at a glance
| | **ALL** (acute lymphoblastic leukemia) | **AML** (acute myeloid leukemia) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical patient | **Child** (most common childhood cancer) | **Older adult** |
| Marker | **TdT+** (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase, a DNA polymerase in the nucleus) | **MPO+** (myeloperoxidase); MPO crystals = **Auer rods** |
| Down syndrome link | Usually **after age 5** | Acute megakaryoblastic leukemia, usually **before age 5** |
| Pages | 63 | 63-64 |

### ALL (p. 63)
- **TdT** is in lymphoblasts but **not** in myeloid blasts or mature lymphocytes (p. 63).
- **B-ALL** (most ALL; your notes give **70-80%**) blasts express **CD10, CD19 and CD20**. They respond well to chemotherapy but need treatment aimed at the **CSF (cerebrospinal fluid) and testes**, where cells hide (p. 63).
- **Prognosis by translocation:** **t(12;21)** = **good**, more common in children; **t(9;22)** (Philadelphia chromosome) = **poor**, more common in adults (p. 63).
- **T-ALL** blasts carry T-cell markers (CD2 to CD8, such as CD3, CD4, CD7) but **not CD10**. The classic patient is a **teenager with a mediastinal (thymic) mass** that can press on the **superior vena cava** (face and arm swelling), **esophagus** (trouble swallowing) or **trachea** (stridor) (p. 63).

**Other source (ALL details):** MSD: ALL "is the most common pediatric cancer"; diagnosis needs lymphoid blasts "≥ 20% of marrow nucleated cells"; favorable features include **high hyperdiploidy (51 to 65 chromosomes), t(1;19) and t(12;21)**; Ph+ t(9;22) is unfavorable; "CNS prophylaxis starts during induction and continues throughout all phases of treatment" ([MSD: Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/leukemias/acute-lymphoblastic-leukemia-all)).

**Recent change (ALL treatment):** for **Ph+ ALL**, MSD adds a **tyrosine kinase inhibitor** (imatinib, dasatinib). **Blinatumomab** (an antibody that links CD19 on B cells to CD3 on T cells; mechanism is standard teaching) "prolongs overall survival in children and adults with relapsed or refractory B-cell precursor ALL", and **CAR-T cells** (the patient's own T cells engineered to attack the leukemia) "induce remission in patients with relapsed ALL with remarkable efficacy, albeit with significant toxicity" (same MSD page).

### AML (pp. 63-64)
- Your notes give an average age of **50-60** (p. 63).
- **APL (acute promyelocytic leukemia, FAB M3):** **t(15;17)** moves the **retinoic acid receptor (RAR)** gene from chromosome 17 to chromosome 15. The broken receptor **blocks maturation**, so promyelocytes pile up. Their many granules (Auer rods) raise the risk of **DIC** (disseminated intravascular coagulation). Treatment in your notes is **ATRA** (all-trans retinoic acid, a vitamin A derivative), which binds the abnormal receptor and makes the blasts mature and die (pp. 63-64).
- **Acute monocytic leukemia:** monoblasts, usually **MPO negative**, **infiltrate the gums** (p. 64).
- **Acute megakaryoblastic leukemia:** MPO negative; **Down syndrome before age 5** (p. 64).

**Other source (AML age and Auer rods):** MSD gives a "median age of onset of 68 years" for AML (older than your notes' 50-60) and a median age of **31** for APL, so **APL is the AML of younger adults**. MSD calls Auer rods "pathognomic for AML" ([MSD: AML, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/leukemias/acute-myeloid-leukemia-aml)).

**Recent change (APL treatment):** MSD now describes **tretinoin (ATRA) plus arsenic trioxide, without conventional chemotherapy**, as the standard of care for low- and intermediate-risk APL, with a 100% complete remission rate and over 90% cure; ATRA "corrects the DIC in 2 to 5 days" (same MSD page). Your notes' ATRA mechanism is unchanged; arsenic is now its partner.

### Myelodysplastic syndrome (p. 64)
- **Low blood counts** with a **busy (hypercellular) marrow**, **abnormal-looking maturing cells** and blasts **under 20%** (p. 64).
- Can turn into AML, especially after **alkylating agents or radiation**. Most patients die of **infection or bleeding** (p. 64).
- **Other source:** MSD lists prior **alkylating agents, hydroxyurea or topoisomerase II inhibitors** as causes of therapy-related MDS/AML, and a "preceding myelodysplastic phase" as a bad sign in AML (MSD AML page).

## Chronic leukemia (pp. 65-66)
Mature-looking cells, a **high WBC count**, a **slow start** and an **older patient** (p. 65).

### CLL / SLL: chronic lymphocytic leukemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma (p. 65)
- **Naive B cells** that carry **CD5 and CD20** together. CD5 is normally a T-cell marker, so a B cell with CD5 is the clue (standard teaching). Your notes call CLL the **most common leukemia overall** (p. 65).
- Patient: **over 60**, often no symptoms, slow course. Blood smear: many lymphocytes and **smudge cells** ("**C**rushed **L**ittle **L**ymphocytes") (p. 65).
- Same disease in lymph nodes = **SLL** (generalized lymph node swelling) (p. 65).
- **Complications** (p. 65):
  1. **Low antibodies (hypogammaglobulinemia)**: **infection is the most common cause of death**.
  2. **Autoimmune hemolytic anemia** (warm, IgG; see guide 40).
  3. **Richter transformation** to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma: a node or spleen that suddenly **grows**.

**Other source (CLL details):** MSD: CLL is "the most common type of leukemia in the Western world"; the cells "should express CD5, CD19, CD20, CD23"; hypogammaglobulinemia develops "in up to two-thirds of patients"; patients are also prone to **immune thrombocytopenia**; about **2-10%** develop Richter syndrome ([MSD: CLL, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/leukemias/chronic-lymphocytic-leukemia-cll)). MSD's 2025 US numbers make CLL the largest single leukemia in adults (**35%** of new cases, vs AML 33%) ([MSD: Overview of Leukemia, updated Feb 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/leukemias/overview-of-leukemia)). Memory hook (standard teaching): **CD5 + CD23 = CLL**; CD5 without CD23 points to mantle cell lymphoma.

**Recent change (CLL treatment):** MSD: early CLL is watched, "Patients are observed until symptoms develop". Treatment now centers on **BTK (Bruton tyrosine kinase) inhibitors** (ibrutinib; acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, which have fewer off-target effects) and **venetoclax** (a BCL2 inhibitor; mechanism is standard teaching) given "in combination with obinutuzumab" for a fixed 12 months (same MSD CLL page).

### Hairy cell leukemia (pp. 65-66)
- Mature B cells with **hair-like projections**; positive for **TRAP** (tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase; "trapped in a hairy situation") (p. 65).
- **Big spleen** (hairy cells fill the **red pulp**), **dry tap** on marrow aspiration (marrow fibrosis), and **no lymph node swelling** (pp. 65-66).
- Treatment: **cladribine (2-CdA)**, a purine (adenosine) analog (pp. 66, 104).

**Correction (how cladribine works):** p. 66 calls cladribine "an adenosine deaminase inhibitor" that lets adenosine build up. The FDA label says the opposite: cladribine "is resistant to deamination by adenosine deaminase". Cells rich in **deoxycytidine kinase** (such as lymphocytes) phosphorylate it, and its active triphosphate (**2-CdATP**) builds up inside them; the label lists hairy cell leukemia as its indication ([Cladribine Injection US prescribing information, Pfizer, rev. Oct 2011](https://cdn.pfizer.com/pfizercom/products/uspi_cladribine.pdf)). So the drug itself is the toxic adenosine look-alike, and adenosine deaminase cannot break it down. The adenosine deaminase **inhibitor** used in hairy cell leukemia is **pentostatin** (standard teaching). Your notes' drug page (p. 104) correctly calls cladribine a purine analog.
- MSD lists hairy cell leukemia only among "other leukemias" and does not cover these details; they come from your notes.

### CML: chronic myeloid leukemia (p. 66)
- Too many **mature granulocytes and their precursors**, with **high basophils** (p. 66).
- **t(9;22)**, the **Philadelphia chromosome**, makes the **BCR-ABL** fusion protein, a tyrosine kinase that is always on. First-line drug: **imatinib**, which blocks that kinase (p. 66).
- Age: peak **45-85**, median **64** in your notes (p. 66). Big spleen is common; a **growing spleen** means the **accelerated phase**, and **blast crisis** usually follows (p. 66).
- Because the mutation sits in a **pluripotent stem cell**, blast crisis can be **AML (about 2/3)** or **ALL (about 1/3)** (p. 66).
- **CML vs leukemoid reaction** (a big but harmless rise in neutrophils, for example in infection) (p. 66):

| | **CML** | **Leukemoid reaction** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LAP (leukocyte alkaline phosphatase) stain | **Low / negative** | **High / positive** |
| Basophils | **Increased** | Not increased |
| t(9;22) | **Present** | Absent |
| Smear clue | Immature granulocytes | **Döhle bodies** (light-blue patches of rough ER in neutrophils) |

**Other source (CML numbers):** MSD gives an average age of **66**, splenomegaly in **60-70%**, and a blast phase of "myeloblasts (60% of patients), lymphoblasts (30%)" and megakaryoblasts (10%), close to your notes' 2/3 vs 1/3 ([MSD: CML, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/leukemias/chronic-myeloid-leukemia-cml)). MSD does not mention the LAP stain; the table above follows your notes.

**Recent change (CML treatment):** imatinib now shares first line with other **tyrosine kinase inhibitors** ("imatinib, nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib, ponatinib"), which "are not curative but are extremely effective". **Asciminib** is approved after 2 or more prior TKIs and for new chronic-phase CML with the **T315I** mutation, and "Some patients may be able to discontinue tyrosine kinase inhibitors and remain in remission" (same MSD page).

## Myeloproliferative neoplasms (pp. 68-70)
- Too many **mature myeloid cells**; disease of **late adulthood** (average **50-60** in your notes). High WBC count and a busy marrow; all myeloid lines rise, and each disease is named for the **dominant** cell (p. 68).
- Most carry a **JAK2** mutation (a non-receptor tyrosine kinase); **CML is the exception** (BCR-ABL) (p. 68).
- Shared complications: **gout** (high cell turnover makes uric acid), **marrow fibrosis**, and **turning into acute leukemia** (p. 68).

### Polycythemia vera (pp. 68-69)
- Mostly **red cells**, but granulocytes and platelets also rise. JAK2 is always switched on, so cells over-respond to **EPO (erythropoietin)** and thrombopoietin (p. 68).
- Symptoms come from **thick (hyperviscous) blood** (p. 68):
  - **Blurry vision and headache**.
  - Clots in odd veins: **hepatic vein (Budd-Chiari), portal vein, dural sinus**.
  - **Erythromelalgia**: burning, red-blue fingers and toes from small clots; **responds to aspirin**.
  - **Red face (plethora)** and **itching after a bath** (histamine from extra mast cells).
  - Mild high blood pressure from the larger blood volume.
- Treatment in your notes: **phlebotomy**; second line **hydroxyurea** or **ruxolitinib** (a JAK1/2 inhibitor). Your notes say untreated patients usually die **within one year** (p. 69).
- **PV vs reactive polycythemia** (p. 69):

| | **EPO** | **SaO2** (arterial oxygen saturation) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Polycythemia vera** | **Low** | Normal |
| High altitude, lung disease | High | **Low** |
| EPO-making tumor (renal cell carcinoma) | High | Normal |

**Other source (PV details):** MSD: the **JAK2 V617F** mutation (exon 14) "is present in 90% of patients" and **exon 12** mutations in **5%**; EPO is "low or low-normal"; itching is typical "after a hot bath or shower (aquagenic pruritus)". Phlebotomy targets "a hematocrit < 45% in men and < 42% in women", with "aspirin 81 to 100 mg orally once a day unless contraindicated". MSD gives **no** untreated survival figure; it names **thrombosis** as "the most common cause of morbidity and death" ([MSD: Polycythemia Vera, updated Sept 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology/myeloproliferative-disorders/polycythemia-vera)).

**Recent change (PV drugs):** MSD now lists the interferons, "peginterferon alfa-2b, peginterferon alfa-2a, and ropeginterferon alfa-2b", as preferred cell-lowering options. It warns that hydroxyurea "can increase the incidence of acute leukemia and solid tumors" and "should be prescribed only by specialists familiar with its use". Ruxolitinib is still used (same MSD page).

### Essential thrombocythemia (p. 70)
- Mostly **platelets**; your notes say RBCs and granulocytes also rise. **JAK2** in **30-50%** (p. 70).
- Risk of **both bleeding and clotting**; rarely goes on to fibrosis or leukemia; **no** real gout risk (p. 70).

**Other source (ET blood counts):** MSD describes ET as an **isolated** platelet count over **450,000/mcL** with a **normal hematocrit** and **normal WBC count**; JAK2 "is present in approximately 50% of patients"; leukemic change "occurs in < 2% of patients"; and first-line treatment is "Aspirin for most patients" ([MSD: Essential Thrombocythemia, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/myeloproliferative-disorders/essential-thrombocythemia)). On an exam, a **very high platelet count with otherwise normal counts** points to ET.

### Primary myelofibrosis (p. 70)
- Mostly **megakaryocytes**, which release **PDGF** (platelet-derived growth factor) and scar the marrow. **JAK2** in **30-50%** in your notes (p. 70).
- Signs: **big spleen** (blood is made in the spleen instead: extramedullary hematopoiesis), **teardrop red cells** ("the marrow is crying"), **dry tap**, and more infection, clotting and bleeding (p. 70).

**Other source (myelofibrosis):** MSD says JAK2 is present "in a high proportion of cases" without a number, describes the scarring as neoplastic cells driving normal fibroblasts "to produce excessive collagen", calls teardrop cells (dacryocytes) "a characteristic morphologic feature", and notes nucleated red cells and young white cells in the blood (**leukoerythroblastosis**) ([MSD: Primary Myelofibrosis, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology/myeloproliferative-disorders/primary-myelofibrosis-pmf)).

**Recent change (myelofibrosis drugs):** MSD lists "Sometimes ruxolitinib, fedratinib, pacritinib, or momelotinib" (JAK inhibitors) and says "Only allogeneic stem cell transplantation is curative" (same MSD page).

## Lymph nodes and lymphoma (pp. 71-77)

### Swollen lymph nodes (p. 71)
- **Painful** node = draining an **acute infection**. **Painless** node = chronic inflammation, **metastatic cancer** or **lymphoma** (p. 71).
- Reactive patterns: **follicles (B-cell zone)** grow in **rheumatoid arthritis**; the **paracortex (T-cell zone)** grows in **viral infection** such as infectious mononucleosis (p. 71).
- **Polyclonal** growth = reactive (benign); **monoclonal** growth = strong evidence of cancer (p. 77).

### Hodgkin vs non-Hodgkin (p. 71)
| | **Hodgkin lymphoma** | **Non-Hodgkin lymphoma** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spread | **One group of nodes**, spreads **to the next group** in order (contiguous); stage best predicts outcome | **Many nodes**, often **outside nodes**; skips groups |
| Hallmark cell | **Reed-Sternberg cell** | Mostly **B cells**; a few T cells |
| Age | Two peaks: young adults and older adults | Children and adults |
| Links | **EBV** (Epstein-Barr virus) | Autoimmune disease; HIV, EBV, HTLV |
| Outlook | Better | Worse |
| Both | "**B symptoms**": fever, night sweats, weight loss | |

**Correction (how common each is):** p. 71 splits lymphomas into "NHL, 60%" and "HL, 40%". MSD's numbers are very different: **over 80,000** new non-Hodgkin cases a year in the US and "Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is more common than Hodgkin lymphoma" ([MSD: Non-Hodgkin Lymphomas, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/lymphomas/non-hodgkin-lymphomas)), against about **8,830** Hodgkin cases (2023 estimate) ([MSD: Hodgkin Lymphoma, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/lymphomas/hodgkin-lymphoma)). Arithmetic: 8,830 ÷ (80,000 + 8,830) ≈ 0.10, so Hodgkin is about **1 in 10** lymphomas and non-Hodgkin about **9 in 10**. The two MSD figures come from different years, so treat the ratio as approximate.

**Other source (Hodgkin age):** MSD: Hodgkin is "most common between ages 15 and 40 years; a second peak occurs in people > 60 years" (your notes say over 55) (MSD Hodgkin page).

### Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: sorted by cell size (p. 72)
- **Small B cells:** follicular, mantle cell, marginal zone, small lymphocytic (CLL in tissue).
- **Medium B cells:** Burkitt.
- **Large B cells:** diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

| Lymphoma | Translocation / gene | Key facts | Pages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Follicular** | **t(14;18)**: **BCL2** moves next to the Ig heavy chain gene → too much Bcl2 → cells **cannot die (no apoptosis)** | Older adult, painless nodes all over, **waxes and wanes**; treat only if symptoms (low-dose chemo or **rituximab**, an anti-CD20 antibody); can turn into **diffuse large B-cell** (a node that grows fast) | 72 |
| **Mantle cell** | **t(11;14)**: **cyclin D1** → pushes cells through the **G1/S checkpoint** | Older adult, painless nodes; expands the mantle zone | 73 |
| **Marginal zone / MALT** | **t(11;18)** | Grows from **chronic inflammation**: **Hashimoto thyroiditis, Sjögren syndrome, *H. pylori* gastritis**; gastric MALToma **may shrink after *H. pylori* is treated** | 73 |
| **Burkitt** | **t(8;14)**: **c-MYC** → nonstop growth | Child or young adult; **endemic (African) = jaw**, **sporadic = abdomen**; endemic form almost always **EBV**; **"starry sky"** (macrophages eating dead cells) | 73 |
| **Diffuse large B-cell** | BCL2, BCL6 changes | **Most common NHL**; aggressive; new or from a low-grade lymphoma; older adult with a growing node or mass | 74 |

Your notes spell these "Patitrier microabscesses" and "HTLV-L" (p. 74); the usual spellings are **Pautrier** and **HTLV-1** (standard teaching).

**Follicular lymphoma vs reactive follicles** (p. 72): lymphoma **destroys the node's normal layout**, shows **Bcl2 in the follicles**, and is **monoclonal**; reactive follicles keep the layout, lack Bcl2 and are polyclonal.

**Other source (Burkitt and brain lymphoma):** MSD: Burkitt is "closely associated with Epstein-Barr virus infection in endemic lymphoma"; in sporadic cases "abdominal disease predominates", often near the ileocecal valve; and "With treatment, tumor lysis syndrome is common" ([MSD: Burkitt Lymphoma, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/lymphomas/burkitt-lymphoma)). Brain lymphoma in HIV is "frequently associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection", happens at a "very low CD4 count", and shows "ring-enhancing lesions" on imaging ([MSD: Cancers Common in HIV](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/human-immunodeficiency-virus-hiv-infection/cancers-common-among-patients-with-human-immunodeficiency-virus-hiv-infection)). MSD also says "*Helicobacter pylori* infection also increases lymphoma risk" (MSD NHL page). The MSD NHL page does not rank subtypes; "most common NHL" for diffuse large B-cell comes from your notes.

### Hodgkin lymphoma (pp. 76-77)
- **Reed-Sternberg cells**: large B cells with two mirror-image nuclei and big nucleoli (**"owl eyes"**), **CD15+ and CD30+** (p. 76).
- RS cells release **cytokines** that cause B symptoms, draw in reactive cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, **eosinophils**) and can cause fibrosis. Those **reactive cells make up most of the tumor** and define the subtypes (p. 76).

| Subtype | Key fact (p. 76) |
| --- | --- |
| **Nodular sclerosis** | **Most common (70%)**; **young woman** with a growing **neck or mediastinal node**; collagen bands; RS cells sit in clear spaces (**lacunar cells**) |
| **Lymphocyte-rich** | **Best** outlook |
| **Mixed cellularity** | Many **eosinophils** (RS cells make **IL-5**) |
| **Lymphocyte-depleted** | **Worst** outlook; elderly and **HIV** |

**Other source (Hodgkin markers and cure):** MSD's table lists classic Hodgkin as "CD15+, CD30+, CD20–"; nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin is the opposite (CD15–, CD30–, CD20+). Other clues: itching ("Intense pruritus ... may occur early") and, rarely, node pain right after drinking alcohol. About **85-90%** of limited-stage and **75-80%** of advanced-stage classic Hodgkin is cured (MSD Hodgkin page). MSD does not give subtype percentages, so the 70% figure is from your notes.

**EBV** raises the risk of **Hodgkin**, **non-Hodgkin** lymphoma and **nasopharyngeal carcinoma** (p. 77).

## Plasma cell disorders (pp. 78-79)

### Multiple myeloma (p. 78)
- Cancerous **plasma cells in the marrow**. Your notes call it the **most common primary bone cancer**, though **metastases** are the most common cancer found in bone (p. 78).
- **IL-6** helps plasma cells grow (p. 78).
- **CRAB** (p. 78):
  - **C**alcium high: myeloma cells activate **RANK** on osteoclasts → bone is eaten → **punched-out lytic lesions** (spine, skull), back pain, fractures.
  - **R**enal failure: free **light chains** leak into urine (**Bence Jones protein**) and clog tubules (**myeloma kidney**).
  - **A**nemia: normocytic.
  - **B**one lesions / back pain.
- Other clues: an **M spike** on **SPEP** (serum protein electrophoresis), usually **IgG or IgA**; **rouleaux** (red cells stacked like poker chips, because extra protein cancels the cells' negative charge); plasma cells with **"clock-face" chromatin**; **AL amyloidosis** from light chains; and **infection as the most common cause of death**, because the one antibody made has no variety (p. 78).

**Other source (myeloma diagnosis and types):** MSD diagnoses myeloma when "≥ 10%" of marrow cells are clonal plasma cells, with an M-protein and organ damage (high calcium, kidney failure, anemia or bone lesions). The M-protein is "IgG in about 50 to 55%" and "IgA in about 20%"; **15-20%** of patients make **only light chains** (Bence Jones protein), so the serum M spike can be small or absent ([MSD: Multiple Myeloma, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/plasma-cell-disorders/multiple-myeloma)). MSD does not use the word "CRAB"; it is the usual memory aid.

**Recent change (myeloma drugs):** besides **proteasome inhibitors** (bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib) and **immunomodulatory drugs** (lenalidomide, thalidomide, pomalidomide), MSD lists **anti-CD38 antibodies** (daratumumab, isatuximab), **CAR-T cells** (idecabtagene vicleucel, ciltacabtagene autoleucel) and **bispecific antibodies** (teclistamab, elranatamab, talquetamab), which MSD groups under "Antibody- and T-cell-based approaches to targeting of B-cell maturation antigen", plus autologous stem cell transplant for suitable patients (same MSD page).

### MGUS: monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (p. 79)
- An **M spike** with **none** of myeloma's damage: no lytic lesions, high calcium, amyloid or Bence Jones proteinuria (p. 79).
- Common in older people (**5% of 70-year-olds**); **1% per year** turn into myeloma (p. 79).
- **Other source:** MSD: from "1% of people aged 25 years to > 5% of people > 70 years"; "up to 25% (1%/year)" progress to myeloma or a related disorder; serum M-protein usually "< 3 g/dL"; check-ups "Every 6 to 12 months" ([MSD: MGUS, updated Aug 2024](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/plasma-cell-disorders/monoclonal-gammopathy-of-undetermined-significance-mgus)).

### Waldenström macroglobulinemia (p. 79)
- B-cell lymphoma making **IgM**. Nodes all over, **no lytic bone lesions**, M spike made of IgM (p. 79).
- IgM is a big **pentamer**, so the blood gets **thick**: **vision loss, retinal bleeding, stroke**; and **bleeding**, because thick serum stops platelets clumping (p. 79).
- Acute problems: **plasmapheresis** to pull IgM out (p. 79).

**Other source (Waldenström):** MSD: retinal veins that look like "sausage links" suggest hyperviscosity; treatment is "plasma exchange, which rapidly reverses bleeding as well as neurologic abnormalities" ([MSD: Macroglobulinemia, updated Apr 2025](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/plasma-cell-disorders/macroglobulinemia)).

**Recent change (Waldenström drugs):** MSD: "BTK inhibitors, including ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, and zanubrutinib, have shown high levels of activity", and rituximab can briefly **raise** IgM, sometimes needing plasma exchange (same MSD page).

## Histiocytic disorders (p. 80)

### Langerhans cell histiocytosis
- Abnormal **dendritic (Langerhans) cells** that cannot present antigen well (p. 80).
- Child with **lytic bone lesions and a skin rash**, or **recurrent ear infections** with a **mastoid mass** (p. 80).
- Markers: **S-100** and **CD1a**; **Birbeck granules** ("tennis rackets" or rods on electron microscopy) (p. 80).

**Other source (LCH):** MSD: an "oncogene-driven cancer of myeloid lineage"; **BRAF V600E** in "approximately two-thirds of patients"; markers "CD1a, CD207 (langerin), and S-100"; a "scaly seborrheic, eczematoid, sometimes purpuric rash" on the scalp, ear canals and skin folds; and **central diabetes insipidus** (low ADH, antidiuretic hormone), part of the classic triad with flat bone lesions and **proptosis**, which "affects 5 to 50% of patients", more often in children with skull and orbit disease ([MSD: Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis, updated Apr 2026](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/histiocytic-syndromes/langerhans-cell-histiocytosis)). Your notes add "(mesodermal origin)" after S-100; MSD instead calls the cells myeloid-derived, so remember the **markers**, not the origin label.

### HLH: hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis
- Macrophages and cytotoxic T cells are **overactive everywhere** → **fever, low blood counts, big liver and spleen** and a **very high ferritin** (p. 80).
- **Inherited**, or triggered by strong immune activation (**EBV**, cancer); marrow shows **macrophages eating blood cells** (p. 80).

**Other source (HLH criteria):** MSD: diagnosis needs an HLH gene mutation (such as *PRF1*, the perforin gene; the gene name is standard teaching) or **5 of 8** findings, including fever over 38.5 °C for over 7 days, a spleen over 3 cm below the ribs, low counts in 2 or more lines, "Serum ferritin > 500 ng/mL", fasting triglycerides over 177 mg/dL, fibrinogen under 150 mg/dL, high soluble CD25 (IL-2 receptor), and hemophagocytosis on biopsy. Treatment: "chemotherapy with etoposide and dexamethasone", **emapalumab** ("an interferon gamma–blocking monoclonal antibody"), and possibly stem cell transplant ([MSD: HLH, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/histiocytic-syndromes/hemophagocytic-lymphohistiocytosis-hlh)).

## Chronic venous insufficiency (pp. 81-82)
- Leaky **vein valves** → high pressure in the **deep leg veins** → blood pushed back into **superficial veins** → **varicose veins** (p. 81).
- Risk factors: older age, obesity, family history, pregnancy, inactivity, past leg injury, **past DVT** (deep vein thrombosis) (p. 81).
- High pressure damages capillaries, leaking fluid, protein and **red cells**. Red cells leave **hemosiderin** → brown **stasis dermatitis** on the **medial leg above the medial malleolus**; later **lipodermatosclerosis** and **medial ulcers** (p. 81).
- Most common sign: **pitting edema**, worse in the **evening** or after standing, better with walking or raising the leg (p. 81).
- First treatment: **leg elevation, exercise, compression stockings** (p. 82).

**Other source (CVI):** MSD: venous stasis ulcers "typically occur around the medial malleolus"; symptoms "worsen with standing or walking and are relieved by rest and elevation"; risk factors include occupations needing "prolonged standing or carrying heavy loads"; raising the leg above the right atrium (heart level) at least 3 times a day "decreases venous hypertension and edema" ([MSD: Chronic Venous Insufficiency and Post-Thrombotic Syndrome, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/peripheral-venous-disorders/chronic-venous-insufficiency-and-postphlebitic-syndrome)).

## Massive transfusion and citrate (p. 83)
- More than **one blood volume (5-6 L)** of blood in **24 hours** can raise plasma **citrate**, the anticoagulant in stored blood (p. 83).
- Citrate **binds calcium and magnesium**, so calcium falls → **tingling (paresthesias)** (p. 83).

**Other source (how often citrate matters):** MSD defines massive transfusion as "greater than or equal to one blood volume in 24 hours (eg, 10 units in a 70-kg adult)" but says "Citrate and potassium toxicities generally are not of concern even in massive transfusion" and "Hypocalcemia can result but rarely necessitates treatment"; **liver failure** slows citrate clearance. MSD stresses **dilutional coagulopathy** (factors and platelets diluted) and **hypothermia** from cold blood ([MSD: Complications of Transfusion, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/transfusion-medicine/complications-of-transfusion)). On the exam: tingling around the mouth during a big transfusion = **low ionized calcium from citrate**.

## Clinical vignette
A 34-year-old woman has had gum bleeding and bruising for a week. She is oozing from her IV site. Platelets are 22,000/mcL, PT and PTT are prolonged, fibrinogen is low and D-dimer is very high. The smear shows large cells packed with granules and bundles of rod-shaped inclusions.
- **Diagnosis:** **APL** (acute promyelocytic leukemia) with **DIC**.
- **Why:** t(15;17) breaks the retinoic acid receptor, promyelocytes pile up, and their granules trigger DIC (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, pp. 63-64). MSD lists APL among cancers that cause DIC and puts its median age at 31 ([MSD: DIC](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/coagulation-disorders/disseminated-intravascular-coagulation-dic); MSD AML).
- **Next step:** start **ATRA** right away; it "corrects the DIC in 2 to 5 days" (MSD AML), now paired with **arsenic trioxide**.
- **Trap:** TTP, which has low platelets but a **normal** PT and PTT (guide 38).

## Common traps
- **TdT = lymphoblast (ALL); MPO and Auer rods = myeloblast (AML).**
- **Child + mediastinal mass + TdT+ = T-ALL**; young woman + mediastinal mass + **CD15/CD30** = nodular sclerosis Hodgkin.
- **t(12;21) good, t(9;22) bad** in ALL.
- **APL = t(15;17) + DIC + ATRA** (now ATRA plus arsenic).
- **Old patient + lymphocytosis + smudge cells + CD5/CD23 = CLL**; watch for **warm AIHA** and **Richter** transformation.
- **TRAP + big spleen + dry tap + no nodes = hairy cell → cladribine.**
- **High WBC + basophils + low LAP = CML; high LAP + Döhle bodies = leukemoid reaction.**
- **Itching after a hot shower + high hematocrit + low EPO = PV (JAK2).** High EPO points to a secondary cause.
- **Teardrop cells + big spleen + dry tap = myelofibrosis.**
- **Translocations: 14;18 follicular (BCL2), 11;14 mantle (cyclin D1), 8;14 Burkitt (MYC), 11;18 MALT, 15;17 APL, 9;22 CML/Ph+ ALL.**
- **CRAB + rouleaux + M spike = myeloma; M spike alone = MGUS; IgM + thick blood + no bone lesions = Waldenström.**
- **Birbeck granules + CD1a + S-100 = Langerhans cell histiocytosis.**
- **Fever + pancytopenia + huge ferritin after EBV = HLH.**
- **Tingling during massive transfusion = citrate binding calcium.**

## Practice questions
1. A 4-year-old boy has 2 weeks of tiredness, leg pain and easy bruising. Hb is 7.8 g/dL, platelets 31,000/mcL and WBC 48,000/mcL. Marrow shows 88% blasts that are TdT positive and express CD10, CD19 and CD20. Which translocation would most strongly predict a favorable outcome?
   - **Answer:** **t(12;21)** (*ETV6-RUNX1*).
   - **Why:** TdT with CD10/CD19/CD20 = B-ALL; your notes rate t(12;21) "good" and more common in children (p. 63); MSD lists t(12;21) and high hyperdiploidy as favorable (MSD ALL).
   - **Trap:** **t(9;22)**, the Philadelphia chromosome, which predicts a **poor** outcome.

2. A 34-year-old woman has gum bleeding, a low fibrinogen and prolonged PT and PTT. Her blasts are full of granules with many Auer rods, and cytogenetics show t(15;17). The drug started first acts by which mechanism?
   - **Answer:** it **binds the abnormal retinoic acid receptor** and lets the promyelocytes **mature** (ATRA, all-trans retinoic acid).
   - **Why:** the fusion receptor blocks maturation; ATRA overrides the block and the cells mature and die (pp. 63-64). MSD: ATRA "corrects the DIC in 2 to 5 days" and is paired with arsenic trioxide (MSD AML).
   - **Trap:** **inhibiting a tyrosine kinase** (imatinib), which treats t(9;22) CML.

3. A 61-year-old man has early fullness after meals. His spleen is felt 8 cm below the ribs. WBC is 112,000/mcL with neutrophils, myelocytes and metamyelocytes, and an absolute basophil count of 2,400/mcL. The LAP score is low. Which mechanism best explains how the first-line drug works?
   - **Answer:** it **blocks the BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase** (imatinib and related TKIs).
   - **Why:** high WBC + basophilia + low LAP + big spleen = CML, driven by t(9;22) BCR-ABL (p. 66); MSD lists imatinib, nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib and ponatinib as initial choices (MSD CML).
   - **Trap:** **leukemoid reaction**, which has a **high** LAP and no basophilia.

4. A 72-year-old man with a lymphocyte count of 46,000/mcL, smudge cells and lymphocytes that are CD5+, CD19+, CD20+ and CD23+ now has tiredness and yellow eyes. Hb is 8.1 g/dL, reticulocytes are high, the smear shows spherocytes, and the direct Coombs test is positive with anti-IgG. Which is the most likely cause of his anemia?
   - **Answer:** **warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia** caused by **CLL**.
   - **Why:** CLL brings "increased susceptibility to autoimmune hemolytic anemia" (MSD CLL; your notes p. 65). High reticulocytes show the marrow is working, so the cells are being destroyed, not under-made.
   - **Trap:** **marrow replacement** by leukemia, which gives a **low** reticulocyte count and a negative Coombs test.

5. A 58-year-old man has a red face, headaches and itching after every hot shower. Hematocrit is 59%, oxygen saturation on room air is 98%, the serum EPO is low and the spleen is enlarged. Which genetic finding is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **JAK2 V617F** mutation.
   - **Why:** high hematocrit + low EPO + normal oxygen = polycythemia vera (p. 69); MSD: JAK2 V617F "is present in 90% of patients" (MSD PV).
   - **Trap:** **secondary polycythemia** (lung disease, altitude, an EPO-making kidney tumor), where **EPO is high**.

6. A 24-year-old woman has a painless, slowly growing lump in her neck. Chest x-ray shows a mediastinal mass. Node biopsy shows bands of collagen dividing the node and large cells sitting in clear spaces; the large cells are CD15+ and CD30+. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **nodular sclerosis Hodgkin lymphoma**.
   - **Why:** collagen bands + lacunar cells + young woman + neck or mediastinal node (p. 76); CD15+ CD30+ = classic Hodgkin (MSD Hodgkin table).
   - **Trap:** **T-ALL / lymphoblastic lymphoma**, a mediastinal mass in a teenager whose cells are **TdT+**, not CD15/CD30+.

7. A 67-year-old man has 3 months of back pain. Calcium is 12.4 mg/dL, creatinine 2.1 mg/dL and Hb 9.2 g/dL. The smear shows red cells stacked like coins, and skull x-ray shows punched-out lesions. Which mechanism best explains his high calcium?
   - **Answer:** myeloma cells **switch on osteoclasts through the RANK receptor** (by raising RANKL, its activating signal; RANKL is standard teaching), and the osteoclasts dissolve bone.
   - **Why:** CRAB + rouleaux + punched-out lesions = multiple myeloma (p. 78).
   - **Trap:** **PTHrP** (parathyroid hormone-related protein) from a cancer such as squamous cell lung carcinoma (standard teaching), which raises calcium **without** lytic lesions or rouleaux.

8. A 9-year-old boy from Malawi has a fast-growing mass of the jaw. Biopsy shows sheets of medium-sized CD20+ B cells with scattered pale macrophages, giving a "starry sky" look. Overexpression of which gene drives this tumor?
   - **Answer:** **c-MYC**, moved next to the Ig heavy chain gene by **t(8;14)**.
   - **Why:** endemic (African) Burkitt lymphoma involves the jaw and is almost always EBV-linked (p. 73; MSD Burkitt).
   - **Trap:** **BCL2** (t(14;18)), which drives slow-growing follicular lymphoma in older adults.

9. A 74-year-old woman has blurry vision, headaches and nosebleeds. Her retinal veins look like sausage links. SPEP shows an IgM M spike; bone x-rays are normal. Which is the most appropriate immediate treatment?
   - **Answer:** **plasma exchange (plasmapheresis)** to remove IgM.
   - **Why:** big IgM pentamers make the blood thick (Waldenström macroglobulinemia, p. 79); MSD: plasma exchange "rapidly reverses bleeding as well as neurologic abnormalities" (MSD Macroglobulinemia).
   - **Trap:** **multiple myeloma**, which usually makes IgG or IgA and has **lytic bone lesions**.

10. A 2-year-old girl has a scaly rash on her scalp and behind her ears, a lytic skull lesion and new heavy thirst and urination. Biopsy cells are CD1a+ and S-100+. Which finding on electron microscopy is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **Birbeck granules** (tennis-racket shaped).
   - **Why:** lytic bone lesion + skin rash in a child + CD1a/S-100 = Langerhans cell histiocytosis (p. 80); the thirst fits **diabetes insipidus**, which MSD lists in LCH (MSD LCH).
   - **Trap:** **HLH**, which causes fever, low counts and a very high ferritin rather than lytic bone lesions.
