# Cancer drugs: how each chemotherapy and targeted drug works and what it damages

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: not yet audited
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials for USMLE Step 1, Hematology & Oncology, Pharmacology chapter (antimetabolites pp. 102-104, alkylating agents p. 105, antitumor antibiotics p. 106, microtubule drugs p. 107, platinum and topoisomerase drugs pp. 108-109, hydroxyurea and targeted drugs pp. 109-111, toxicity list p. 111; p. 101 is a title page). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual or the drug's US prescribing information (FDA label); where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Learn every cancer drug as a pair: **what it breaks** (DNA building blocks, the DNA strand, the mitotic spindle, a growth signal) and **its signature damage** (lungs, heart, kidneys, nerves, bladder). The exam usually gives the damage and asks for the drug, or gives the drug and asks for the mechanism.

**Other source (why chemo hurts normal tissue):** MSD: "The normal tissues most commonly affected are those with the highest intrinsic turnover rate", that is, **bone marrow, hair follicles and the gut lining** ([MSD: Systemic Cancer Therapy, updated Jul 2024](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/principles-of-cancer-therapy/systemic-cancer-therapy)). That explains why low blood counts, hair loss and mouth sores are so common.

## Antimetabolites: fake building blocks for DNA (pp. 102-104)
They work in the **S phase**, when cells copy DNA (standard teaching).

| Drug | Mechanism | Uses | Toxicity | Pages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Methotrexate** | Folic acid look-alike; blocks **dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)** → less **dTMP** → less DNA | **ALL**, lymphomas, **choriocarcinoma**, sarcomas; **ectopic pregnancy**, abortion (with misoprostol), **rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis**, IBD, vasculitis | **Marrow suppression (rescue with leucovorin)**; "toxic to 2 L": **lung** fibrosis and **liver** damage; **mouth sores** | 102-103 |
| **5-Fluorouracil (5-FU)** | Pyrimidine look-alike → **5-FdUMP**, which locks **thymidylate synthase** together with folate → less dTMP | **Colon**, pancreatic cancer; **topical** for basal cell carcinoma | **Marrow suppression**, made **worse by leucovorin** | 103 |
| **6-Mercaptopurine, azathioprine** | Purine look-alikes; **activated by HGPRT**; block new purine synthesis. Azathioprine turns into 6-MP | Preventing **transplant rejection**, RA, IBD, SLE; steroid-sparing | Marrow suppression, GI and liver toxicity; **more toxic with allopurinol or febuxostat** | 103-104 |
| **Cladribine** | Purine (adenosine) look-alike; blocks DNA polymerase, breaks DNA | **Hairy cell leukemia** (drug of choice) | Marrow suppression, kidney and nerve toxicity | 104 |
| **Cytarabine** | Pyrimidine look-alike; blocks **DNA polymerase** | **AML**, lymphomas | **Pancytopenia** ("**CYT**arabine → pan**CYT**openia"), megaloblastic anemia | 104 |

### Leucovorin: rescues one drug, boosts the other (p. 103)
- **Leucovorin** (folinic acid) is a folate that is **already reduced**, so it does **not need DHFR** (p. 103).
- **With methotrexate:** methotrexate blocks DHFR; leucovorin **goes around the block** and **rescues normal cells** (p. 103).
- **With 5-FU:** 5-FU needs reduced folate to lock onto thymidylate synthase, so leucovorin **strengthens** 5-FU (and its marrow toxicity) (p. 103).

**Other source (methotrexate label):** the FDA label says "Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolic acid reductase", lists **leucovorin** and **levoleucovorin** to lessen toxicity and **glucarpidase** "for the treatment of toxic methotrexate concentrations", and warns of **embryo-fetal toxicity** and damage to "bone marrow, kidneys, liver, nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, lungs, and skin" ([Methotrexate Injection prescribing information, rev. 12/2025, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/methotrexate-injection.html)).

### 6-MP, xanthine oxidase and TPMT (p. 104)
- Two liver enzymes break 6-MP and 6-thioguanine down: **xanthine oxidase (XO)** and **thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT)** (p. 104).
- **Allopurinol blocks XO**, so 6-MP builds up; your notes say the dose must be cut by **75%** (p. 104).

**Other source (allopurinol dose):** the mercaptopurine label: "Reduce the dose of MERCAPTOPURINE to one-third to one-quarter of the current dosage" with allopurinol, which matches your notes' 75% cut ([Mercaptopurine prescribing information, rev. 7/2026, via Drugs.com](https://drugs.com/pro/mercaptopurine.html)).

**Recent change (genetic testing):** the current mercaptopurine label adds a second enzyme, **NUDT15**: "Consider testing for TPMT and NUDT15 deficiency in patients who experience severe myelosuppression", and "Patients with homozygous deficiency of either enzyme typically require 10% or less of the recommended dosage" (same label). Your notes cover TPMT and XO only.

## Alkylating agents: glue the DNA strands together (p. 105)
| Drug | Key facts | Toxicity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Busulfan** | **Cross-links DNA**; **CML**; wipes out the marrow **before a bone marrow transplant** | **Severe marrow suppression**, **pulmonary fibrosis**, **darker skin** |
| **Cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide** | Cross-link DNA at **guanine N-7**; need **activation by the liver**; solid tumors, leukemia, lymphoma | Marrow suppression; **hemorrhagic cystitis** from the metabolite **acrolein** → prevent with **mesna** (its thiol group binds acrolein) or N-acetylcysteine |
| **Nitrosoureas** (carmustine, lomustine, semustine, streptozocin) | Need activation; **cross the blood-brain barrier** → **brain tumors** (glioblastoma) | **CNS toxicity**: seizures, dizziness, ataxia |

**Other source (classing):** MSD groups "Alkylating agents (including nitrosoureas), which damage DNA (eg, busulfan, chlorambucil, cisplatin)" and notes that "At high doses, alkylating agents damage the bone marrow" (MSD Systemic Cancer Therapy). MSD puts **cisplatin** in this group too.

## Antitumor antibiotics: break or wedge into DNA (p. 106)
| Drug | Mechanism | Uses | Toxicity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bleomycin** | **Free radicals → DNA strand breaks** | **Testicular cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma** | **Pulmonary fibrosis**, darker skin; **little marrow suppression** |
| **Dactinomycin** (actinomycin D) | **Slides between DNA bases (intercalates)** | **Childhood tumors**: **Wilms tumor, Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma** ("children **act** out") | Marrow suppression |
| **Doxorubicin, daunorubicin** (anthracyclines) | **Free radicals** + intercalation → DNA breaks | Solid tumors, leukemias, lymphomas | **Dilated cardiomyopathy**, marrow suppression, **hair loss** |

- Anthracycline heart damage depends on the **total (cumulative) dose** and can appear **months after** the drug is stopped. It is blamed on **free radicals**. **Dexrazoxane**, an **iron chelator**, cuts free-radical formation and is your notes' most effective way to prevent it (p. 106).

**Other source (bleomycin label):** "Pulmonary fibrosis is the most severe toxicity associated with bleomycin"; in "approximately 1%" the pneumonitis "progresses to pulmonary fibrosis and death"; it causes "single, and to a lesser extent, double-stranded breaks in DNA"; and in lymphoma patients it can cause "A severe idiosyncratic reaction consisting of hypotension, mental confusion, fever, chills, and wheezing". The label does not list marrow suppression ([Bleomycin prescribing information, rev. Mar 2026, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/bleomycin.html)).

**Other source (dexrazoxane label):** dexrazoxane is "a cyclic derivative of EDTA" that becomes "a ring-opened chelating agent that interferes with iron-mediated free radical generation"; its labeled use is "women with metastatic breast cancer who have received a cumulative doxorubicin dose of 300 mg/m2" ([Zinecard prescribing information, rev. 10/2016, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/zinecard.html)).

## Microtubule drugs: jam the mitotic spindle (p. 107)
| | **Vinca alkaloids**: vincristine, vinblastine | **Taxanes**: paclitaxel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Action | Bind **β-tubulin** and **stop microtubules forming** → no spindle (**M-phase** arrest) | **Over-stabilize** microtubules → the spindle **cannot come apart** (no anaphase) |
| Uses | Solid tumors, leukemias; **Hodgkin (vinblastine)**, **non-Hodgkin (vincristine)** | **Ovarian and breast** cancer |
| Toxicity | **Vincristine: neuropathy** (lost reflexes, nerve pain) and **constipation / paralytic ileus**, because axons need microtubules. **Vinblastine: marrow suppression** ("vin**BLAST**ine **blasts** the bone marrow") | Marrow suppression, neuropathy, **allergic reactions** |

**Other source (vincristine label):** the boxed warning says vincristine given **into the spinal fluid** "usually results in death"; the label relates its action to "inhibition of microtubule formation in mitotic spindle, resulting in an arrest of dividing cells at the metaphase stage"; neurotoxicity "appears to be dose related"; "A routine prophylactic regimen against constipation is recommended for all patients"; and "Serious bone–marrow depression is usually not a major dose–limiting event" ([Vincristine Sulfate Injection prescribing information, rev. 12/2025, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/vincristine.html)).

## Platinum drugs and topoisomerase blockers (pp. 108-109)

### Cisplatin, carboplatin (p. 108)
- **Uses:** **testicular, bladder, ovarian and lung** cancers (p. 108).
- **Toxicity:** **kidney injury** (most prominent; acute tubular injury), **peripheral neuropathy**, **hearing loss** (p. 108).
- **Prevention:** **amifostine** (a thiol free-radical scavenger) and **chloride diuresis** with IV normal saline, because cisplatin stays inactive where chloride is high (p. 108).

**Correction (how cisplatin works):** p. 108 says cisplatin works "by forming a reactive oxygen species that can form DNA crosslinks". The FDA label says "The main mechanism of the cytotoxic action involves the binding of cisplatin to genomic DNA in the cell nucleus", forming **cross-links within and between DNA strands** that block copying and transcription. The label does not mention reactive oxygen species. It describes the chlorine atoms as "more subject to chemical displacement reactions by nucleophiles", which fits your notes' point that high chloride keeps cisplatin inactive ([Cisplatin for Injection prescribing information, rev. 6/2026, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/cisplatin.html)). Think of cisplatin as an **alkylating-like DNA cross-linker** (MSD lists it with the alkylating agents).

**Other source (cisplatin label):** the label's labeled cancers are advanced **testicular, ovarian and bladder** cancer; its boxed warning lists **severe kidney toxicity**, **dose-related peripheral neuropathy**, **severe nausea and vomiting** and **severe myelosuppression**; and it says to "Maintain adequate hydration and urinary output for 24 hours after" each dose. **Ototoxicity** "is cumulative and may be severe" (same label).

### Topoisomerase blockers (pp. 108-109)
| Drug | Blocks | Uses | Toxicity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Etoposide**, teniposide | **Topoisomerase II** | **Testicular, small cell lung** cancer, leukemias, lymphomas | Marrow suppression, hair loss |
| **Irinotecan, topotecan** | **Topoisomerase I** (stops DNA unwinding) | **Colon** (irinotecan); **ovarian, small cell lung** (topotecan) | **Severe marrow suppression, diarrhea** |

Memory hook (standard teaching): **"I" for irinotecan and topotecan = topo I; etoposide = topo II.**

## Hydroxyurea (p. 109)
- Blocks **ribonucleotide reductase** → less DNA synthesis (**S phase**) (p. 109).
- Your notes list **melanoma, CML and sickle cell disease** (raises **HbF**); toxicity: **severe marrow suppression** (p. 109).

**Recent change (hydroxyurea uses):** the current Hydrea label lists only "Resistant chronic myeloid leukemia" and "Locally advanced squamous cell carcinomas of the head and neck (excluding the lip) in combination with chemoradiation"; **melanoma is no longer a labeled use**. The label confirms it works "as a ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor" ([Hydrea prescribing information, rev. 12/2023, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/hydrea.html)). The sickle cell use comes from separate hydroxyurea products; guide 40 covers it from MSD.

## Targeted drugs and antibodies (pp. 109-111)
| Drug | Target | Uses | Signature toxicity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bevacizumab** | Antibody against **VEGF** → no new blood vessels ("Be**V**acizumab blocks **B**lood **V**essels") | Colorectal cancer, renal cell carcinoma | **Bleeding, clots, poor wound healing** |
| **Erlotinib** | **EGFR tyrosine kinase** inhibitor (pill) | **Non-small cell lung cancer** | **Rash** |
| **Cetuximab, panitumumab** | Antibody against **EGFR** | Stage IV **colorectal** cancer with **wild-type (normal) KRAS**; head and neck cancer | **Rash**, raised liver enzymes, diarrhea |
| **Imatinib, dasatinib** | **BCR-ABL** (CML) and **c-KIT** (GI stromal tumors) tyrosine kinase | **CML, GIST** | **Fluid retention** |
| **Rituximab** | Antibody against **CD20** (most B-cell cancers) | Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, **CLL**, ITP, rheumatoid arthritis | **Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML)** |
| **Vemurafenib, dabrafenib** | **BRAF V600E** ("**VE**mu**RAF**enib = **V**600**E** BRAF") | **Metastatic melanoma** | |
| **Trastuzumab** | (HER2; standard teaching) | | **Cardiotoxicity** (p. 111) |

**Other source (cetuximab label):** the label limits colorectal use to "K-Ras wild-type, EGFR-expressing, metastatic colorectal cancer as determined by an FDA-approved test" and states "ERBITUX is not indicated for treatment of Ras-mutant colorectal cancer". It reports an **acneiform rash** in **82%** of trial patients and warns of **low magnesium** and serious **infusion reactions** ([Erbitux prescribing information, rev. 9/2021, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/erbitux.html)). Why it matters: a **mutant KRAS** is switched on **below** EGFR, so blocking EGFR cannot stop the signal (standard teaching).

**Other source (rituximab label):** the boxed warning covers "FATAL INFUSION-RELATED REACTIONS", "SEVERE MUCOCUTANEOUS REACTIONS", "HEPATITIS B VIRUS REACTIVATION" and "PROGRESSIVE MULTIFOCAL LEUKOENCEPHALOPATHY"; it is a "CD20-directed cytolytic antibody" labeled for NHL, CLL, RA, **GPA/MPA** (two ANCA vasculitides) and **pemphigus vulgaris** ([Rituxan prescribing information, rev. 12/2021, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/rituxan.html)). Check **hepatitis B** status before starting (standard teaching, following the boxed warning). ITP use (your notes) is covered by MSD in guide 38.

## Clinical vignette
A 27-year-old man with testicular cancer receives bleomycin, etoposide and cisplatin. Three weeks later he notices ringing in his ears and trouble hearing conversations, and his creatinine has risen from 0.9 to 1.6 mg/dL. He also has a new dry cough, and fine crackles are heard at both lung bases.
- **Ears and kidneys:** **cisplatin** (p. 108; cisplatin label).
- **Lungs:** **bleomycin** (p. 106; bleomycin label: pulmonary fibrosis is its "most severe toxicity").
- **Prevention he should have had for the kidneys:** **vigorous IV normal saline** (chloride diuresis), and amifostine in some patients (p. 108); the label says to keep up hydration and urine output for 24 hours.
- **Trap:** **etoposide**, whose main toxicity is marrow suppression, not ears, kidneys or lungs.

## Common traps
- **Methotrexate blocks DHFR; leucovorin rescues it. 5-FU blocks thymidylate synthase; leucovorin boosts it.**
- **6-MP + allopurinol = toxic 6-MP** (cut the dose to 1/3-1/4). Low TPMT or NUDT15 = toxicity at normal doses.
- **Cyclophosphamide → acrolein → hemorrhagic cystitis → mesna.**
- **Bleomycin and busulfan → lung fibrosis.** Bleomycin spares the marrow.
- **Doxorubicin → dilated cardiomyopathy (cumulative dose) → dexrazoxane.** Trastuzumab also hurts the heart.
- **Vincristine → neuropathy and constipation (marrow spared); vinblastine → marrow.** Vincristine into the spinal fluid is usually fatal.
- **Vincas stop microtubules forming; taxanes stop them coming apart.**
- **Cisplatin → kidneys, ears, nerves → saline (chloride) diuresis, amifostine.** It cross-links DNA directly.
- **Topo I = irinotecan, topotecan; topo II = etoposide.**
- **Nitrosoureas cross the blood-brain barrier → brain tumors.**
- **Dactinomycin = children's tumors (Wilms, Ewing, rhabdomyosarcoma).**
- **Cetuximab works only if RAS is not mutated; it causes an acne-like rash.**
- **Rituximab → PML and hepatitis B reactivation.**

## Practice questions
1. A 6-year-old girl with ALL receives high-dose methotrexate. Twenty-four hours later she is given a second drug to protect her marrow and gut lining. Which property of the second drug explains how it protects normal cells?
   - **Answer:** it is an **already-reduced folate** (leucovorin), so it supplies tetrahydrofolate **without needing dihydrofolate reductase**.
   - **Why:** methotrexate blocks DHFR; leucovorin bypasses that step (p. 103). The methotrexate label lists leucovorin "to diminish the toxicity".
   - **Trap:** **glucarpidase**, which removes methotrexate from the blood when levels are toxic (methotrexate label) rather than bypassing the blocked enzyme.

2. A 15-year-old boy has been on daily 6-mercaptopurine for ALL maintenance for 8 months with stable blood counts. Two weeks after he starts a new drug for gout, his WBC falls to 900/mcL and platelets to 18,000/mcL. Inhibition of which enzyme best explains this?
   - **Answer:** **xanthine oxidase** (allopurinol).
   - **Why:** xanthine oxidase inactivates 6-MP; blocking it lets 6-MP build up (p. 104). The label cuts the 6-MP dose to one-third to one-quarter with allopurinol.
   - **Trap:** **TPMT deficiency**, which is inherited and would have caused toxicity from the start, not after 8 stable months.

3. A 58-year-old man with non-Hodgkin lymphoma receives IV cyclophosphamide. Two days later he has painful, bloody urine. Which drug, given with the chemotherapy, would most likely have prevented this?
   - **Answer:** **mesna**.
   - **Why:** the metabolite **acrolein** injures the bladder lining; mesna's thiol group binds it (p. 105).
   - **Trap:** **amifostine**, which protects the **kidneys** from cisplatin.

4. A 52-year-old woman finished doxorubicin-based chemotherapy for breast cancer 8 months ago (cumulative dose 480 mg/m²). She now has shortness of breath on exertion, and echocardiography shows a dilated left ventricle with an ejection fraction of 30%. Which mechanism best explains her heart damage?
   - **Answer:** **iron-dependent free radicals** generated by the anthracycline injure heart muscle cells.
   - **Why:** anthracycline cardiomyopathy is dose-dependent and can appear months later (p. 106); dexrazoxane, an iron chelator, "interferes with iron-mediated free radical generation" (Zinecard label).
   - **Trap:** **HER2 blockade** (trastuzumab), whose heart damage is not linked to a cumulative dose (standard teaching).

5. A 24-year-old man being treated for Hodgkin lymphoma has a dry cough and shortness of breath. He has fine crackles at both bases, PFTs show a restrictive pattern with a low diffusing capacity, and his blood counts are normal. Which drug in his regimen most likely caused this?
   - **Answer:** **bleomycin**.
   - **Why:** lung fibrosis with **little marrow suppression** (p. 106); "Pulmonary fibrosis is the most severe toxicity associated with bleomycin" (bleomycin label).
   - **Trap:** **doxorubicin**, which damages the **heart** and also suppresses the marrow.

6. A 5-year-old boy with ALL has lost his ankle reflexes, cannot walk on his heels and has not had a bowel movement in 6 days. His blood counts are near normal. The drug responsible acts by which mechanism?
   - **Answer:** it **binds β-tubulin and stops microtubules from forming** (vincristine).
   - **Why:** axons need microtubules, so nerves and gut nerves suffer; vincristine spares the marrow (p. 107; vincristine label).
   - **Trap:** **stabilizing microtubules so they cannot come apart** (paclitaxel).

7. A 27-year-old man starts cisplatin for testicular cancer. Which measure given with each dose most reduces his risk of kidney injury?
   - **Answer:** **generous IV normal saline (chloride diuresis)**.
   - **Why:** high chloride keeps cisplatin in its inactive form in the tubules (p. 108); the label says to "Maintain adequate hydration and urinary output for 24 hours".
   - **Trap:** **mesna**, which protects the bladder from cyclophosphamide.

8. A 61-year-old man has metastatic colon cancer. His oncologist plans an antibody that blocks the epidermal growth factor receptor. Which tumor test result must be present for this drug to be useful?
   - **Answer:** **RAS (KRAS) that is not mutated (wild-type)**.
   - **Why:** cetuximab and panitumumab need wild-type KRAS (p. 110); the cetuximab label says it "is not indicated for treatment of Ras-mutant colorectal cancer".
   - **Trap:** **a BRAF V600E mutation**, the target of vemurafenib in melanoma.

9. A 16-year-old girl with sickle cell disease has had four pain crises this year. She starts an oral drug that raises her fetal hemoglobin. Which enzyme does this drug inhibit?
   - **Answer:** **ribonucleotide reductase** (hydroxyurea).
   - **Why:** p. 109; the Hydrea label says it works "as a ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor".
   - **Trap:** **thymidylate synthase**, the target of 5-FU.
