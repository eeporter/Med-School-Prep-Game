# General pharmacology: how drugs move and how they act

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: pending
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials: General Principles (pharmacokinetics pp. 30-46, pharmacodynamics pp. 47-52). Each fact is also checked against StatPearls and the MSD Manual; where a different source adds to your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
**Pharmacokinetics** is what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination. **Pharmacodynamics** is what the drug does to the body: receptors, potency and efficacy. Most exam questions come down to four ideas: bioavailability, volume of distribution, half-life, and how an antagonist moves the dose-response curve.

## Core mechanism
1. **Crossing membranes:** only the **nonionized** form crosses membranes. The **ionized** form is water soluble and stays trapped in the urine (El-Husseiny General Principles, pp. 31-32).
2. **Bioavailability (f):** the fraction of a dose that reaches the systemic circulation; IV is 100% (pp. 33-34). MSD adds rate as well as extent, and calls area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) the most reliable measure. [MSD Manual: Drug Bioavailability](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-bioavailability)
3. **First-pass effect:** an oral drug goes through the portal vein to the liver before it reaches the body, so a heavily metabolized drug has low oral bioavailability (p. 34). Sublingual and IV routes avoid this, and rectal dosing partly does (p. 35).
4. **Volume of distribution (Vd):** Vd = dose ÷ initial plasma concentration (p. 36). It is an apparent volume, not a real one. [StatPearls: Volume of Distribution](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545280/)
5. **Half-life:** t½ = 0.693 × Vd ÷ clearance, for first-order drugs. [StatPearls: Elimination Half-Life](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK554498/)
6. **Dose-response curve:** left-right position shows **potency**, and maximum height shows **efficacy** (p. 47). MSD describes them the same way. [MSD Manual: Dose-Response Relationships](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacodynamics/dose-response-relationships)

## High-yield facts

### Urine pH and drug clearance
- **Like dissolves like:** a drug stays nonionized in a fluid of the same type, so it is reabsorbed. In the opposite pH it ionizes and is excreted (El-Husseiny, p. 32).
- **Weak acids** (aspirin, penicillin, cephalosporins, loop and thiazide diuretics): alkalinize the urine to clear them. **Weak bases** (morphine, local anesthetics, amphetamine, PCP): acidifying the urine ionizes them (pp. 31-32).
- Alkalinize with sodium bicarbonate or acetazolamide; your notes list ammonium chloride, vitamin C and cranberry juice as acidifiers (p. 32).
- **Other source:** MSD confirms the principle and gives salicylate overdose as its example of alkalinizing urine. The effect is limited by how much of the drug is cleared by the kidney. [MSD Manual: Drug Excretion](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-excretion), [MSD Manual: General Principles of Poisoning](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/general-principles-of-poisoning)
- **Lactulose:** gut bacteria turn it into lactic acid, which converts ammonia (NH3) to ammonium (NH4+). The charged form is trapped in the stool, which helps in hepatic encephalopathy (p. 32).

### Transport across membranes (p. 33)
| Mechanism | Direction | Energy | Carrier | Saturable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Passive diffusion | Down the gradient | No | No | No |
| Facilitated diffusion | Down the gradient | No | Yes | Yes |
| Active transport | Against the gradient | Yes | Yes | Yes |

### Volume of distribution (p. 36)
| Vd in a 70-kg adult | Where the drug is | What keeps it there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About 3 L | Plasma | Large size, high protein binding, high charge, water solubility |
| About 12-14 L | Extracellular fluid | |
| About 40-42 L | Total body water | |
| Far above 42 L | Stored in tissues | Tight tissue binding leaves little drug in the plasma |

- Only **unbound** drug crosses into tissues. [MSD Manual: Drug Distribution to Tissues](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-distribution-to-tissues)
- **Displacement:** drugs compete for binding sites, which raises the free fraction. Your notes' example is sulfonamides displacing bilirubin in a newborn (p. 36).
- **Redistribution:** a lipid-soluble CNS drug can stop working because it moves into fat, not because it has been eliminated. A second dose lasts longer because the fat has already taken up drug (p. 37). MSD gives thiopental as its example. [MSD Manual: Drug Distribution to Tissues](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-distribution-to-tissues)

### Metabolism (pp. 38-39)
| Phase | Reaction | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phase I | Oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis | Includes CYP450 enzymes. MSD: aging reduces CYP450 capacity by 30% or more |
| Phase II | Conjugation: glucuronidation, acetylation, glutathione | Makes the drug more polar. MSD: aging does not affect glucuronidation or acetylation |

- **Inducers** in your notes: barbiturates, phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampin, chronic alcohol (p. 38).
- **Inhibitors** in your notes: cimetidine, omeprazole, chloramphenicol, macrolides, ritonavir, ketoconazole, acute alcohol, grapefruit juice (p. 38).
- **Acetylation:** slow acetylators get drug-induced lupus from hydralazine, procainamide and isoniazid (p. 39).
- **Glutathione:** running out of it in the liver causes acetaminophen liver injury (p. 39).
- **Glucuronidation:** reduced in newborns (p. 39).
- Phase details: [MSD Manual: Drug Metabolism](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism)

### Elimination
| | First order | Zero order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is removed per unit time | A constant **fraction** | A constant **amount** |
| Half-life | Constant | Changes with concentration |
| Rate vs plasma level | Proportional | Independent |
| Why | Enzymes are not saturated | Enzymes are saturated, at Vmax |
| Examples | Most drugs | Ethanol, phenytoin at high doses, salicylates at toxic doses |

Source: El-Husseiny, pp. 40-41; [StatPearls: Zero- and First-Order Kinetics](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK499866/).

### Steady state
- **Time to steady state depends only on the half-life**, not on the dose or how often it is given, for first-order drugs (p. 42).
- Your notes: 50% at 1 half-life, 90% at 3.3, and clinical steady state at 4-5 half-lives (p. 43). StatPearls gives the same figures for elimination: 90% after about 3.3 half-lives and 94-97% after 4-5. [StatPearls: Elimination Half-Life](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK554498/)
- **Doubling the infusion rate doubles the steady-state level** but does not change how long it takes to get there (pp. 43-44).
- **Loading dose** reaches the target faster and depends on Vd. **Maintenance dose** replaces what is cleared and depends on clearance. [StatPearls: Steady State Concentration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553132/)

### Formulas with worked examples
- **Vd = dose ÷ C0.** 500 mg gives an initial level of 10 mg/L: 500 ÷ 10 = **50 L**.
- **t½ = 0.693 × Vd ÷ CL.** Vd 40 L and clearance 4 L/h: 0.693 × 40 ÷ 4 = **6.9 hours**.
- **Loading dose = target level × Vd ÷ f.** Target 10 mg/L, Vd 40 L, f 0.5: 10 × 40 ÷ 0.5 = **800 mg**.
- **Maintenance rate = target level × CL ÷ f.** Target 10 mg/L, CL 4 L/h, f 0.5: 10 × 4 ÷ 0.5 = **80 mg/h**.

The formulas come from [StatPearls: Steady State Concentration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553132/) and [StatPearls: Elimination Half-Life](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK554498/). The numbers are made-up examples.

### Enzyme kinetics (pp. 45-46)
- **Km** is the substrate level at half of Vmax. A **higher Km means lower affinity**.
- **Vmax** rises only with more enzyme, which is what enzyme induction does.
- **Lineweaver-Burk plot:** the x-intercept is -1/Km and the y-intercept is 1/Vmax.
- [StatPearls: Noncompetitive Inhibitor](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545242/) says noncompetitive inhibition lowers the maximal rate and leaves affinity unchanged.

| Enzyme inhibitor (p. 51) | Looks like the substrate? | Overcome by more substrate? | Vmax | Km |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Competitive, reversible | Yes | Yes | Unchanged | Raised |
| Competitive, irreversible | Yes | No | Lowered | Unchanged |
| Noncompetitive | No | No | Lowered | Unchanged |

## Compare and contrast: drug interactions on the dose-response curve
| Type | Curve shift | Effect | Beaten by more agonist? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Competitive antagonist | Parallel, to the right | Lower apparent potency | Yes |
| Noncompetitive antagonist | Nonparallel, to the right | Lower apparent efficacy | Only partly |
| Partial agonist, alone | Lower ceiling | Less than a full response | Not applicable |
| Partial agonist with a full agonist | Response falls | Acts as an antagonist | Not applicable |
| Potentiation | Parallel, to the left | Higher apparent potency | Not applicable |

Source: El-Husseiny, pp. 48-52. MSD agrees that more agonist overcomes competitive antagonism. [MSD Manual: Drug-Receptor Interactions](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacodynamics/drug-receptor-interactions)

- **Physiologic antagonism:** two agonists oppose each other at different receptors, such as a vasoconstrictor and a vasodilator (p. 52).
- **Chemical antagonism:** one compound binds the drug directly, such as protamine binding heparin (p. 52).
- **Therapeutic index:** compares the median toxic dose (TD50) or lethal dose (LD50) with the median effective dose (ED50) (p. 52). A larger index is safer. MSD calls it the ratio of the minimum toxic concentration to the median effective concentration. [MSD Manual: Dose-Response Relationships](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacodynamics/dose-response-relationships)

## Clinical vignette
A 19-year-old man is brought in 6 hours after taking a large amount of aspirin. He is breathing fast and has ringing in his ears. His team starts an infusion of sodium bicarbonate. How does this speed up removal of the drug?

**Answer:** it makes the urine alkaline, so more of the weak acid is ionized and trapped in the urine.
**Why:** only the nonionized form is reabsorbed (El-Husseiny, p. 32). [MSD Manual: General Principles of Poisoning](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/general-principles-of-poisoning)
**Trap:** thinking bicarbonate helps by binding the drug. It changes the drug's charge.

## Common traps
- **Potency is not efficacy.** Potency is how much drug you need. Efficacy is the most it can do.
- **Giving a bigger dose does not reach steady state sooner.** Only a loading dose gets to the target faster.
- **Zero-order half-life is not constant.** Saying "half-life of ethanol" is the trap.
- **High Vd means low plasma level**, not high.
- **Low albumin** raises the free fraction of a highly protein-bound drug.
- **Competitive antagonists do not lower the maximum** when enough agonist is given.
- **A partial agonist can lower the response** when a full agonist is present. Your notes use this idea (p. 49); buprenorphine causing withdrawal in a patient on full opioids is the classic case (see guide 53).
- **Higher Km means lower affinity**, which runs opposite to what the number suggests.

## Practice questions
1. A drug given IV has an AUC of 100 units. The same dose by mouth has an AUC of 25 units. What is the oral bioavailability?
   - **Answer:** 25%.
   - **Why:** bioavailability compares oral exposure with IV exposure: 25 ÷ 100 = 0.25. AUC is the usual measure. [MSD Manual: Drug Bioavailability](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-bioavailability)
   - **Trap:** dividing IV by oral, which gives 4.
2. A 500-mg IV dose gives an initial plasma level of 50 mg/L. What is the Vd, and where is the drug?
   - **Answer:** 10 L, close to the extracellular fluid volume.
   - **Why:** 500 ÷ 50 = 10 L. (El-Husseiny, p. 36)
   - **Trap:** reading Vd as a real body space. Many drugs have a Vd far above total body water.
3. A patient starts a drug with a 12-hour half-life. About when is clinical steady state reached? What changes if the dose is doubled?
   - **Answer:** about 2-2.5 days (4-5 half-lives). Doubling the dose doubles the level but not the time.
   - **Why:** 12 h × 4 = 48 h and 12 h × 5 = 60 h. Time to steady state depends only on half-life. (El-Husseiny, pp. 42-44)
   - **Trap:** assuming a higher dose gets there sooner.
4. A man's blood alcohol falls by the same amount every hour, whatever the starting level. What kind of elimination is this?
   - **Answer:** zero order.
   - **Why:** a constant amount leaves per unit time because the enzyme is saturated. (El-Husseiny, pp. 40-41; [StatPearls: Zero- and First-Order Kinetics](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK499866/))
   - **Trap:** calling it first order, which removes a constant fraction.
5. A new drug moves the agonist curve to the right, but the maximum response is unchanged at high agonist doses. What kind of drug is it?
   - **Answer:** a competitive antagonist.
   - **Why:** a parallel right shift that more agonist overcomes is competitive. (El-Husseiny, p. 52; [MSD Manual: Drug-Receptor Interactions](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacodynamics/drug-receptor-interactions))
   - **Trap:** a noncompetitive antagonist, which lowers the maximum.
6. A patient on warfarin starts rifampin, and the INR falls. Why?
   - **Answer:** rifampin induces CYP450, so warfarin is cleared faster.
   - **Why:** rifampin is on your notes' list of inducers (p. 38), and MSD lists it as a CYP3A4 inducer. [MSD Manual: Drug Metabolism](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/clinical-pharmacology/pharmacokinetics/drug-metabolism) The warfarin example is standard teaching.
   - **Trap:** calling rifampin an inhibitor.
