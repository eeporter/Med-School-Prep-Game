# Breathing trouble in children: croup, epiglottitis, bronchiolitis, foreign body and chest trauma

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
From the question log: "differential diagnosis for acute respiratory distress and trauma in pediatric patients".
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Respiratory System (aspiration p. 7, surfactant and L/S ratio p. 10, epiglottitis and croup pp. 72-73) and Essentials of Microbiology and Immunology (croup and RSV pp. 289-290). Your notes do not cover pediatric chest trauma, so that part rests on the cited pages. Where a different source disagrees with your notes, or newer guidance has changed, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Sort a child's breathing trouble by where the noise is. **Stridor** (a noise on breathing in) points to the upper airway: croup, epiglottitis or a stuck object. **Wheeze and crackles** in an infant point to bronchiolitis. After chest trauma, a child can have a badly bruised lung with no broken ribs, and blood pressure stays normal until late.

## Core mechanism
1. **Croup** is viral swelling below the vocal cords (subglottis), so the narrowed airway gives a barking cough and inspiratory stridor. [MSD Manual: Croup](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/croup)
2. **Epiglottitis** is a bacterial infection of the epiglottis above the cords; the swollen epiglottis can suddenly close the airway, so airway control comes before everything else. [MSD Manual: Epiglottitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/epiglottitis)
3. **Bronchiolitis** is a viral infection of the small airways (bronchioles) in infants, giving wheeze, prolonged expiration and fine crackles. [MSD Manual: Bronchiolitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/bronchiolitis)
4. **Foreign body aspiration:** the right mainstem bronchus is more vertical and in line with the trachea, so objects slightly favor the right side. A partly blocking object acts as a ball valve: air gets in but not out, so that side is overinflated. [StatPearls: Airway Foreign Bodies](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/10/21909/)
5. **Chest trauma in children:** the chest wall is more pliant, so more energy reaches the lungs. Lung bruising (pulmonary contusion) is more common than rib fractures. The mobile mediastinum shifts easily, so a tension pneumothorax can compromise breathing and circulation **quickly**. Separately, children hold their blood pressure up by raising heart rate and constricting vessels, so **hypotension is a late sign**. [StatPearls: Thoracic Trauma](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482194/), [Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/)

## What your notes say
- **Epiglottitis (El-Husseiny Respiratory, pp. 72-73):** sudden fever, trouble swallowing, drooling and respiratory distress. Warning signs of closing airway: restlessness, anxiety, worsening stridor and a muffled "hot potato" voice; the child may hyperextend the neck in the tripod position. Hib was the main cause; since the vaccine, other *H. influenzae* strains, streptococci and *S. aureus* make up more of the cases. An X-ray is not needed when suspicion is high; a lateral view shows the enlarged epiglottis (thumb sign). First step: secure the airway, usually by intubation, then broad-spectrum antibiotics.
- **Croup (pp. 73; Microbiology p. 289):** parainfluenza virus spreads from the nose to the larynx and trachea and swells the subglottis; a cold is followed by a dry, barky, seal-like cough, hoarseness and inspiratory stridor. It is a clinical diagnosis; if unsure, a front (anteroposterior) neck X-ray shows the **steeple sign**. Moderate to severe croup (distress, stridor at rest) gets a corticosteroid plus nebulized (racemic) epinephrine, which constricts mucosal arterioles and shrinks the swelling.
- **Bronchiolitis (Microbiology p. 290):** usually before age 2 and mostly RSV; a runny nose and congestion, then cough, low fever and harder breathing, with low oxygen, fast breathing, retractions, wheezes and crackles. Usually mild, but infants under 2 months or born preterm can develop apnea or respiratory failure.
- **Aspiration (El-Husseiny Respiratory, p. 7):** the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, so aspirated material favors it ("swallow a bite, goes down the right"). Lying flat sends it to the posterior upper lobe and superior lower lobe segments; upright sends it to the lower lobe bases.
- **Other source (croup age):** your notes give 3 months to 3 years (El-Husseiny Respiratory, p. 73); MSD gives 6 months to 3 years. [MSD Manual: Croup](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/croup)
- **Recent change (RSV prevention):** your notes give **palivizumab** (an antibody against the RSV F protein) for premature infants (Microbiology, p. 290). CDC now recommends a long-acting RSV antibody (**nirsevimab or clesrovimab**) for infants younger than 8 months in their first RSV season unless the mother was vaccinated in pregnancy, and states: "Starting December 31, 2025, palivizumab will no longer be available." [CDC: RSV Immunization Guidance for Infants and Young Children](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/infants-young-children.html)

## Compare and contrast: infections
| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis | Bronchiolitis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Parainfluenza virus type 1 (most common) | *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib) classically; now also *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus* | Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), up to 80% |
| Age | 6 months to 3 years | About 2-8 years if unvaccinated; now more often adults | Under 24 months, peak 2-6 months |
| Onset and look | Cold, then barking cough, stridor | Abrupt; toxic, drooling, sore throat, trouble swallowing, sits leaning forward (tripod) | Cold, then fast breathing, retractions, cough, fever |
| Cough | Barking ("seal-like") | Usually **no** barking cough | Wheezy or hacking |
| Lung sounds | Inspiratory stridor | Stridor, muffled voice (standard teaching) | Wheeze, prolonged expiration, fine crackles |
| X-ray | Subglottic narrowing on front view (**steeple sign**) | Swollen epiglottis (**thumb sign**) on a side (lateral) neck view (El-Husseiny Respiratory, p. 73, which spells it "thump sign"; Microbiology p. 289 says thumb sign) | Not needed for the diagnosis in mild cases ([MSD Manual: Bronchiolitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/bronchiolitis)) |
| Season | Fall (parainfluenza) | Any (standard teaching) | December to February (Northern Hemisphere) |
| Treatment | Dexamethasone; add nebulized racemic epinephrine if moderate or severe | Secure the airway first; IV antibiotics (e.g. ceftriaxone) | Supportive: oxygen and fluids; steroids not recommended for a first episode |
| Prevention | — | Hib conjugate vaccine has almost eliminated it in children | A long-acting antibody against RSV (nirsevimab or clesrovimab) for infants under 8 months in their first RSV season, unless the mother got the RSV vaccine in pregnancy; nirsevimab only for high-risk children 8-19 months ([CDC: RSV Immunization Guidance for Infants and Young Children](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/infants-young-children.html)) |

Sources: [MSD Manual: Croup](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/croup), [MSD Manual: Epiglottitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/epiglottitis), [MSD Manual: Bronchiolitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/bronchiolitis)

## High-yield facts
- Epiglottitis: "drooling without a barking cough is a hallmark." Do **not** examine the throat with a tongue blade; it can trigger complete obstruction. Examine only in the operating room with airway experts ready. [MSD Manual: Epiglottitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/epiglottitis)
- A child who gives up the tripod position may be heading into respiratory failure. [MSD Manual: Epiglottitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/epiglottitis)
- Infants under 2 months and preterm infants with bronchiolitis can present with **apnea** (pauses in breathing). [MSD Manual: Bronchiolitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/bronchiolitis)
- Foreign bodies: 80% of cases are under 3 years old, peak 1-2 years; nuts and seeds are most common, and hot dogs cause the most food-choking deaths. Definitive treatment is **rigid bronchoscopy**. [StatPearls: Airway Foreign Bodies](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/10/21909/); sudden, persistent, one-sided wheeze in a young child and inspiratory-expiratory films: [MSD Manual: Wheezing](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/symptoms-of-pulmonary-disorders/wheezing)
- Many aspirated foods do not show on X-ray; inspiratory-expiratory films show air trapping on the blocked side. [StatPearls: Airway Foreign Bodies](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/10/21909/)

## Newborn breathing trouble: neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)
- **Answer first:** a preterm newborn with grunting, retractions and fast breathing immediately or within a few hours after birth most likely has RDS from **surfactant deficiency**. [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)
- **Mechanism:** surfactant lowers surface tension in the alveoli. Without it, alveoli collapse at the end of each breath (atelectasis), so gas exchange fails. The lungs usually do not make enough surfactant until about **34-36 weeks** of gestation. [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)
- **Risk factors:** prematurity (the main one), maternal diabetes, cesarean delivery and male sex. [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)
- **Chest X-ray:** diffuse **ground-glass** appearance with **air bronchograms**. [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)
- **Prevention:** give the mother a corticosteroid (betamethasone or dexamethasone) when delivery before 34 weeks is expected; steroids speed up surfactant production in the fetal lung. **Treatment:** surfactant, plus breathing support, preferably noninvasive such as continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)
- **Complications:** bronchopulmonary dysplasia (chronic lung disease of prematurity), bleeding inside the skull (intracranial hemorrhage) and air leaks such as pneumothorax. [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)
- **Trap:** the lecithin-to-sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio in amniotic fluid still shows up on Step 1 questions. Your notes: lecithin and sphingomyelin are about equal until the middle of the third trimester, then lecithin rises; by about 35 weeks the ratio is 2:1 or more, which means mature lungs; maternal and fetal cortisol speed up surfactant production (El-Husseiny Respiratory, p. 10). **Recent change:** MSD notes that fetal lung maturity testing is no longer recommended in practice. [MSD Manual: Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Neonates](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-problems-in-neonates/respiratory-distress-syndrome-in-neonates)

## Chest trauma in children
| Injury | Key findings | First step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tension pneumothorax | Mechanism: the injury acts as a one-way valve, so air enters the pleural space but cannot leave. Pressure rises, the lung collapses, the mediastinum shifts and venous return falls, giving **obstructive shock**. Signs: respiratory distress, low oxygen (standard teaching), absent breath sounds and **hyperresonance** on the affected side, trachea pushed **away** to the other side, **distended neck veins** ([Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/); neck veins may be flat if the child is also bleeding, standard teaching), then hypotension | Immediate needle decompression (or finger thoracostomy), then a chest tube; it is a clinical diagnosis, so don't wait for an X-ray |
| Hemothorax | Low breath sounds, **dull** to percussion (vs hyperresonant in pneumothorax), signs of blood loss | Chest tube to drain it |
| Pulmonary contusion | Bruised lung; can occur **without** rib fractures in children; the X-ray can look normal at first and change over 1-3 days; computed tomography (CT) is far more sensitive early | Supportive: oxygen, pain control, careful fluids |

Sources: [StatPearls: Thoracic Trauma](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482194/), [StatPearls: Pulmonary Contusion](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558914/), [MSD Manual: Tension Pneumothorax](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/thoracic-trauma/pneumothorax-tension), [Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/)

- Children can keep a normal blood pressure despite significant blood loss, so **hypotension is a late sign**. [StatPearls: Thoracic Trauma](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482194/), [Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/)
- Rib fractures in a child imply a lot of force, because the pliant chest bends before it breaks. [Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/) In children, rib fractures are "among the injuries highly indicative of physical abuse", so when there is no good story for that force, think about abuse. [MSD Manual: Rib Fracture](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/thoracic-trauma/rib-fracture)

## Clinical vignette
A 4-year-old who has never been vaccinated develops a high fever and severe sore throat over a few hours. He sits leaning forward, drooling, with a muffled voice and soft stridor. He has no cough. What is the next step?

**Answer:** secure the airway in a controlled setting (operating room, with anesthesia and an ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgeon ready) before any throat exam; then give intravenous (IV) antibiotics.
**Why:** abrupt fever, drooling, tripod position and no barking cough in an unvaccinated child mean epiglottitis, most classically from Hib; looking in the throat can trigger complete obstruction. [MSD Manual: Epiglottitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/oral-and-pharyngeal-disorders/epiglottitis)
**Trap:** examining the throat with a tongue blade, or sending him to radiology first.

## Common traps
- Barking cough = croup; drooling with **no** barking cough = epiglottitis.
- Steeple sign (front view, below the cords) = croup; thumb sign (side view, epiglottis) = epiglottitis.
- Wheeze in a 3-month-old in winter = bronchiolitis, not asthma; steroids are not recommended for a first episode.
- Sudden choking, then a one-sided wheeze in a toddler = foreign body until proven otherwise, even with a normal chest X-ray.
- A muffled "hot potato" voice and restlessness in epiglottitis mean the airway is closing (El-Husseiny Respiratory, p. 72).
- The palivizumab answer is outdated; current prevention is nirsevimab or clesrovimab (see the Recent change above).
- In tension pneumothorax the trachea moves **away** from the bad side (to the opposite side). [Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/)
- A normal blood pressure does not rule out serious bleeding in a child.

## Practice questions
1. A 2-year-old has had a runny nose for 2 days and now has a barking cough and stridor at night in October. What is the most likely organism?
   - **Answer:** parainfluenza virus type 1.
   - **Why:** croup (barking cough, inspiratory stridor, age 6 months to 3 years) is most often parainfluenza type 1, and parainfluenza croup typically occurs in the fall. [MSD Manual: Croup](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/croup)
   - **Trap:** choosing RSV, the main cause of bronchiolitis.
2. A 4-month-old in January has a cold, then fast breathing, retractions, wheeze and fine crackles. Which treatment is best supported?
   - **Answer:** supportive care with oxygen and fluids as needed.
   - **Why:** bronchiolitis (mostly RSV) is treated supportively; steroids are not recommended for a first episode and bronchodilators have not reduced admissions. [MSD Manual: Bronchiolitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/pediatrics/respiratory-disorders-in-young-children/bronchiolitis)
   - **Trap:** giving steroids as if it were asthma.
3. An 18-month-old choked while eating peanuts. Now she has a wheeze on the right, and the expiratory film shows the right lung staying overinflated. Diagnosis and treatment?
   - **Answer:** foreign body in the right mainstem bronchus; remove it by rigid bronchoscopy.
   - **Why:** the right mainstem is more vertical, and a partly blocking object traps air like a ball valve. [StatPearls: Airway Foreign Bodies](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/10/21909/)
   - **Trap:** a normal inspiratory film does not rule it out, because many foods, like nuts, are radiolucent (do not show on X-ray).
4. A 9-year-old hit by a car is short of breath. The left chest has absent breath sounds and is **hyperresonant** to percussion, neck veins are distended, and the trachea is shifted to the right. Blood pressure is still normal. What explains the distended neck veins, and why will his blood pressure eventually fall?
   - **Answer:** air trapped in the left pleural space (tension pneumothorax) raises pressure in the chest, which pushes the mediastinum to the right and squeezes the great veins. Blood backs up into the neck veins, less blood returns to the heart, so cardiac output and then blood pressure fall (obstructive shock). The fix is immediate needle decompression, then a chest tube.
   - **Why:** MSD notes that "as intrathoracic pressure increases, patients develop hypotension, tracheal deviation, and jugular venous distention" [MSD Manual: Tension Pneumothorax](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/thoracic-trauma/pneumothorax-tension); the shift to the opposite side and distended neck veins are listed by [Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: Chest Injury](https://www.rch.org.au/trauma-service/manual/chest-injury/). The reduced venous return step is standard teaching. Hyperresonance means air; dullness would mean blood (massive hemothorax).
   - **Trap:** calling it hypovolemic shock (bleeding gives flat, not distended, neck veins; standard teaching), or feeling reassured by the normal blood pressure, which falls late in children.
