# Substance use, intoxication and withdrawal

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the chapter on substance-related and addictive disorders (pp. 543-665; printed page numbers); Osmosis HY Pathology Vol. 2, chapter 104 (pp. 748-757); NBME Top Concepts 2021, drugs of abuse (pp. 411-413); Pharmacology High Yield (PDF pages); Osmosis HY Neurology (p. 91). Treatment details come from the MSD Manual, and rule changes from the FDA, DEA, SAMHSA and CDC. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied. Where another source disagrees or something has changed, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **SUD** = substance use disorder; **AUD** = alcohol use disorder; **OUD** = opioid use disorder; **DTs** = delirium tremens (alcohol withdrawal delirium); **PCP** = phencyclidine; **LSD** = lysergic acid diethylamide; **NMDA** = N-methyl-D-aspartate (a glutamate receptor); **GABA** = gamma-aminobutyric acid; **GGT** = gamma-glutamyltransferase; **CDT** = carbohydrate-deficient transferrin; **OTP** = opioid treatment program (methadone clinic); **NRT** = nicotine replacement therapy; **HPPD** = hallucinogen persisting perception disorder.

## In one line
A substance use disorder is a **harmful pattern of use with 2 or more of 11 problems in 12 months**. Severity is set by the count: **2-3 mild, 4-5 moderate, 6 or more severe** (pp. 553-554). Then learn each drug's **intoxication signs** (pupils, nystagmus, vital signs) and **withdrawal**, which usually looks like the **opposite** of intoxication (standard teaching).

## Substance use disorder: the 11 criteria (pp. 545-546, 553-554)
DSM groups the 11 items into four sets (p. 545):
- **Impaired control (1-4):** uses more or longer than planned; wants to cut down but can't; spends lots of time getting, using or recovering; **craving**.
- **Social impairment (5-7):** fails at work, school or home duties; keeps using despite relationship problems; gives up important activities.
- **Risky use (8-9):** uses in physically dangerous situations; keeps using despite knowing it causes or worsens a physical or mental problem.
- **Pharmacological (10-11):** **tolerance** (needs more for the same effect, or gets less effect from the same dose) and **withdrawal** (the typical syndrome, or using the drug or a close relative to avoid it).

Key rules:
- **Tolerance and withdrawal are not required** for the diagnosis (p. 546).
- They **don't count** for someone taking a prescribed opioid or stimulant only under appropriate medical supervision (p. 609 for opioids, p. 633 for stimulants).
- **No withdrawal criterion for PCP, other hallucinogens or inhalants**, because significant withdrawal has not been shown in humans (p. 546).
- **Remission:** early = no criteria (except craving) for 3 to under 12 months; sustained = 12 months or more (p. 554).
- **Lab clues to heavy drinking:** GGT above 35 units, and CDT of 20 units or more (p. 559).
- **AUD is common:** lifetime prevalence about 29% in U.S. adults, higher in men (36%) than women (23%); onset peaks in the late teens to mid-20s (pp. 556-557).

Later re-experiencing of trip perceptions = **HPPD** ("flashbacks") | pp. 596, 598, 546 |

| **Tobacco** | Not a DSM diagnosis | 4 or more **within 24 hours**: irritability, anxiety, poor concentration, **increased appetite**, restlessness, low mood, insomnia | p. 649 |

**Pupil shortcut:** small pupils = opioid intoxication. Big pupils = stimulants, hallucinogens and opioid **withdrawal**. **Nystagmus** = alcohol, sedatives, inhalants, and PCP, which can be **vertical** (pp. 561, 595, 605, 615, 617, 627, 640). The NBME notes test the same pairs: miosis and slow breathing = opioids; red eyes and giggling = cannabis; vertical nystagmus and "super-human strength" = PCP, an NMDA antagonist (NBME Top Concepts p. 412).

## Withdrawal timing and danger
- **Alcohol:**
  - Withdrawal starts **4-12 hours** after the blood level falls, **peaks on day 2** and is much better by **day 4-5**. Anxiety, insomnia and autonomic symptoms can last 3-6 months (p. 565).
  - **Seizures:** 6-48 hours (MSD).
  - **Alcoholic hallucinosis:** 12-24 hours (MSD).
  - **DTs:** usually start **48-72 hours** after the last drink and **can be fatal** (MSD).
  - **DTs features:** confusion with visual, tactile or (rarely) auditory hallucinations (p. 566). Look for a medical trigger such as liver failure, pneumonia, GI bleeding, head injury or low blood sugar (p. 566).
- **Benzodiazepines:**
  - Short-acting drugs (lorazepam, oxazepam, temazepam): symptoms start within 6-8 hours, peak on day 2 and improve by day 4-5.
  - **Diazepam** (long half-life): may not start for over a week, peaks in week 2 (p. 630).
- **Opioids:**
  - **Heroin:** symptoms start within **6-12 hours**, peak in 1-3 days and fade over 5-7 days.
  - **Methadone or buprenorphine:** symptoms take **2-4 days** to appear (p. 618).
  - MSD: opioid withdrawal is miserable but **"not life threatening"**.
- **Stimulants:** the crash after a binge brings fatigue, depression and hunger, and needs several days of rest (p. 644).
- **Tobacco:** symptoms are worst in the first 3 days and mostly gone in 2-4 weeks; craving can last much longer (MSD).
- **Cannabis:** symptoms often start about 12 hours after last use, peak at 2-3 days and last up to 7 days (MSD).
- **Remember (standard teaching):** alcohol and sedative withdrawal can kill (seizures, DTs). Opioid withdrawal is not life-threatening, though overdose is.

## Alcohol: complications and treatment
- **Blood alcohol:**
  - The legal limit is **0.08% (80 mg/dL)**.
  - 100-150 mg/dL brings unsteady gait, nystagmus and slurred speech.
  - 300-400 mg/dL often causes unconsciousness in people who rarely drink (MSD).
- **Withdrawal treatment:**
  - **Benzodiazepines are the mainstay** (diazepam, lorazepam or chlordiazepoxide). With liver disease, use **lorazepam or oxazepam** (MSD).
  - Phenobarbital is an alternative (MSD).
  - DTs need **high-dose IV benzodiazepines** (MSD).
  - Her notes: diazepam for DTs (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 44). Lorazepam, oxazepam and temazepam are usable in liver failure (PDF p. 44).
- **Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine, vitamin B1, deficiency):**
  - Signs: sudden **confusion, nystagmus, partial ophthalmoplegia and ataxia** (MSD). Her notes give the same signs (Osmosis Neurology p. 91; Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 749).
  - Lesions are typical in the **mammillary bodies** and dorsomedial thalamus (MSD).
  - **A carbohydrate load can trigger it, so give thiamine before (or with) glucose** (MSD; "Avoid glucose before thiamine", Osmosis Neurology p. 91).
  - Treatment: thiamine 100 mg IV or IM daily for at least 3-5 days (MSD).
  - Most survivors (80%) develop **Korsakoff psychosis**: severe trouble forming new memories, with **confabulation** (MSD; Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 749; DSM p. 556).
- **Relapse prevention** (her notes: Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 749; MSD):
  - **Naltrexone** (opioid antagonist) lowers relapse and drinking days. Avoid it in acute hepatitis, liver failure or opioid dependence.
  - **Acamprosate**: MSD calls it a GABA analogue. Her notes call it an NMDA (glutamate) blocker with GABA-agonist action and an "anti-craving agent" (PDF p. 49). It lowers relapse.
  - **Disulfiram** blocks acetaldehyde breakdown, so drinking within 12 hours brings flushing in 5-15 minutes, plus nausea, vomiting and low blood pressure. It works best with supervision in highly motivated patients. Avoid it in pregnancy and cardiac decompensation (severe heart disease).
  - Drugs that cause a **disulfiram-like reaction** with alcohol include **metronidazole**, tinidazole, cefoperazone, chlorpropamide, griseofulvin, procarbazine and nitrofurantoin (PDF p. 37).
- **Other source (anti-craving list):** her notes list baclofen, topiramate, acamprosate and naltrexone as anti-craving drugs (PDF p. 37). MSD says topiramate is still "under study" for craving and does not mention baclofen. For exam answers, use **naltrexone, acamprosate or disulfiram** for AUD.

## Opioids: overdose and treatment
- **Overdose:**
  - Signs: **miosis**, slow breathing and low ventilation, which can progress to apnea (MSD).
  - Give **naloxone**, a pure antagonist (PDF p. 67). Dose: 0.4-2 mg IV in 0.4 mg steps. Intranasal, IM or subcutaneous routes also work (MSD).
  - **Naloxone's half-life (about 1 hour) is shorter than many opioids', so breathing can slow again**. Admit long-acting overdoses for observation (MSD; NBME Top Concepts p. 412: "shorter duration of action").
  - Fentanyl-type drugs may need higher doses (MSD).
- **Withdrawal symptom relief:** **clonidine**, an alpha-2 agonist (PDF p. 20; MSD). Withdrawal can also be treated with buprenorphine or methadone (MSD).
- **Maintenance (medications for OUD):**
  - **Methadone**: a long-acting **full agonist** that prevents withdrawal and craving without much euphoria. It can **prolong the QTc** and cause torsades de pointes, and in the U.S. it must be given through a licensed **OTP** (MSD). Her notes: "DOC in opioid de-addiction" (PDF p. 66).
  - **Buprenorphine**: a **partial mu agonist with a ceiling effect** (MSD; PDF p. 68: partial agonist at mu, antagonist at kappa). It is often combined with naloxone, which is inactive when the tablet is taken under the tongue but discourages injecting (MSD).
  - **Precipitated withdrawal:** starting buprenorphine while a full agonist is still on the receptors pushes the patient into sudden withdrawal, so start it once mild withdrawal has begun (standard teaching).
  - **Naltrexone**: needs to be at least **7 days after the last full-agonist dose**. The monthly IM depot form is preferred for OUD (MSD).
- **Tolerance:** with morphine, tolerance develops to most effects **except constipation, convulsions and miosis** (PDF p. 66).
- **Other source (first choice for maintenance):** her notes call methadone the "DOC" (PDF p. 66). MSD presents methadone and buprenorphine as first-line options, with extended-release naltrexone as another choice. Know all three.

**Recent change (buprenorphine prescribing):**
- The **X-waiver is gone**. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023, signed **29 Dec 2022**, ended the DATA-Waiver program.
- Buprenorphine for OUD now needs only a standard DEA registration, with **no patient caps** ([DEA Dear Registrant letter](https://www.deadiversion.usdoj.gov/pubs/docs/dear-registrant-AM-01122023.html); [SAMHSA: Waiver Elimination (MAT Act)](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment/resources/mat-act)).

**Recent change (naloxone and nalmefene):**
- The FDA approved **Narcan 4 mg naloxone nasal spray for over-the-counter use on 29 Mar 2023** ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-over-counter-naloxone-nasal-spray)).
- The FDA approved the **first nalmefene nasal spray in May 2023** to reverse opioid overdose ([FDA: Naloxone and Nalmefene, current as of 8 Aug 2024](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/information-about-naloxone-and-nalmefene)).
- Her notes list nalmefene and IV naloxone (PDF p. 67).

**Recent change (methadone clinics):**
- A federal rule effective **2 Apr 2024** (compliance by 2 Oct 2024) changed OTP practice in three ways:
  - Patients can get **take-home methadone doses from the start of treatment**, based on clinical judgment.
  - The **1-year opioid addiction history** requirement for admission is removed.
  - OTPs can **start buprenorphine by telehealth** (audio-only or video) and **methadone by video** ([Federal Register, 2 Feb 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/02/02/2024-01693/medications-for-the-treatment-of-opioid-use-disorder)).

**Recent change (xylazine):**
- Xylazine is a non-opioid sedative now often mixed with fentanyl.
- **Naloxone does not reverse xylazine, but should still be given** because opioids are usually present.
- Xylazine causes **wounds that can become infected** and lead to amputation ([CDC, reviewed 16 May 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/about/what-you-should-know-about-xylazine.html)).

## Stimulants, PCP, hallucinogens, cannabis, tobacco
- **Cocaine:**
  - Blocks reuptake of norepinephrine, dopamine and serotonin. Dopamine drives the euphoria (MSD).
  - Toxicity: hypertension, tachycardia, **mydriasis**, sweating, **chest pain from coronary vasospasm**, MI, arrhythmia, aortic dissection, stroke, seizures, **hyperthermia** (MSD).
  - **Benzodiazepines first; beta-blockers are contraindicated** (MSD).
  - **Nasal septal perforation** comes from vasoconstriction and ischemic necrosis (MSD; Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 753).
- **PCP and ketamine:**
  - Both are **NMDA receptor antagonists** (MSD; PDF p. 49 lists ketamine as an NMDA blocker).
  - PCP users may be **violent** and **impervious to pain** despite injuries (MSD).
  - Treat with a **quiet, calm room** and benzodiazepines for agitation or seizures (MSD).
- **LSD and psilocybin:**
  - **Serotonin 5-HT2A agonists** (MSD).
  - For a **bad trip**, give reassurance in a quiet room, plus benzodiazepines if anxiety is severe (MSD).
  - **No withdrawal or physical dependence** (MSD).
  - **HPPD / flashbacks** usually fade in 6-12 months but can recur for years (MSD).
- **Cannabis:**
  - **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome** is cyclic nausea and vomiting in chronic users.
  - **Hot baths relieve it** (a diagnostic clue). The main treatment is **stopping cannabis**, and symptoms usually resolve within 48 hours after stopping. Haloperidol or topical capsaicin help in case reports (MSD).
  - Cannabis can worsen or set off psychosis in schizophrenia (MSD).
  - Urine screens show use within about the past month (MSD).
  - No FDA-approved medicine treats cannabis use disorder; treatment is behavioral (MSD).
- **Tobacco:** first-line aids are **NRT** (gum, lozenge, patch, nasal spray), **bupropion** and **varenicline** (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 756; MSD).
  - **Varenicline** is a **partial agonist at the alpha-4 beta-2 nicotinic receptor**, so it reduces craving and blocks nicotine's reward (MSD).
  - **Bupropion** is **contraindicated with seizures, an eating disorder**, or an MAO inhibitor within 2 weeks (MSD).
  - **Clonidine** also helps (PDF p. 20).

**Other source (e-cigarettes):** her notes suggest switching to electronic cigarettes (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 757). MSD cites the National Academies, which found **insufficient evidence** that e-cigarettes work as cessation devices. On exams, choose NRT, varenicline or bupropion.

**Other source (stimulant use disorder drugs):** her notes list **modafinil** for cocaine dependence (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 753). MSD lists **no medication** for cocaine use disorder; treatment is outpatient support, self-help groups and hotlines, with inpatient care for serious comorbidity. Do not pick a drug as "the treatment" for cocaine use disorder.

## Gambling disorder (pp. 661-663)
- The only **behavioral addiction** in this chapter.
- Diagnosis: **4 or more of 9** problems in 12 months.
  - Needs bigger bets for excitement, and is restless or irritable when cutting down.
  - Has failed attempts to stop, is preoccupied with gambling, and gambles when upset.
  - **"Chasing" losses** by returning to win the money back.
  - Lies to hide it, has risked or lost a relationship or job, and relies on others for bailouts.
- **Not better explained by a manic episode.**
- Past-year prevalence: about 0.2-0.3%. Lifetime prevalence: about 0.4-1.0%, higher in men (about 0.6%) than women (about 0.2%).

## Clinical vignette
A 54-year-old man is 3 days into a hospital stay after surgery for a hip fracture from a fall. Today he is disoriented, picks at "bugs" on his sheets, and is tremulous and sweating. His temperature is 38.6 °C, pulse 128/min and blood pressure 172/98 mm Hg. His wife says he drinks a fifth of vodka daily.

Diagnosis:
- Confusion with visual and tactile hallucinations plus autonomic hyperactivity, **48-72 hours after his last drink**, is **alcohol withdrawal delirium (DTs)** (p. 566; MSD).
- Simple alcohol withdrawal would peak around day 2 without confusion (p. 565).

Management:
- DTs can be fatal, so treat promptly with **high-dose IV benzodiazepines** (MSD).
- Look for a medical trigger such as infection, bleeding or electrolyte problems (p. 566).

## Common traps
- **Severity by count:** 2-3 mild, 4-5 moderate, 6+ severe. **Tolerance and withdrawal are not required**, and they don't count for prescribed opioids or stimulants taken under medical supervision (pp. 546, 554, 609, 633).
- **Small pupils = opioid intoxication; big pupils = stimulants, hallucinogens, opioid withdrawal.** Vertical nystagmus = PCP.
- **Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can kill (seizures, DTs); opioid withdrawal does not** (MSD; p. 630).
- **DTs at 48-72 hours; seizures at 6-48 hours; hallucinosis at 12-24 hours** (MSD).
- **Thiamine before glucose** in a malnourished drinker (MSD; Osmosis Neurology p. 91).
- **Naloxone wears off before long-acting opioids → watch for re-sedation** (MSD).
- **Buprenorphine is a partial agonist**; giving it too early precipitates withdrawal (standard teaching).
- **Naltrexone: avoid in liver failure and in anyone still on opioids** (MSD).
- **Cocaine chest pain → benzodiazepines, no beta-blockers** (MSD).
- **Stimulant crash → depression and suicidality, hypersomnia, hunger** (pp. 643-644).
- **No DSM withdrawal for PCP, hallucinogens or inhalants** (p. 546).
- **Bupropion is out if the patient has seizures or an eating disorder** (MSD).

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 34-year-old man often drinks more than he planned at parties, has tried twice to cut down without success, has missed work several times after weekend drinking, and keeps drinking even though his wife says it is ruining their marriage. He has never needed more alcohol to feel its effect and has never had shakes or sweats after stopping. Which is the most accurate diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **alcohol use disorder, moderate**.
   - **Why:** four criteria (more than intended, failed attempts to cut down, failed role obligations, continued use despite relationship problems) in 12 months = moderate (4-5) (pp. 553-554).
   - **Trap:** **no diagnosis because there is no tolerance or withdrawal**; neither is needed (p. 546).

2. A 27-year-old man is found unresponsive with a respiratory rate of 5/min and pinpoint pupils. Naloxone is given, and he wakes up and breathes normally. His friend says he took methadone pills. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **admit him for observation**, giving more naloxone if his breathing slows again.
   - **Why:** naloxone's half-life is about 1 hour, shorter than that of long-acting opioids like methadone, so respiratory depression can return within hours (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **discharge once he is awake**.

3. A 31-year-old woman who uses heroin daily wants treatment. Her last dose was 2 hours ago, and she has no withdrawal signs. She is given a sublingual buprenorphine dose in clinic. Within an hour she develops yawning, rhinorrhea, dilated pupils, abdominal cramps and goosebumps. Which property of buprenorphine best explains this?
   - **Answer:** it is a **partial agonist at the mu opioid receptor**.
   - **Why:** buprenorphine displaces the full agonist (heroin) but activates the receptor less, so the net effect is sudden withdrawal (precipitated withdrawal; standard teaching). Buprenorphine is a mu partial agonist (MSD; PDF p. 68). Yawning, rhinorrhea, mydriasis and piloerection are opioid withdrawal signs (p. 617; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **opioid receptor antagonism by the naloxone in the combination tablet**, which is inactive when taken under the tongue (MSD).

4. A 29-year-old man has crushing chest pain after snorting cocaine. Blood pressure is 188/110 mm Hg, pulse 124/min, pupils dilated, and he is agitated. ECG shows ST elevation in the anterior leads. Which drug should be given first for his agitation and hypertension?
   - **Answer:** **a benzodiazepine** (for example lorazepam or diazepam).
   - **Why:** benzodiazepines are the preferred first treatment for cocaine toxicity; cocaine causes coronary vasospasm and MI (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **metoprolol** or another beta-blocker, which MSD says is contraindicated in cocaine toxicity.

5. A 22-year-old man is brought in by police after fighting four officers. He does not seem to notice a deep laceration on his hand. Blood pressure is 170/100 mm Hg, pulse 120/min, and he has vertical nystagmus. Which mechanism best explains the drug's effects?
   - **Answer:** **NMDA (glutamate) receptor antagonism**.
   - **Why:** violence, numbness to pain, hypertension, tachycardia and vertical nystagmus fit PCP intoxication (pp. 594-595); PCP is a noncompetitive NMDA antagonist (MSD; NBME Top Concepts p. 412).
   - **Trap:** **serotonin 5-HT2A agonism** (LSD), which causes perceptual changes and dilated pupils, not vertical nystagmus or analgesia (p. 596; MSD).

6. A 25-year-old man has had three ED visits in 6 months for severe cyclic nausea and vomiting. He has smoked cannabis several times a day for 4 years and says long hot baths are the only thing that helps. Endoscopy and imaging are normal. Which is the most effective long-term treatment?
   - **Answer:** **stopping cannabis use**.
   - **Why:** cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome; hot bathing relief is a diagnostic clue, and the main treatment is to stop using cannabis (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **ondansetron alone**: antiemetics help acute episodes, but MSD names stopping cannabis as the main treatment.

7. A 20-year-old college student took a drug at a concert and now says he can "hear the colors" of the stage lights. He is anxious but oriented, his pupils are dilated, and his pulse is 108/min. He has no nystagmus. What is the main receptor action of the drug?
   - **Answer:** **serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonism** (LSD or psilocybin).
   - **Why:** perceptual changes while fully alert, synesthesia, dilated pupils and tachycardia (p. 596); the hallucinogenic effects work through 5-HT2A (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **NMDA antagonism** (PCP or ketamine), which causes nystagmus, violence and analgesia (p. 595).

8. A 24-year-old woman stopped using methamphetamine 2 days ago after a week-long binge. She sleeps 14 hours a day, is eating far more than usual, has vivid unpleasant dreams and feels hopeless. Which complication is the most serious concern?
   - **Answer:** **suicidal thoughts or behavior**.
   - **Why:** stimulant withdrawal ("crash") causes dysphoria, fatigue, hypersomnia, increased appetite and vivid dreams; depressive symptoms with suicidality are the most serious problem (pp. 643-644).
   - **Trap:** **withdrawal seizures**, which belong to alcohol and sedative withdrawal (pp. 564, 629).

9. A 49-year-old man with alcohol use disorder and decompensated cirrhosis has been abstinent for 2 weeks after a hospital detox and wants a medicine to help prevent relapse. He takes no opioids. Which drug is the most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **acamprosate**.
   - **Why:** acamprosate lowers relapse rates (MSD; Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 749) and is cleared by the kidneys, not the liver (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **naltrexone**, which MSD says is contraindicated in acute hepatitis or liver failure.

10. A 45-year-old woman taking a medicine to help her stay sober drinks a glass of wine at a wedding. Within 10 minutes she develops facial flushing, nausea, vomiting and light-headedness. Accumulation of which substance best explains her symptoms?
    - **Answer:** **acetaldehyde**.
    - **Why:** disulfiram blocks acetaldehyde metabolism, so alcohol within 12 hours causes flushing in 5-15 minutes, then nausea, vomiting and hypotension (MSD).
    - **Trap:** **ethanol**: blood alcohol is not unusually high; the toxic intermediate builds up.

11. A 38-year-old man with epilepsy controlled on levetiracetam smokes a pack a day and wants a medicine to help him quit. Which drug is most appropriate to add to counseling?
    - **Answer:** **varenicline**.
    - **Why:** varenicline, a partial agonist at the alpha-4 beta-2 nicotinic receptor, is a first-line aid (MSD; Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 756).
    - **Trap:** **bupropion**, which is contraindicated with a history of seizures (MSD).

12. A 46-year-old homeless man with heavy daily drinking is brought in confused. He walks with a wide-based, unsteady gait and has nystagmus and weakness of lateral gaze. Fingerstick glucose is 62 mg/dL, and the team is about to give IV dextrose. Which should be given first?
    - **Answer:** **IV thiamine**.
    - **Why:** confusion, ataxia, nystagmus and ophthalmoplegia = Wernicke encephalopathy (MSD; Osmosis Neurology p. 91); a carbohydrate load in thiamine deficiency can trigger or worsen it, so thiamine goes before or with glucose (MSD; Osmosis Neurology p. 91).
    - **Trap:** **dextrose alone first**.

13. A 41-year-old man has lost his savings and his marriage over sports betting. He bets larger amounts to feel excited, keeps going back to win back losses, lies to his wife about it and has borrowed from his brother to pay debts. He has never had a period of decreased need for sleep, racing thoughts or grandiosity. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **gambling disorder**.
    - **Why:** 4 or more of 9 criteria in 12 months (tolerance-like escalation, chasing losses, lying, bailouts, lost relationship), not better explained by mania (pp. 661-662).
    - **Trap:** **bipolar I disorder**, which needs a manic episode (guide 47).
