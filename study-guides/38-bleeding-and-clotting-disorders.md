# Bleeding and clotting disorders: platelets, clotting factors, DIC and inherited clotting tendencies

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials for USMLE Step 1, Hematology & Oncology (hemostasis pp. 12-26; pp. 13 and 19 are diagrams only). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Ask **where it bleeds**. Skin and mucosa (nosebleeds, petechiae, heavy periods) means a **platelet** problem (primary hemostasis). Joints, muscles and bleeding again hours after surgery means a **clotting factor** problem (secondary hemostasis). Then read the four labs: **platelet count, bleeding time, PT (prothrombin time) and PTT (partial thromboplastin time)**.

## Primary hemostasis: building the platelet plug (p. 12)
| Step | What happens | Key molecule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Vasoconstriction | Brief squeeze of the injured vessel | Neural reflex, **endothelin** |
| 2. Adhesion | **vWF (von Willebrand factor)** sticks to exposed collagen; platelets grab vWF with **GPIb** (glycoprotein Ib) | vWF comes from endothelial **Weibel-Palade bodies** and platelet **alpha granules** |
| 3. Degranulation | **ADP** (from dense granules) exposes **GPIIb/IIIa**; platelet **COX (cyclooxygenase)** makes **TXA2 (thromboxane A2)** | ADP, TXA2 |
| 4. Aggregation | Platelets link to each other through GPIIb/IIIa using **fibrinogen** as the bridge | Weak plug; secondary hemostasis must stabilize it |

Memory hook: **"Ib binds the wall, IIb/IIIa binds each other."** Adhesion uses GPIb; aggregation uses GPIIb/IIIa.

## Secondary hemostasis: the clotting cascade (p. 18)
- The cascade makes **thrombin**, which turns fibrinogen into **fibrin**; fibrin is cross-linked into a stable plug.
- Clotting factors are made in the **liver** in inactive form. Activation needs a trigger, the platelet **phospholipid** surface and **calcium** (from dense granules).
- **Extrinsic** pathway starts when **tissue thromboplastin (tissue factor)** activates **factor VII**. **Intrinsic** pathway starts when subendothelial collagen activates **factor XII**.

## Platelet bleeding vs factor bleeding (pp. 14, 20)
| | **Platelet problem** (primary) | **Factor problem** (secondary) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it bleeds | Skin and mucosa: **nosebleeds (most common symptom)**, gum, GI, urine, heavy periods | **Deep tissue**: muscles, **joints (hemarthrosis)** |
| Skin signs | **Petechiae** (1-2 mm), purpura (over 3 mm), ecchymoses (over 1 cm) | Large bruises, hematomas |
| After surgery | Bleeds right away | **Bleeds again later** (circumcision, wisdom tooth) |
| Key labs | Platelet count, bleeding time, smear, marrow | PT, PTT, **mixing study** |

- **Petechiae point to a low platelet count**, not to a platelet function problem (p. 14).
- Normal platelets are **150,000-400,000/mcL**; symptoms start below **50,000**; severe drops cause brain bleeding (p. 14).

## Reading the coagulation labs (p. 20)
| Test | Measures | Your notes' memory hook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PT** | **Extrinsic (VII)** + common pathway (**II, V, X, fibrinogen**) | "**P**lay **T**ennis **outside**" |
| **PTT** | **Intrinsic (XII, XI, IX, VIII)** + common pathway | "**P**lay **T**able **T**ennis **inside**" |
| **Mixing study** | Add normal plasma to the patient's plasma | **Corrects = factor deficiency; does not correct = inhibitor** |

**Correction (PTT common pathway):** p. 20 lists the PTT's common pathway as "factors I, V, X, and fibrinogen". Factor I *is* fibrinogen, so that list repeats fibrinogen and leaves out **factor II (prothrombin)**. The common pathway is **II, V, X and fibrinogen (I)**, exactly as the PT line on the same page says. MSD confirms that a lack of factor X, factor V or prothrombin prolongs **both** the PT and the PTT ([MSD table: Inherited Coagulation Deficiency](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/screening-laboratory-test-results-and-treatment-of-inherited-blood-coagulation-deficiency)).

## Low platelets from destruction

### ITP: immune thrombocytopenia (p. 15)
- **IgG autoantibodies** against platelet **GPIIb/IIIa**, made by plasma cells in the **spleen**; antibody-coated platelets are eaten by **splenic macrophages**.
- **Acute (children):** weeks after a **viral infection or vaccination**; goes away on its own within weeks.
- **Chronic (adults):** usually **women of childbearing age**; may be primary or secondary to **SLE (lupus)**. The IgG **crosses the placenta**, so the newborn can have brief low platelets.
- **Labs:** platelets often under 50,000, long bleeding time, **normal PT/PTT**, **more megakaryocytes** in the marrow (the marrow is working harder).
- **Your notes' treatment:** **corticosteroids** first (children respond well; adults often relapse); **IVIG (intravenous immune globulin)** for bleeding, short-lived; **splenectomy** for cases that do not respond.

**Recent change (second-line ITP treatment):** MSD now lists **second-line drugs** as "thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs), rituximab, rilzabrutinib, fostamatinib" (TPO-RAs: romiplostim, eltrombopag, avatrombopag) and says "splenectomy is often not necessary" because these drugs usually control the count. Splenectomy is usually reserved for counts under 15,000/mcL or disease lasting past 12 months ([MSD: Immune Thrombocytopenia, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombocytopenia-and-platelet-dysfunction/immune-thrombocytopenia-itp)).

**Other source (who gets treated):** MSD usually does not treat an adult with no bleeding and a count over **30,000/mcL**; IVIG raises the count "within 2 to 4 days"; "Platelet transfusions are not used prophylactically" and are kept for life-threatening bleeding; children "typically recover spontaneously", while adult spontaneous remission is uncommon (same MSD page).

### TTP and HUS: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (pp. 16-17)
**The picture:** platelets are used up forming **tiny clots in small vessels**, and red cells are **sliced** passing through them, giving **schistocytes** (fragmented red cells).

| | **TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura)** | **HUS (hemolytic uremic syndrome)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Low **ADAMTS13**, the enzyme that cuts vWF multimers; usually an acquired **autoantibody** | **Endothelial damage** from drugs or infection |
| Classic patient | **Adult woman** | **Child** with bloody diarrhea from **E. coli O157:H7** (undercooked beef); its **verotoxin** damages endothelium |
| Organ hit hardest | **Brain** (confusion, neurologic signs) | **Kidney** (renal failure) |

- **Both:** skin and mucosal bleeding, hemolytic anemia, kidney and brain involvement; your notes also list **fever** (p. 16).
- **Labs:** low platelets, long bleeding time, **normal PT/PTT** (the cascade is not switched on), schistocytes, **low haptoglobin**, **high LDH (lactate dehydrogenase)** and indirect bilirubin, more marrow megakaryocytes (p. 16).
- **Treatment:** **plasmapheresis (plasma exchange)** and corticosteroids, especially in TTP (p. 17). Plasma exchange removes the antibody and replaces ADAMTS13 (standard teaching).

**Other source (TTP details):** MSD says ADAMTS13 activity "usually has to be < 10% of normal for TTP to manifest", "Fever does not usually occur", and the direct Coombs (antiglobulin) test is **negative**, which separates it from autoimmune hemolysis. MSD's treatment list is "plasma exchange, glucocorticoids, rituximab, and rarely caplacizumab" ([MSD: TTP, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombocytopenia-and-platelet-dysfunction/thrombotic-thrombocytopenic-purpura-ttp)).

## Platelets that do not work (p. 17)
| Disorder | Defect | What fails | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bernard-Soulier syndrome** | Inherited lack of **GPIb** | **Adhesion** (cannot grab vWF) | **Big platelets**; abnormal **ristocetin** test |
| **Glanzmann thrombasthenia** | Inherited lack of **GPIIb/IIIa** | **Aggregation** (cannot link to each other) | Normal-size platelets |
| **Aspirin** | **Irreversibly** blocks COX → no TXA2 | Aggregation | Lasts the platelet's life |
| **Uremia** | Toxins in kidney failure | **Both** adhesion and aggregation | Kidney failure patient bleeding |

- **Ristocetin** makes vWF bind GPIb (p. 23), so the test is abnormal when either **vWF** (von Willebrand disease) or **GPIb** (Bernard-Soulier) is missing. In Glanzmann the ristocetin test is normal because adhesion still works (standard teaching).

## Clotting factor problems

### Hemophilia A and B (pp. 20-21)
- **Hemophilia A = factor VIII** deficiency; **hemophilia B (Christmas disease) = factor IX**. Both **X-linked recessive** (mostly boys), and can arise **de novo** with no family history.
- **Bleeding:** joints, deep tissue, after surgery; severity tracks how low the factor is.
- **Labs:** **long PTT, normal PT**, normal platelets and bleeding time; low factor VIII (or IX). A and B look the same clinically; only a **factor assay** tells them apart.
- **Treatment:** **recombinant factor VIII**. **Desmopressin (DDAVP)**, a vasopressin analog, releases vWF and factor VIII from endothelium for milder hemophilia A (p. 20).
- **Why giving thrombin works:** without VIII or IX, factor X is not activated and prothrombin is not converted, but **adding thrombin skips the missing steps** (p. 21).

**Other source (hemophilia details):** MSD gives the numbers: hemophilia A is about **80%** of cases; severe = factor level **under 1%**, moderate 1-5%, mild 5-49%; in one study **55%** of severe hemophilia A was sporadic; about **30%** of severe hemophilia A patients develop factor VIII **inhibitors** after replacement (3% in B). MSD limits desmopressin to **mild** hemophilia A with a proven response and factor VIII at least 5% (your notes say "mild-to-moderate", p. 20). Patients avoid **aspirin, NSAIDs** and **IM (intramuscular) injections** ([MSD: Hemophilia, updated Jul 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/coagulation-disorders/hemophilia)).

**Recent change (non-factor treatments):** MSD now lists drugs that are not clotting factors: **emicizumab** (an antibody that links factors IXa and X into "a factor Xase-like active complex that obviates the need for factor VIII"), **fitusiran** (an RNA drug that lowers antithrombin), **concizumab** and **marstacimab** (antibodies that block **TFPI**, tissue factor pathway inhibitor), and "AAV vector gene therapy products for hemophilia A and B" (same MSD page).

### Coagulation factor inhibitor (p. 21)
- An **acquired antibody** against a factor; **anti-factor VIII** is most common. Looks like hemophilia A.
- **Key test:** the PTT **does not correct** on mixing with normal plasma, because the antibody also attacks the factor in the added plasma. In hemophilia A it **does** correct.

### Vitamin K deficiency and liver disease (pp. 21-22)
- Vitamin K (activated by **epoxide reductase** in the liver) **gamma-carboxylates factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S**; without it they cannot work. Memory hook: **"1972"**: factors **10, 9, 7, 2** (standard teaching).
- **Causes:** **newborns** (no gut bacteria yet; every newborn gets a vitamin K shot at birth to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn), **long antibiotic courses** (kill gut bacteria that make it), **fat malabsorption** (vitamin K is fat-soluble).
- **Factor VII has the shortest half-life**, so the **PT prolongs first** in vitamin K deficiency and in liver disease.
- **A long PT that does not correct with vitamin K means liver disease**: the liver cannot make the proteins at all (p. 22).

**Other source (newborn dose):** MSD gives phytonadione (vitamin K1) **1 mg IM** to every newborn over 1500 g (0.3-0.5 mg/kg if 1500 g or less) within 6 hours of birth; the bleeding usually appears **1 to 7 days** after birth ([MSD: Vitamin K Deficiency, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nutritional-disorders/vitamin-deficiency-dependency-and-toxicity/vitamin-k-deficiency)).

## Mixed platelet and factor problems

### von Willebrand disease (p. 23)
- Inherited **vWF deficiency**; your notes call it the **most common inherited bleeding (coagulation) disorder**. Most common type: **autosomal dominant**, low vWF levels.
- **Two jobs of vWF, two kinds of trouble:**
  1. **Glues platelets to collagen** → low vWF gives **mucosal and skin bleeding** and a long bleeding time.
  2. **Carries and protects factor VIII** (half-life 12 hours bound vs 2 hours free) → low vWF gives a **long PTT**. Deep joint bleeding is usually **not** seen.
- **Labs:** long bleeding time, **long PTT, normal PT**, **abnormal ristocetin test**, normal platelet count.
- **Treatment:** **desmopressin**, which releases vWF from **Weibel-Palade bodies**.

**Other source (types and desmopressin):** MSD: **type 1** (quantitative, autosomal dominant) is most common; **type 2** is a qualitative defect (2A, 2B, 2M, 2N); **type 3** is a rare **autosomal recessive** form with no detectable vWF. Desmopressin is "usually ineffective in type 2 and in type 3", and in **type 2B** it "can exacerbate thrombocytopenia". Bleeding is treated with **vWF-containing factor VIII concentrates** or **recombinant vWF**, plus tranexamic acid for heavy periods or minor procedures ([MSD: Von Willebrand Disease, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombocytopenia-and-platelet-dysfunction/von-willebrand-disease)).

### DIC: disseminated intravascular coagulation (pp. 23-24)
- **Clotting switched on everywhere** → tiny clots cause ischemia, and **platelets and factors are used up** → bleeding from **IV sites** and body openings.
- **Almost always secondary.** Causes in your notes:
  - **Obstetric**: tissue thromboplastin in amniotic fluid, **retained dead fetus**.
  - **Sepsis**, especially ***N. meningitidis***: endotoxin, TNF and IL-1 make endothelium express tissue factor.
  - **Adenocarcinoma** (mucin), **acute promyelocytic leukemia** (primary granules), **snake bite** (venom).
- **Labs:** **low platelets**, long bleeding time, **long PT and PTT**, **low fibrinogen**, schistocytes, **high D-dimer** (your notes: best screening test; it comes from breakdown of **cross-linked** fibrin).
- **Treatment:** treat the cause; give blood products and **cryoprecipitate** (fibrinogen and factors) as needed.

**Other source (DIC workup):** MSD does not name one best test. It stresses a **fibrinogen that falls on two measurements in a row** (fibrinogen rises as an acute-phase protein, so one normal value can mislead), and says **factor VIII** helps separate DIC (low) from severe liver disease (normal or high). It adds **fresh frozen plasma** for severe bleeding, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen is under 100 mg/dL, and **heparin** only for slowly evolving DIC with clots ([MSD: DIC, updated Jul 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/coagulation-disorders/disseminated-intravascular-coagulation-dic)).

## Clotting too much: inherited thrombophilias (pp. 25-26)
**Classic clue:** **repeated DVTs (deep vein thromboses)** or a DVT at a **young age**; also hepatic and cerebral veins (p. 25).

| Disorder | Defect | Exam hook | Pages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Factor V Leiden** | Factor V lacks the cutting site for **activated protein C** | **Most common inherited cause** (your notes); DVT, cerebral vein clots, **recurrent miscarriage** | 26 |
| **Prothrombin gene mutation** | Mutation in the **3' untranslated region** → more prothrombin → more thrombin | Venous clots | 26 |
| **Protein C or S deficiency** | Autosomal dominant; less shut-off of **factors V and VIII** | **Warfarin skin necrosis** | 25 |
| **Antithrombin (AT, ATIII) deficiency** | Less inactivation of **thrombin (IIa) and Xa** | **PTT does not rise with heparin**; acquired in **nephrotic syndrome** (lost in urine) | 26 |

**Warfarin skin necrosis, step by step (p. 25):**
1. Warfarin blocks carboxylation of II, VII, IX, X **and** the natural anticoagulants **protein C and S**.
2. **Protein C has a short half-life**, so its activity falls by about **50% in the first day**.
3. Factors **II, IX and X** last longer and keep clotting, so for a few days the blood is **more** clot-prone.
4. Clots in skin vessels cause **skin necrosis**. Risk is highest with **protein C deficiency** or a big loading dose.
5. **Prevention:** start **heparin first and overlap** ("heparin bridge").

**Antithrombin and heparin (p. 26):** heparin works by **activating antithrombin**. With little antithrombin, standard heparin doses barely raise the PTT; your notes give high-dose heparin, then **warfarin (Coumadin)** to keep the patient anticoagulated.

**Other source (thrombophilia numbers):** MSD: factor V Leiden is present in about **5% of White Americans** and in 20-60% of people with unexplained venous clots; homozygotes are at higher risk than heterozygotes; it is screened with the **activated protein C resistance assay** and confirmed by DNA testing ([MSD: Factor V Resistance to APC, updated Jul 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombotic-disorders/factor-v-resistance-to-activated-protein-c-apc)). The prothrombin 20210 mutation occurs in under 1% to 4%, is "very rare in individuals of Asian or African descent", and raises **venous** but not arterial clot risk ([MSD: Prothrombin 20210, updated Jul 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombotic-disorders/prothrombin-factor-ii-20210-gene-mutation)). Heterozygous antithrombin deficiency affects about 0.02-0.2%, the homozygous form "is probably lethal to the fetus in utero", acquired causes include **DIC, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome and heparin therapy**, and long-term treatment is a **DOAC (direct oral anticoagulant) or warfarin** ([MSD: Antithrombin Deficiency, updated Jul 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombotic-disorders/antithrombin-deficiency)).

## Clinical vignette
A 34-year-old woman is brought in confused. She has petechiae, Hb 8 g/dL, platelets 14,000/mcL, schistocytes on the smear, high LDH, low haptoglobin and a mildly raised creatinine. PT and PTT are normal and the direct Coombs test is negative.
- **Diagnosis:** TTP.
- **Why:** low platelets plus hemolysis with schistocytes and **normal PT/PTT** means platelet microthrombi, not DIC; brain symptoms in an adult woman point to TTP over HUS; the cause is an autoantibody against **ADAMTS13**, so huge vWF multimers build up (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, p. 16).
- **Next step:** urgent **plasma exchange** plus corticosteroids (p. 17); MSD adds rituximab (MSD TTP page).

## Common traps
- **Petechiae and nosebleeds = platelets; joints and late surgical bleeding = factors.**
- **Normal PT/PTT with low platelets and schistocytes = TTP/HUS; long PT and PTT with low platelets = DIC.**
- **Mixing study corrects = deficiency; does not correct = inhibitor.**
- **vWD: long bleeding time AND long PTT**, because vWF also carries factor VIII.
- **Bernard-Soulier = GPIb, big platelets; Glanzmann = GPIIb/IIIa, aggregation fails.**
- **PT prolongs first in liver disease and vitamin K deficiency** (factor VII has the shortest half-life). Not fixed by vitamin K = liver.
- **New skin necrosis days after starting warfarin = protein C deficiency.**
- **Heparin will not raise the PTT = antithrombin deficiency** (think nephrotic syndrome).
- **The PTT's common pathway is II, V, X and fibrinogen**, not "I, V, X and fibrinogen".

## Practice questions
1. A 5-year-old has new petechiae 3 weeks after a cold. Platelets are 18,000/mcL; hemoglobin, white count, PT and PTT are normal. What is the most likely course?
   - **Answer:** acute ITP that resolves on its own within weeks.
   - **Why:** isolated low platelets after a viral infection in a child, with normal factors; childhood ITP is self-limited (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, p. 15); MSD: children "typically recover spontaneously".
   - **Trap:** leukemia, which would usually affect more than one cell line (standard teaching).
2. A 2-year-old boy has a swollen, painful knee after a minor fall. PTT is long, PT and platelets are normal, and the PTT corrects after mixing with normal plasma. Factor VIII activity is 2% of normal. Most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency), moderate severity.
   - **Why:** joint bleeding in a boy plus isolated long PTT that corrects = factor deficiency; low factor VIII = hemophilia A (pp. 20-21); MSD calls 1-5% moderate (MSD Hemophilia).
   - **Trap:** a factor inhibitor, which does **not** correct on mixing.
3. A 19-year-old woman has heavy periods and frequent nosebleeds; her father bruises easily. Platelet count is normal, the PTT is mildly long, and the ristocetin test is abnormal. Best first treatment for a minor procedure?
   - **Answer:** desmopressin (von Willebrand disease, most likely type 1).
   - **Why:** mucosal bleeding with autosomal dominant inheritance, normal platelets, long PTT (low factor VIII carrier) and abnormal ristocetin = vWD; desmopressin releases vWF from Weibel-Palade bodies (p. 23).
   - **Trap:** Bernard-Soulier, which has **big platelets** and does not prolong the PTT (p. 17).
4. On day 3 of warfarin for a DVT, a man develops painful purple patches that turn black on his thigh. What underlying problem is most likely?
   - **Answer:** protein C deficiency (warfarin-induced skin necrosis).
   - **Why:** protein C's short half-life makes it fall before II, IX and X, causing a brief clot-prone state that is worst with existing protein C deficiency (p. 25).
   - **Trap:** factor V Leiden, which raises clot risk but is not the classic cause of this reaction.
5. A child with nephrotic syndrome develops a renal vein clot. On standard IV heparin the PTT barely rises. Why?
   - **Answer:** acquired antithrombin deficiency from urinary loss.
   - **Why:** heparin works only by activating antithrombin, so too little antithrombin blunts its effect on the PTT (p. 26).
   - **Trap:** protein C deficiency, which does not change heparin's effect.
6. A patient with gram-negative sepsis oozes from every IV site. Platelets are 40,000/mcL, PT and PTT are long, fibrinogen is low and D-dimer is very high. What is the main treatment?
   - **Answer:** treat the sepsis, and give blood products and cryoprecipitate as needed (DIC).
   - **Why:** endotoxin and cytokines make endothelium express tissue factor, using up platelets and factors (pp. 23-24); MSD: "Immediate correction of the cause is the priority."
   - **Trap:** TTP, which has normal PT and PTT.
