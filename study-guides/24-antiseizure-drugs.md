# Seizure types and antiseizure drugs

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (seizures and antiepileptics pp. 216-224) and the Osmosis High-Yield Neurology notes (epilepsy, absence, febrile seizures). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual and FDA labels; where a newer or different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Pick the drug from the seizure type, then know each drug's signature toxicity. **Absence** = ethosuximide (or valproate). **Status epilepticus** = IV benzodiazepine first, then a longer-acting drug. **Carbamazepine** = low sodium, bone marrow and skin reactions (HLA-B*1502). **Phenytoin** = gum overgrowth, nystagmus then ataxia as levels rise, and saturable metabolism. **Valproate** = liver failure, pancreatitis and neural tube defects.

## Seizure types in your notes
| Type | What happens | Drug of choice in your notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple partial | One body part; **no** loss of consciousness, no postictal confusion | Carbamazepine |
| Complex partial | Almost always **temporal lobe**; impaired consciousness and a postictal state | Carbamazepine |
| Absence (petit mal) | Brief staring spells, **no** postictal confusion | Ethosuximide first, valproate second |
| Tonic-clonic (grand mal) | Stiffening, then rhythmic jerking; loss of consciousness and long postictal confusion | Phenytoin, carbamazepine or valproate |
| Myoclonic | Brief jerks under 1 second, often in clusters; consciousness kept | Valproate |
| Atonic | "Drop" attacks, often mistaken for fainting | (not given) |

Source: El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 216-217. A partial seizure that spreads to the whole cortex is "secondarily generalized". Epilepsy means **recurrent** seizures; febrile seizures are not epilepsy (p. 216). Common causes by age: children, genetic, febrile infection, trauma; adults, tumor, trauma, stroke; elderly, stroke and tumor (p. 216). Valproate is first line for absence **when tonic-clonic or myoclonic seizures also occur** (p. 218; Osmosis HY Neurology, "Absence seizure").

- **Recent change (names):** "partial" is now **focal**. MSD, following 2025 international guidelines, calls simple partial seizures **focal preserved-consciousness** seizures, complex partial seizures **focal impaired-consciousness** seizures, and secondarily generalized seizures **focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic** seizures. The in-between 2017 names "focal aware" and "focal impaired awareness", are the ones your Osmosis notes use. Be ready to recognize all three sets of names (standard teaching). [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders); Osmosis HY Neurology, "Epileptic seizure"
- **Other source (first-line drugs):** MSD lists carbamazepine as first-line for focal seizures **alongside** lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin and topiramate, and says newer drugs are no more effective but have fewer side effects. [MSD Manual: Antiseizure Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/antiseizure-medications) **Recent change:** the UK NICE guideline now suggests **lamotrigine or levetiracetam** first for focal seizures, and avoids valproate for tonic-clonic and myoclonic seizures in women and girls who could become pregnant (levetiracetam or lamotrigine instead). [NICE guideline NG217: Epilepsies, section 5 (UK, 2022)](https://nice.org.uk/guidance/ng217/chapter/treating-epileptic-seizures-in-children-young-people-and-adults) For a classic Step 1 question, your notes' answers (carbamazepine for focal, valproate for myoclonic) are still the expected ones.

## Core mechanism
1. **Focal vs generalized:** focal-onset seizures start in networks in **one** hemisphere; generalized-onset seizures start in networks in **both**. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
2. **Absence seizures** are genetic, mostly in children aged 5-15, with a 10-30 second loss of consciousness and eyelid fluttering. The EEG shows spike and slow-wave discharges on both sides at **3 per second**. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
3. **How the classic drugs work (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 218-222):** phenytoin, carbamazepine and valproate block voltage-gated **sodium** channels, holding them in the **inactivated** state so fewer are ready to fire the next action potential; this stops rapid repetitive firing. Valproate also raises GABA by inhibiting **GABA transaminase** and blocks T-type calcium channels. Ethosuximide blocks **T-type calcium** channels in thalamic neurons. Benzodiazepines increase how **often** the GABA-A chloride channel opens; barbiturates increase how **long** it stays open. Lamotrigine blocks sodium channels and cuts **glutamate** release; topiramate blocks sodium channels and boosts GABA. The FDA labels are more cautious: carbamazepine's mechanism "remains unknown", and phenytoin's is "thought to involve the voltage-dependent blockade of membrane sodium channels". [FDA label: Tegretol 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8d409411-aa9f-4f3a-a52c-fbcb0c3ec053), [FDA label: Dilantin (DailyMed)](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=86dd27d1-9cee-48ae-b55d-e2d2f7dbc593)
4. The ethosuximide label says it "suppresses the paroxysmal three cycle per second spike and wave activity associated with lapses of consciousness". That fits why it is used for absence. [FDA label: Zarontin 2021](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2021/012380s037lbl.pdf)

**Correction (gabapentin):** your notes say gabapentin raises brain GABA. The FDA label says it is shaped like GABA but does not bind GABA-A or GABA-B receptors, is not turned into GABA, and does not block GABA uptake or breakdown; it binds a subunit of **voltage-gated calcium channels**. [FDA label: Neurontin (gabapentin), revised May 2013](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2013/020235s054s055s056%2C020882s038s039s040%2C021129s035s036s037lbl.pdf) Calling that subunit alpha-2-delta is standard teaching.

## Why phenytoin levels jump (saturable metabolism)
At low doses the liver clears phenytoin in proportion to its level. Once the enzymes are saturated, they clear a fixed amount per hour, so a small dose increase can push the level up a lot (the label: phenytoin "is subject to saturable metabolism"). [FDA label: Dilantin (DailyMed)](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=86dd27d1-9cee-48ae-b55d-e2d2f7dbc593) The terms first-order (proportional) and zero-order (fixed amount) are standard teaching.

**Worked example (illustrative numbers, standard teaching):** if a patient's level is 12 mcg/mL on 300 mg a day and the enzymes are close to saturation, raising the dose by a third to 400 mg can more than double the level. A linear drug would only rise by a third, to 16 mcg/mL (12 × 400/300 = 16). Using the label's cut points, a level above 20 mcg/mL brings nystagmus and above 30 mcg/mL brings ataxia.

## Status epilepticus and special situations
- **Status epilepticus in your notes:** seizures lasting **5 minutes or more** without waking between them; it can cause high blood pressure, fast heart rate, arrhythmias and lactic acidosis. Steps: (1) **lorazepam** (benzodiazepines work within minutes); (2) **phenytoin or fosphenytoin** given at the same time to stop recurrence (phenytoin takes about 15 minutes to act); (3) **phenobarbital** if still seizing; (4) intubate and give midazolam, propofol or inhaled anesthetics. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 223)
  - **Other source:** MSD says there is no consensus on the second drug, and lists fosphenytoin, phenytoin, **levetiracetam** or **valproate**; for refractory status, options include lacosamide, phenobarbital, propofol, midazolam, levetiracetam and valproate. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
- **Status epilepticus (MSD):** tonic-clonic activity lasting **more than 5 minutes**, or 2 or more seizures without full recovery in between. Give an **IV benzodiazepine** (lorazepam, midazolam or diazepam) first, then a longer-acting drug: fosphenytoin, phenytoin, levetiracetam or valproate. There is no consensus on which second drug is best. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
- **Pregnancy:** carbamazepine, phenytoin and valproate have caused congenital malformations. The neural tube defect risk is somewhat higher with valproate, which is generally not recommended for women who could become pregnant. Lamotrigine seems lower risk. Give folate before conception to all women of childbearing age on these drugs. Continuing treatment is usually advised, because uncontrolled tonic-clonic seizures can harm the fetus. [MSD Manual: Antiseizure Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/antiseizure-medications)
- **Febrile seizures:** about 4% of children aged 3 months to 5 years. Simple ones are brief, single and generalized, and most need no antiseizure drug. About 2% go on to a seizure disorder. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
- **First seizure in an adult:** check glucose and sodium; alcohol withdrawal, head trauma, tumors and strokes are common causes; no cause is found in about half. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
- **Do not stop antiseizure drugs suddenly**; withdrawal can bring on seizures (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 222).
- **Valproate and neural tube defects:** your notes explain the risk by valproate blocking folic acid absorption from the gut (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 8).
- **DRESS** (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms): appears **2-8 weeks** after starting a drug; classic culprits are **phenytoin, carbamazepine**, allopurinol, sulfonamides, minocycline and vancomycin. Fever, lymph node swelling, facial swelling and a spreading measles-like rash, with **eosinophilia**, atypical lymphocytes and high ALT; can hit the liver, kidney (interstitial nephritis) and lungs. Thought to involve herpesvirus reactivation. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 224)
- **Febrile seizures in Osmosis:** age 6 months to 5 years, fever over 38 C. **Simple:** generalized, under 15 minutes, no repeat within 24 hours. **Complex:** any one of focal, over 15 minutes, or repeat within 24 hours. (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Febrile seizure") **Other source:** MSD gives 3 months to 5 years (below).
- **Enzyme inducers** (carbamazepine, phenytoin, and phenobarbital per your notes, p. 222) can lower levels of oral contraceptives, anticoagulants and antiretrovirals. [MSD Manual: Antiseizure Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/antiseizure-medications)

## Clinical vignette
A 7-year-old girl has several episodes a day where she stops talking, stares with fluttering eyelids for about 15 seconds, then carries on as if nothing happened. Hyperventilation in clinic triggers one, and EEG shows generalized 3-per-second spike and wave. What is the best first drug, and what does it block?

**Answer:** ethosuximide; it blocks T-type calcium channels in thalamic neurons (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 221).
**Why:** brief lapses with eyelid fluttering and 3-per-second spike and wave in a school-age child mean absence seizures. Ethosuximide is first line and is indicated only for absence. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders), [MSD Manual: Antiseizure Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/antiseizure-medications), [FDA label: Zarontin 2021](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2021/012380s037lbl.pdf); MSD notes absence seizures "can be precipitated by hyperventilation".
**Trap:** carbamazepine, which can **worsen** absence seizures.

## Common traps
- Absence: ethosuximide or valproate, never carbamazepine.
- Carbamazepine plus low sodium = SIADH. Carbamazepine plus sore throat and fever = check the blood count (agranulocytosis).
- Before carbamazepine in a patient of Asian ancestry, test for HLA-B*1502. The Tegretol boxed warning says allele-positive patients should not be treated, and the Dilantin label notes the strong link in "patients of Chinese ancestry". [FDA label: Dilantin (DailyMed)](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=86dd27d1-9cee-48ae-b55d-e2d2f7dbc593)
- Phenytoin toxicity: nystagmus first, then ataxia, then confusion as the level climbs.
- Valproate in a young woman: think neural tube defects and give folate; valproate in a child with a mitochondrial disease: liver failure.
- Status epilepticus: benzodiazepine first. Phenytoin alone is not the first step (it takes about 15 minutes to work; El-Husseiny p. 223).
- Gabapentin does **not** act on GABA receptors, despite the name (FDA label).
- Vigabatrin blocks GABA **transaminase**; tiagabine blocks GABA **reuptake**. (El-Husseiny p. 222)
- Complex partial (focal impaired-consciousness) seizures are usually **temporal lobe**, with a postictal state; absence seizures have **no** postictal state. (El-Husseiny pp. 216-217)
- Rash, fever, eosinophilia and liver injury weeks after starting phenytoin or carbamazepine = **DRESS**. (El-Husseiny p. 224)
- Topiramate = stones and weight loss; valproate = weight gain.
- Carbamazepine and phenytoin **induce** liver enzymes; valproate **inhibits** them. Adding valproate to lamotrigine raises lamotrigine levels and the rash risk (the rash link is standard teaching).

## Practice questions
1. A 30-year-old on carbamazepine for focal seizures becomes confused. Sodium is 122 mEq/L and urine is concentrated. What is the cause?
   - **Answer:** carbamazepine-induced SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone).
   - **Why:** the label reports hyponatremia and inappropriate ADH secretion with carbamazepine. [FDA label: Tegretol 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8d409411-aa9f-4f3a-a52c-fbcb0c3ec053), [MSD Manual: Antiseizure Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/antiseizure-medications)
   - **Trap:** blaming a new seizure; check the drug first.
2. A man on phenytoin has swollen, overgrown gums and new side-to-side eye jerking on lateral gaze. His dose was raised slightly last week. Why did a small dose change cause toxicity?
   - **Answer:** phenytoin's metabolism is saturable, so once the enzymes are saturated, small dose increases cause large rises in level; nystagmus appears around 20 mcg/mL.
   - **Why:** [FDA label: Dilantin (DailyMed)](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=86dd27d1-9cee-48ae-b55d-e2d2f7dbc593) (saturable metabolism, nystagmus at about 20 mcg/mL, gingival hyperplasia).
   - **Trap:** assuming levels rise in proportion to dose, as with most drugs.
3. A 26-year-old woman with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy on valproate wants to become pregnant. Her neurologist plans to switch her to another drug before conception. Why?
   - **Answer:** valproate has the highest risk of neural tube defects and can lower the child's IQ.
   - **Why:** MSD says valproate's neural tube defect risk is somewhat greater and it is not generally recommended for women of childbearing age; the label carries a boxed warning on fetal risk. [MSD Manual: Antiseizure Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/antiseizure-medications), [FDA label: Depakote ER 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0dc024ce-efc8-4690-7cb5-639c728fccac)
   - **Trap:** stopping all antiseizure drugs; uncontrolled seizures also harm the fetus.
4. A patient has had a tonic-clonic seizure for 7 minutes with no recovery. What is the first drug?
   - **Answer:** an IV benzodiazepine, such as lorazepam.
   - **Why:** more than 5 minutes defines status epilepticus, and an IV benzodiazepine comes first, followed by a longer-acting drug. [MSD Manual: Seizure Disorders](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/seizure-disorders/seizure-disorders)
   - **Trap:** starting with fosphenytoin alone, which is the second step.
5. Four weeks after starting phenytoin, a 40-year-old has fever, a swollen face, enlarged lymph nodes, a widespread rash, eosinophils of 15% and raised ALT. What is the diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms).
   - **Why:** it appears 2-8 weeks after a culprit drug such as phenytoin, with fever, lymph nodes, facial edema, rash, eosinophilia and organ involvement such as the liver. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 224)
   - **Trap:** Stevens-Johnson syndrome, which is mainly blistering skin and mucosal loss without the eosinophilia and organ picture (standard teaching).
