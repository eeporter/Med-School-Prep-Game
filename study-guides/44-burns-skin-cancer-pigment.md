# Burns, frostbite, skin cancers and pigment disorders

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: Osmosis Dermatology High Yield Notes (burns, frostbite, sunburn and actinic keratosis pp. 1-9; malignant tumors pp. 24-31; pigmentation disorders pp. 38-42; page numbers are the printed footer numbers). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual, FDA or EMA (European Medicines Agency) pages; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **TBSA** = total body surface area; **AK** = actinic keratosis; **BCC** = basal cell carcinoma; **SCC** = squamous cell carcinoma; **UV** = ultraviolet; **tPA** = tissue plasminogen activator; **PD-1** = programmed death-1; **CTLA-4** = cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4; **LAG-3** = lymphocyte activation gene 3; **JAK** = Janus kinase; **OCA** = oculocutaneous albinism.

## In one line
Skin is a barrier that holds in **heat and water** and keeps out **microbes**, so a big burn causes **cooling, fluid loss and infection** (p. 1). Sun damage builds up as **DNA mutations in keratinocytes and melanocytes**. It causes **AK, which can become SCC**, and it also causes **BCC and melanoma**, which do not come from AK (pp. 1, 25-29). Pigment disorders come down to one question: **are the melanocytes missing (vitiligo) or present but unable to make melanin (albinism)?**

## Burns (pp. 2-6)

### Three zones around a burn (p. 3)
| Zone | What happens | Can it be saved? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Coagulation** (center) | No blood flow; **coagulative necrosis** | No |
| **Stasis** (middle) | Sludging and clots in small vessels; cells die within **24-48 hours** without treatment | **Yes, with early fluids and care.** This is the zone treatment fights for |
| **Hyperemia** (outer) | Vasodilation and leaky capillaries; red skin | Usually recovers |

### Burn depth (p. 4)
| Depth | Layer reached | What you see | Healing in her notes (p. 4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Superficial (1st degree)** | Epidermis | Red, painful, blanches, **no blisters** | 5-10 days, no scar |
| **Superficial partial thickness (2nd degree)** | **Papillary** (upper) dermis | **Painful, clear blisters**, blanches, hair follicles present | 2-3 weeks |
| **Deep partial thickness (2nd degree)** | **Reticular** (deep) dermis | Yellow-white, **little or no sensation**, minor blanching | 1-2 months; hypertrophic scars common; grafting recommended |
| **Full thickness (3rd degree)** | Through the whole dermis | **Painless, leathery eschar**, no hair follicles, clotted veins visible | Only from wound edges; needs grafting |
| **4th degree** | Fat, muscle or bone | Black, charred | Debridement or amputation |

The rule to remember: **pain means nerve endings are still alive**, so a painful blistered burn is shallower than a painless leathery one (p. 4).

**Other source (healing times):** MSD says superficial partial-thickness burns heal "within 1 to 2 weeks" and deep partial-thickness burns take "≥ 2 weeks to heal" with common, sometimes severe scarring. MSD describes full-thickness burns as extending "through the entire dermis and into the underlying fat" and does not use a 4th-degree category ([MSD: Burns, updated Sept 2024](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/burns/burns)). The order of depth is the same in both sources.

### Measuring the burn: TBSA (p. 5)
- **Count only partial-thickness and full-thickness burns.** Superficial (1st degree) burns are left out (p. 5). MSD agrees: "only partial-thickness and full-thickness burns are included in this calculation."
- **Hand rule:** the patient's hand print, **palm plus fingers**, is about **1%** of TBSA (p. 5). MSD: "the patient's entire opened hand (not the palm only), which is about 1% of TBSA."
- **Rule of nines (p. 5):**
  - **Adults:** head 9%, each arm 9%, chest 9%, abdomen 9%, back 18%, each leg 18%, groin 1% (total 100%).
  - **Children:** **head 18%**, each arm 9%, chest 9%, abdomen 9%, back 18% (see Correction), **each leg 13.5%**, groin 1%.
  - Children have bigger heads and smaller legs for their size, so MSD uses the **Lund-Browder chart** for them.

**Correction (children's back):** her table prints the children's back as **19%**, which makes the column add up to 101%. GPnotebook gives "thorax and abdomen back: 18%" for children. It also gives each leg as 14% in infants; after age 1 the head falls by 1% and each leg rises by 0.5% a year until adult values at about age 10 ([GPnotebook: Rule of nines (paediatric)](https://gpnotebook.com/pages/trauma-medicine/rule-of-nines/rule-of-nines-paediatric)).

### Fluids: the Parkland formula (p. 5)
**Fluid for the first 24 hours = 4 mL × weight (kg) × %TBSA.** Give **half in the first 8 hours** and the other half over the next 16 hours (p. 5; MSD).

Worked example: a 70-kg man with partial- and full-thickness burns over 30% TBSA.
- 4 × 70 × 30 = **8,400 mL in 24 hours**
- First 8 hours: 8,400 ÷ 2 = **4,200 mL** (about 525 mL per hour)
- Next 16 hours: **4,200 mL** (about 262 mL per hour)

**Correction (how the formula is printed):** p. 5 prints "Volume required in 24 hours = 4 x mass (kg) x (% TBSA x 100)". Read literally, a 30% burn would become 30 × 100 = 3,000 and the answer would be 100 times too big. MSD writes it as "(4 mL/kg) × % TBSA burned": use the **percentage as a plain number** (30 for 30%), and count only partial- and full-thickness burns.

**Other source (checking that fluids are enough):** MSD says to adjust fluids to keep urine output at "0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults and 1.0 mL/kg/hour in children (< 30 kg)", and that "Patients with large burns (> 20% TBSA) require fluid resuscitation."

### Which burns are serious (p. 5)
Her notes use the American Burn Association grades (p. 5):
- **Minor:** < 2% full thickness, < 10% TBSA (< 5% in the very young or old)
- **Moderate:** 2-5% full thickness, 10-20% TBSA (5-10% young/old), or high-voltage injury, possible inhalation injury, a burn all the way around a limb (circumferential), or other illnesses
- **Major:** > 5% full thickness, > 20% TBSA (> 10% young/old), or high-voltage burn, known inhalation injury, significant burns of the face, joints, hands or feet, or other injuries

**Other source (when to call a burn center):** MSD strongly suggests consulting for full-thickness burns > 1% TBSA, partial-thickness burns > 5% TBSA, and "Burns of the hands, face, feet, or perineum (partial-thickness or deeper)", and lists age < 2 or > 60 as a reason to consider transfer or admission. Her notes list age < 3 or > 60 as a risk factor (p. 4).

### Complications (p. 4)
- **Infection:** her notes list ***S. aureus*, *P. aeruginosa* and *Candida albicans*** as the most common organisms (p. 4). **Other source:** MSD: "The most common pathogens are streptococci and staphylococci during the first few days", with gram-negative bacteria more common "after 5 to 7 days". Exam pattern: early burn infection = gram-positive cocci; a burn wound turning green with a fruity smell days later = *Pseudomonas* (standard teaching).
- **Large burns (> 30% TBSA):** strong inflammation lowers organ blood flow, causing GI bleeding, kidney failure and lung failure; high catecholamines and cortisol cause **hypermetabolism** and immune suppression (p. 4).
- **Airway:** singed airways swell shut over time; **carbon monoxide** poisoning (p. 4). MSD: consider early intubation if the patient has breathing symptoms, and give **100% oxygen** for possible smoke inhalation.
- **Long term:** contractures and hypertrophic scars (p. 4).

### Burn types (pp. 2-3, 6)
- **Thermal:** proteins denature above **44 °C** (p. 2).
- **Chemical:** **alkali is worse than acid** because it keeps penetrating (p. 3). Remove clothing, **brush off dry powder first**, then irrigate with water at low pressure; for elemental metals (sodium, potassium, lithium) use **mineral oil, not water** (p. 6). **Other source:** her notes say irrigate for 1-2 hours (p. 6); MSD says "copious amounts of water continuing for at least 20 min" and "powders should be brushed off before wetting."
- **Electrical:** damage **under the skin is much worse than the skin wound** (p. 3). Current through the heart can kill; monitor the heart (p. 6). Bone has the most resistance and nerves the least (p. 3). MSD: "High voltage (> 1000 volts) electrical burns often cause extensive deep tissue damage" even when the skin looks minor.
- **Radiation:** sunlight is the most common cause (p. 3).

### Wound care (pp. 5-6)
Leave blisters intact unless they block circulation or movement over a joint; cut dead tissue back to **bleeding (viable) tissue** (pp. 5-6).

## Frostbite (pp. 6-8)

### What happens (p. 6)
- **Freezing:** below about −4 °C, **ice crystals form in tissue**, damaging cell membranes and small vessels; cold also causes vasoconstriction (p. 6). MSD: "Ice crystals form within or between tissue cells", and nearby tissue is hurt by "local vasoconstriction and thrombosis" ([MSD: Frostbite, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/cold-injury/frostbite)).
- **Thawing:** rewarming opens the vessels, causing swelling, and **clots form in damaged small vessels**, so tissue keeps dying after it thaws (p. 6). This is why clot-busting and vessel-opening drugs help.

### Grades (p. 7)
- **Frostnip:** pale, numb, no ice crystals, reverses fast.
- **1st degree:** central pallor and numbness with swelling around it; full recovery.
- **2nd degree:** **large clear blisters within 24 hours**.
- **3rd degree:** smaller blisters that may be **bloody (hemorrhagic)**; black eschar over weeks.
- **4th degree:** fat, muscle or bone; painless; the tissue **mummifies in 4-10 days**, and **autoamputation** may follow over 1-2 months.

MSD groups these more simply: **superficial** (1st and 2nd degree) "does not involve permanent tissue loss", and **deep** (3rd and 4th degree) "involves permanent tissue loss."

### Treatment (pp. 7-8)
1. **Do not thaw if it might refreeze** (p. 8). MSD: tissue that is thawed and "refrozen, will be more severely damaged than if left frozen", so if the patient must walk out, thawing may wait.
2. **Do not rub** the area or rewarm it at a fire or stove; numb skin burns easily (p. 8).
3. **Rewarm fast in circulating water at 37-39 °C** with an antiseptic (p. 8). Her notes give 10-30 minutes; MSD gives "15 to 30 minutes" and "water that is tolerably warm to the touch (37 to 39° C)."
4. **Pain control** (NSAIDs, opioids) (p. 8). MSD adds that **ibuprofen** is used because blocking prostaglandins may reduce vasoconstriction and ischemia.
5. **Severe cases (grade 2 and higher):** tPA or heparin when amputation is a risk, the vasodilator **iloprost**, and antibiotics (p. 8). MSD: a prostacyclin analog such as iloprost "is indicated after rewarming" for severe injury seen within 48-72 hours; thrombolytics "should be considered within the first 24 hours."
6. **Imaging:** technetium-99m bone scan shows which tissue still has blood flow (p. 7).
7. **Surgery:** escharotomy, fasciotomy for compartment syndrome, debridement (p. 8). MSD: amputation "is usually delayed until definitive demarcation of necrotic tissue occurs."

**Recent change (iloprost approved for frostbite):** On 14 Feb 2024 the FDA approved **Aurlumyn (iloprost) injection**, "a vasodilator (a drug that opens blood vessels)", "to treat severe frostbite in adults to reduce the risk of finger or toe amputation". It was the first drug approved for frostbite. In the trial, **0 of 16** patients given iloprost alone had the day-7 bone scan finding that predicts amputation, compared with **60%** of patients who got neither iloprost nor another drug ([FDA press release, 14 Feb 2024](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-medication-treat-severe-frostbite)). Iloprost copies **prostacyclin (PGI2)**, which widens vessels and stops platelets from clumping (standard teaching).

## Sunburn (pp. 8-9)
- **Mechanism:** UV light makes **thymine dimers** in DNA. Within an hour, mast cells release histamine, serotonin and TNF (tumor necrosis factor), followed by prostaglandins, leukotrienes and white cells (p. 8).
- Melanin production then rises and absorbs UV, acting as a sunscreen (p. 8).
- Repeated sunburn raises the risk of melanoma, BCC and SCC (p. 9).
- **Treatment:** cool baths, moisturizers, NSAIDs, hydrocortisone cream. **Prevention:** avoid 10 AM-4 PM sun, cover up, use broad-spectrum sunscreen (p. 9).

## Actinic keratosis: the precancer (pp. 1-2)
- **What it is:** years of **UVB** damage to keratinocytes (for example **p53 mutations**) cause **small, rough, scaly patches** that feel like **sandpaper**, on the face, scalp and arms (p. 1).
- **Histology:** atypical cells through the epidermis, with nuclei kept in the top dead layer (**parakeratosis**) (p. 2).
- **Risk:** fair skin, older age, immune suppression, albinism, xeroderma pigmentosum (p. 1).
- **Danger:** it can turn into **SCC**. Thickening, tenderness or bleeding in an AK suggests that change (p. 1).
- **Treatment:** 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, cryotherapy with liquid nitrogen, photodynamic therapy, scraping or excision (pp. 1-2).

**Other source (how often AK becomes SCC):** her notes say 0.03-20% (p. 1). MSD says estimates per lesion "most often range from < 1% to 10%" and lists cryosurgery, 5-FU, imiquimod, **diclofenac gel**, **tirbanibulin ointment** and photodynamic therapy ([MSD: Actinic Keratoses, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/reactions-to-sunlight/actinic-keratoses)).

**Recent change (ingenol mebutate withdrawn in Europe):** her notes list **ingenol mebutate** as an AK cream (p. 2). In April 2020 the EMA concluded that "the risks of the medicine outweigh its benefits". Picato "may increase the risk of skin cancer", with more skin cancers, "especially squamous cell carcinoma", in treated areas than with imiquimod. Its EU authorization was withdrawn on 11 Feb 2020 ([EMA, 17 Apr 2020](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/ema-review-picato-concludes-medicines-risks-outweigh-its-benefits)). MSD's current AK treatment list does not include it.

## Skin cancers (pp. 24-31)

### Side-by-side
| | **BCC** | **SCC** | **Melanoma** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cell of origin | Basal cells of the epidermis (p. 25) | Keratinocytes (p. 29) | **Melanocytes** (p. 26) |
| How common | **Most common** skin cancer (p. 25; MSD) | **Second** most common (p. 29; MSD) | Less common, most deadly (standard teaching) |
| Classic look | **Pearly** papule with **telangiectasias** (dilated vessels); may ulcerate in the center (p. 25) | Rough scaly nodule that ulcerates, crusts and bleeds (p. 30) | **ABCDE**: Asymmetry, Border, Color, Diameter, change over time (p. 27) |
| Histology | **Islands of basal cells with peripheral palisading** and clefting (pp. 25-26) | **Keratin pearls**, eosinophilic cells (p. 31) | Atypical melanocytes; thickness measured as **Breslow depth** (p. 24) |
| Spread | **Rarely metastasizes**, but can destroy tissue locally (p. 25) | **Can metastasize** (p. 30) | Metastasizes to nodes, skin, lung, liver, brain (p. 27) |
| Gene or syndrome | **Gorlin syndrome (PTCH1)** (p. 25) | Precursor: **AK**; **Bowen disease** = SCC in situ (p. 29) | **BRAF**, CDKN2A, MC1R (p. 27) |

### Basal cell carcinoma (pp. 25-26)
- **Types:** nodular, superficial, infiltrative, micronodular, morpheaform (p. 25). **Other source:** MSD gives the shares as nodular about 51%, superficial about 34%, morpheaform and infiltrative 5-10%, and calls the last two high-risk ([MSD: Basal Cell Carcinoma, updated Mar 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/cancers-of-the-skin/basal-cell-carcinoma)).
- **Risk:** UV light, **arsenic**, immune deficiency, fair skin, albinism, xeroderma pigmentosum (p. 25).
- **Gorlin syndrome** (basal cell nevus syndrome) causes many BCCs through **PTCH1** mutations (p. 25). MSD: Gorlin is "an autosomal dominant disorder". **PTCH1** normally holds back the **Hedgehog** pathway, so losing it turns the pathway on (standard teaching).
- **Treatment:** curettage and electrodesiccation, excision, cryosurgery, **Mohs surgery**; topical 5-FU or imiquimod; radiation or photodynamic therapy (p. 26).

**Other source (drugs for advanced BCC):** MSD lists the **Hedgehog inhibitors vismodegib or sonidegib** for advanced disease, and **cemiplimab**, a PD-1 inhibitor, for patients who cannot tolerate them. Her notes do not list drugs for advanced BCC. Vismodegib and sonidegib block **Smoothened**, the protein that PTCH1 normally restrains (standard teaching).

### Melanoma (pp. 24, 26-28)
- **Growth phases (p. 27):** **radial** (< 1 mm, spreads sideways, rarely metastasizes) → **vertical** (> 1 mm, grows down into the dermis and can metastasize). Depth is what matters.
- **Types (p. 26):** superficial spreading, lentigo maligna, **nodular (most dangerous; grows down from the start)**, acral lentiginous.
- **Spotting it (p. 27):** **ABCDE** for classic melanoma; **EFG** (Elevated, Firm, Growing) for nodular melanoma.
- **Causes and risks (p. 27):** UV light (especially blistering sunburns early in life, tanning beds), many moles, family history, immune suppression, dysplastic nevus syndrome, xeroderma pigmentosum, albinism; mutations in **BRAF**, CDKN2A, MC1R.
- **Staging (p. 28):** T stage depends on thickness: T1 ≤ 1 mm, T2 > 1-2 mm, T3 > 2-4 mm, T4 > 4 mm. **Sentinel node biopsy** stages the nodes.
- **Treatment (p. 28):** wide excision with 1-3 cm margins depending on depth; node dissection if the sentinel node is positive; immunotherapy for metastases.

**Other source (melanoma details):** from [MSD: Melanoma, updated Apr 2026](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/cancers-of-the-skin/melanoma).
- **Superficial spreading** is the most common type ("70% of melanomas", often with **BRAF V600** mutations). Lentigo maligna is 15% and nodular about 5%.
- **Acral lentiginous** is only about 1% overall, but it is the **most common type in people with dark skin** (palms, soles, under the nails).
- MSD writes the criteria as "D: Diameter—> 6 mm" and "E: **Evolution**—a new mole in a patient > 30 years of age or a changing mole". Her notes say "Diameter: 6 mm" and "Enlargement" (p. 27).
- **Margins:** "a 1-cm lateral tumor-free margin is adequate for lesions < 0.8 mm thick"; thicker lesions may need larger margins.
- **Sentinel node biopsy** for "Breslow depth 1.0–4.0 mm (stages II-III) without clinically positive nodes". MSD also recommends node dissection when the sentinel node is positive, as her notes say.
- **Survival:** her table gives 5-year survival by Breslow depth (in situ or < 1 mm 95-100%, 1-2 mm 80-96%, 2.1-4 mm 60-75%; p. 24). MSD gives it by spread: **99.6%** localized, **73.9%** regional, **35.1%** distant.
- **Gorlin syndrome:** her melanoma risk list includes Gorlin syndrome (p. 27). MSD's melanoma risk list does not, and MSD describes Gorlin as the syndrome of **many basal cell carcinomas**. On the exam, **Gorlin = BCC**.

**Recent change (melanoma drugs):** her notes say only "immunotherapy in case of metastases" (p. 28). MSD now lists immune checkpoint inhibitors: **anti-PD-1** (pembrolizumab, nivolumab), **anti-CTLA-4** (ipilimumab) and **anti-LAG-3** (**relatlimab**, given with nivolumab). Nivolumab plus ipilimumab is typically preferred. **BRAF plus MEK inhibitors** are used together for BRAF-mutant disease. MSD also says "Pre-surgical administration (neoadjuvant therapy) of PD-1 inhibitors are recommended in selected patients."

### Squamous cell carcinoma (pp. 29-31)
- **Causes:** UV light, **HPV**, **AK** as the precursor, **Bowen disease** (SCC in situ) (p. 29).
- **Risks:** **immune suppression** (for example transplant patients), chronic UV, fair skin, albinism, xeroderma pigmentosum, **tobacco** (lip and mouth SCC), **arsenic** (p. 29).
- **Look:** on sun-exposed head and neck; a scaly nodule that ulcerates, crusts and bleeds easily (p. 30).
- **Treatment:** excision, **Mohs surgery**, electrodesiccation and curettage; topical immune modulators; radiation, chemotherapy, photodynamic therapy (p. 30).

**Other source (SCC):** MSD says SCC can arise "in a preexisting actinic keratosis," "in a patch of oral leukoplakia," or "in a burn scar"; SCCs "are also more aggressive and likely to metastasize than basal cell carcinomas"; tumors "near the ear or vermilion border" (the lip edge) are more aggressive; and Mohs "is preferred for high-risk lesions" ([MSD: Squamous Cell Carcinoma, updated Mar 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/oncology/cancers-of-the-skin/squamous-cell-carcinoma)).

**Recent change (drugs for advanced SCC):** MSD: the PD-1 inhibitors **cemiplimab and pembrolizumab** "are reserved for patients with inoperable, advanced, or metastatic disease." Her notes list chemotherapy (p. 30).

## Pigment disorders (pp. 38-42)

### The key contrast
| | **Albinism** | **Vitiligo** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melanocytes | **Normal number**, little or no melanin | **Destroyed** (absent) |
| Cause | **Inherited** enzyme or transporter defect | **Autoimmune** (p. 41) |
| Onset | From birth | Acquired, patches grow over time |
| Eyes | Nystagmus, poor vision, light sensitivity | Not affected (standard teaching) |
| Pattern | Whole body (OCA) or eyes only (OA) | Sharp-edged white patches, often hands and face |

MSD on albinism: "a normal number of melanocytes are present but melanin production is absent or greatly decreased" ([MSD: Albinism, full review Aug 2024](https://msdmanuals.com/en-pt/professional/dermatologic-disorders/pigmentation-disorders/albinism)). MSD on vitiligo: proposed mechanisms include "autoimmune destruction of melanocytes" ([MSD: Vitiligo, updated Aug 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/pigmentation-disorders/vitiligo)).

### Albinism (pp. 39-40)
- **Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA):** skin, eyes and hair; **autosomal recessive** (p. 39). **OCA1 = tyrosinase (TYR) gene defect**; **OCA2 = P gene** defect (a membrane transporter for tyrosine) (p. 39). MSD: TYR mutations give "absent (OCA1A) or reduced (OCA1B) tyrosinase activity."
- **Ocular albinism (OA1):** eyes only; **X-linked recessive** (p. 39).
- **Eyes:** melanin guides how the visual pathway develops, so patients have **abnormal crossing of optic nerve fibers at the chiasm**, **foveal hypoplasia** (poor acuity), **nystagmus** and severe light sensitivity (p. 39). MSD adds strabismus.
- **Skin:** high risk of sunburn and skin cancer (p. 39). MSD: "especially squamous cell carcinoma."
- **Related syndromes:**
  - **Chédiak-Higashi:** caused by the **LYST** (lysosomal trafficking regulator) gene, with partial albinism (p. 39). MSD adds silvery-gray hair and "severe immunodeficiency due to abnormal lymphocyte lytic granules". MSD also names a bleeding diathesis from fewer platelet dense granules. Giant granules in white cells are standard teaching.
  - **Hermansky-Pudlak:** albinism-like findings "with platelet abnormalities" (MSD).
- **Treatment:** no cure; sun protection, eye care, glasses, surgery for strabismus or nystagmus (p. 40).

### Vitiligo (p. 41)
- **What it is:** autoimmune destruction of melanocytes → **sharp-edged white patches**, usually on the arms and legs (p. 41); MSD describes depigmented skin as chalk-white under a Wood light.
- **Types (p. 41):** **segmental** (one side, follows a nerve root band, stable) and **non-segmental** (symmetric, new patches keep appearing; "generalized" is the most common form).
- **Associated autoimmune disease (p. 41):** Hashimoto thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, lupus, celiac disease, Addison disease. **Other source:** MSD says vitiligo "affects up to 2% of the population" and up to 30% of patients have other autoimmune antibodies or endocrine diseases. "The strongest association is with Graves disease and Hashimoto thyroiditis."
- **Wood lamp:** her notes say lesions "turn blue" (p. 41). **Other source:** MSD says lesions are accentuated under a Wood light (365 nm) and depigmented skin looks **chalk-white**.
- **Treatment (p. 41):** topical steroids, calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus), phototherapy, camouflage makeup. MSD: potent topical steroids are traditional first-line therapy; **narrowband UVB** is often preferred for widespread disease; for near-total vitiligo, the remaining pigment can be removed with "20% monobenzyl ether of hydroquinone."

**Recent change (JAK inhibitor cream):** **ruxolitinib cream (Opzelura)** is FDA-approved "for the topical treatment of nonsegmental vitiligo in adult and pediatric patients 12 years of age and older", applied to no more than **10% of body surface area**. Ruxolitinib "inhibits JAK1 and JAK2" ([Opzelura prescribing information, rev. 6/2026, via Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/pro/opzelura-cream.html)). The vitiligo approval came on **18 Jul 2022** ([Drugs.com approval history](https://www.drugs.com/history/opzelura.html)). MSD says depigmentation can return after JAK inhibitors are stopped. The label carries a boxed warning for serious infections, death, cancer, major cardiovascular events and clots.

### Pityriasis alba (p. 40)
- Ill-defined, slightly **scaly, pale patches** on the face, neck and arms of **children with atopy** (eczema); probably pale skin left after eczema-like inflammation (p. 40).
- More visible after sun exposure; often fades after puberty (p. 40).
- Treatment: hydrocortisone, calcineurin inhibitors, moisturizers (p. 40).
- How to tell it from vitiligo: pityriasis alba is **pale but not chalk-white, slightly scaly, with fuzzy borders**; vitiligo is **completely white with sharp borders** (standard teaching).

## Clinical vignette
A 66-year-old man who worked outdoors for 40 years has many rough, scaly, sandpaper-like spots on his scalp and forehead (**actinic keratoses**, p. 1). One spot on his lower lip has become a firm, crusted, bleeding nodule. Biopsy shows invasive atypical keratinocytes with **keratin pearls**: **squamous cell carcinoma** arising in sun-damaged skin (pp. 29-31). MSD rates lip and near-ear tumors as more aggressive, so **Mohs surgery** is preferred. On his nose is a separate **pearly papule with telangiectasias**, a **BCC**, which almost never spreads but can destroy the nose if ignored (p. 25).

## Common traps
- **Parkland uses %TBSA as a whole number** and **leaves out superficial (1st degree) burns**. Half goes in the first 8 hours.
- **Painful blisters = superficial partial thickness; painless leathery = full thickness.** Pain means nerves are still alive.
- **Electrical burns:** the skin wound underestimates the deep damage. Check the heart and watch for compartment syndrome.
- **Chemical burns:** brush powder off **before** water; **alkali is worse than acid**.
- **Frostbite:** never thaw if it might refreeze; never rub; rewarm in **37-39 °C water**. Wait for demarcation before amputating.
- **AK → SCC, not BCC.** Bowen disease is SCC in situ.
- **Gorlin syndrome (PTCH1, Hedgehog) → many BCCs.** Vismodegib targets this pathway.
- **Melanoma prognosis is about depth** (Breslow thickness), not width. Nodular melanoma grows down early; superficial spreading is the most common type; acral lentiginous is most common in dark skin.
- **SCC metastasizes more than BCC**, especially on the lip, near the ear and in transplant patients.
- **Albinism = normal melanocyte number, no melanin; vitiligo = melanocytes destroyed.**
- **Vitiligo → check the thyroid.**

## Practice questions

1. A 35-year-old man weighing 70 kg is brought to the emergency department after a house fire. He has blistered and leathery burns covering 30% of his body surface area, plus red, painful, unblistered skin over another 10%. According to the Parkland formula, how much IV crystalloid should he receive during the first 8 hours after the burn?
   - **Answer:** **4,200 mL**.
   - **Why:** count only partial- and full-thickness burns (30%, not 40%): 4 mL × 70 kg × 30 = 8,400 mL in 24 hours, and half of that, 4,200 mL, goes in the first 8 hours (p. 5; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **5,600 mL**, which comes from wrongly counting the superficial 10% (4 × 70 × 40 = 11,200; half is 5,600).

2. A 28-year-old woman spills boiling water on her forearm. The area is red, moist and very painful, with several clear fluid-filled blisters. Pressing on the red skin makes it turn white, and the color returns quickly. What is the deepest layer injured?
   - **Answer:** **the papillary (superficial) dermis**: a superficial partial-thickness burn.
   - **Why:** blisters mean the injury is below the epidermis; intact pain and quick blanching mean the deep dermis with its nerves and vessels is spared (p. 4; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **the reticular dermis** (deep partial thickness), which looks yellow-white with **little sensation**.

3. A 30-year-old climber is found on a mountain with hard, white, numb toes on both feet. The nearest shelter is a 6-hour hike away, and he must walk there on his own feet in sub-freezing weather. What is the most appropriate management of his feet before he reaches the shelter?
   - **Answer:** **leave the toes frozen and do not rewarm them until refreezing can be prevented**.
   - **Why:** tissue that thaws and refreezes is damaged more than tissue left frozen (p. 8; MSD), and he must walk out in the cold.
   - **Trap:** **rubbing the feet or warming them by a campfire**: rubbing damages the tissue, and numb skin burns easily (p. 8).

4. A 34-year-old man is treated for frostbite of both hands 14 hours after exposure. After rapid rewarming in 38 °C water, several fingers remain cyanotic with hemorrhagic blisters, and a technetium-99m bone scan shows no blood flow to the fingertips. He receives an IV drug approved by the FDA in 2024 to lower the risk of finger amputation in severe frostbite. This drug mimics which naturally occurring substance?
   - **Answer:** **prostacyclin (PGI2)** (the drug is iloprost).
   - **Why:** iloprost is a prostacyclin analog that widens vessels and limits clotting in damaged small vessels (MSD; FDA press release; standard teaching for the platelet effect).
   - **Trap:** **plasminogen activator (tPA)**, a clot-dissolving drug that may be considered within 24 hours but is not the drug approved in 2024.

5. A 70-year-old retired farmer has a dozen rough, scaly, pink papules on his bald scalp and forehead that feel like sandpaper. A biopsy of one shows atypical keratinocytes in the lower epidermis with parakeratosis and no invasion of the dermis. If left untreated, these lesions can progress to which cancer?
   - **Answer:** **squamous cell carcinoma**.
   - **Why:** these are actinic keratoses, UV-damaged keratinocytes with p53 mutations, which can turn into SCC (pp. 1-2; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **basal cell carcinoma**, which does not come from AK.

6. A 79-year-old woman has a large, ulcerated tumor on her cheek that has invaded the bone around her eye. Biopsy shows nests of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading and clefting. She is not a candidate for surgery or radiation, and she starts oral vismodegib. Which signaling pathway does this drug block?
   - **Answer:** **the Hedgehog pathway**.
   - **Why:** this is BCC (pp. 25-26). MSD lists the Hedgehog inhibitors vismodegib and sonidegib for advanced BCC; BCCs come from overactive Hedgehog signaling, as in Gorlin syndrome with PTCH1 loss (p. 25; standard teaching for the pathway link).
   - **Trap:** **the BRAF-MEK (MAP kinase) pathway**, which is targeted in BRAF-mutant **melanoma**.

7. A 45-year-old woman has a mole on her back that has changed color and grown over 6 months. Excisional biopsy shows melanoma with a Breslow depth of 2.3 mm. She has no enlarged lymph nodes, and staging scans show no distant spread. Along with wide local excision, which procedure is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **sentinel lymph node biopsy**.
   - **Why:** MSD recommends it for melanomas 1.0-4.0 mm deep without clinically positive nodes; it stages the nodes (p. 28; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **complete lymph node dissection now**, which is done only when nodes are involved, clinically or on the sentinel biopsy (MSD).

8. A 60-year-old Black man has a 1.5-cm, irregular, dark brown-black patch on the sole of his right foot that has slowly enlarged over a year. He rarely goes in the sun. Biopsy shows malignant melanocytes. Which type of melanoma is this most likely to be?
   - **Answer:** **acral lentiginous melanoma**.
   - **Why:** it is only about 1% of melanomas overall, but it is the most common type in people with dark skin, and it appears on palms, soles and nail beds (p. 26; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **superficial spreading melanoma**, the most common type overall (70%; MSD).

9. A 58-year-old man who had a kidney transplant 10 years ago and takes tacrolimus has a 1-cm crusted, ulcerated nodule on the vermilion border of his lower lip. Biopsy shows invasive atypical keratinocytes with keratin pearls. Compared with a basal cell carcinoma, this tumor is more likely to do which of the following?
   - **Answer:** **spread to regional lymph nodes (metastasize)**.
   - **Why:** this is SCC (pp. 29-31). MSD says SCCs "are also more aggressive and likely to metastasize than basal cell carcinomas", and lip tumors are high-risk. Immune suppression raises the risk (p. 29).
   - **Trap:** **form palisading nests of basaloid cells**, which is the histology of BCC.

10. A 6-year-old girl has had very pale skin, white hair and light blue eyes since birth. She has horizontal nystagmus, squints in bright light, and has poor visual acuity. Her parents have normal pigmentation. A skin biopsy would most likely show which finding?
    - **Answer:** **a normal number of melanocytes that make little or no melanin**.
    - **Why:** this is oculocutaneous albinism, an autosomal recessive defect in melanin synthesis (for example tyrosinase in OCA1) (p. 39). MSD: "a normal number of melanocytes are present but melanin production is absent or greatly decreased."
    - **Trap:** **absent melanocytes**, the finding in vitiligo, where melanocytes are destroyed.

11. A 15-year-old boy has symmetric, sharply bordered, chalk-white patches around his mouth and on the backs of both hands that have spread over 2 years. The patches cover about 4% of his body surface area. His physician prescribes a cream approved by the FDA in 2022 for this condition. Which enzymes does this drug inhibit?
    - **Answer:** **Janus kinases 1 and 2 (JAK1 and JAK2)** (ruxolitinib cream).
    - **Why:** this is nonsegmental vitiligo (p. 41). Ruxolitinib cream is approved for nonsegmental vitiligo from age 12 on up to 10% of body surface area, and it "inhibits JAK1 and JAK2" (Opzelura label).
    - **Trap:** **calcineurin**, the target of tacrolimus and pimecrolimus, which are older vitiligo creams (p. 41; MSD).
