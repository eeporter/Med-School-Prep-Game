# Sleep physiology and sleep disorders

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the sleep-wake disorders chapter (pp. 407-470), by printed page; Osmosis HY Pathology Vol. 2, chapter 103 (pp. 743-747); Osmosis HY Physiology, brain functions and sleep (pp. 458-459); Pharmacology High Yield (PDF p. 44). Treatment facts come from the MSD Manual; newer guidance from the FDA and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM). DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied. Where another source disagrees or something has changed, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **NREM** = non-rapid eye movement; **REM** = rapid eye movement; **EEG** = electroencephalogram; **OSA** = obstructive sleep apnea; **CSA** = central sleep apnea; **AHI** = apnea-hypopnea index (events per hour of sleep); **CPAP** = continuous positive airway pressure; **MSLT** = multiple sleep latency test; **SOREMP** = sleep-onset REM period; **RBD** = REM sleep behavior disorder; **RLS** = restless legs syndrome; **CBT-I** = cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia; **CSF** = cerebrospinal fluid.

## In one line
Sleep cycles through **NREM (N1, N2, N3)** and **REM** about every 90-120 minutes. **N3 (deep, slow-wave) dominates early in the night and REM dominates late** (Osmosis Physiology p. 459; DSM p. 408). So **N3 problems (sleepwalking, sleep terrors) happen in the first third of the night**, and **REM problems (nightmares, RBD) happen in the second half** (pp. 452, 457, 461).

## Sleep stages and EEG
| Stage | EEG clue | Share of sleep (young adult) | Notes | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Awake, alert | **Beta** (14-30 Hz) | | | Osmosis Physiology p. 458 |
| Awake, relaxed (eyes closed) | **Alpha** (8-13 Hz) | | | Osmosis Physiology p. 458 |
| **N1** | Slow, irregular waves right after falling asleep (theta, 4-7 Hz, is standard teaching) | 2-5% | Lightest sleep | MSD; Osmosis Physiology p. 459 |
| **N2** | **Sleep spindles and K complexes** | **45-55%** (largest share) | | MSD |
| **N3** | **Delta waves** (< 4 Hz, high amplitude), slow-wave sleep | 13-23% | **Deep sleep**, high arousal threshold; **decreases with age** and may disappear in older adults; children have more | MSD; Osmosis Physiology pp. 458-459 |
| **REM** | **Low-voltage, fast** activity that looks like being awake ("paradoxical sleep") | 20-25% | **Muscle atonia**, rapid eye movements, **most dreaming**; heart rate, blood pressure and breathing rise; longer REM periods later in the night | MSD; Osmosis Physiology p. 459 |

- **Circadian timing:** the hypothalamus times the sleep cycle. The retina connects directly to the hypothalamus, which controls the **pineal gland's melatonin**; falling light raises melatonin and sleepiness (Osmosis Physiology p. 459). The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the master clock (standard teaching).
- **Sleep needs fall with age:** infants about 16 hours, adults 7.5-8.5 hours (Osmosis Physiology p. 459).

**Other source (stage names):** her physiology notes describe a "4-stage NREM sleep", with theta waves in stage 2 and stages 3/4 as slow-wave sleep (Osmosis Physiology p. 459). MSD uses the current scoring of **3 NREM stages (N1-N3)**, with stages 3 and 4 combined as N3. MSD marks **N2 by sleep spindles and K complexes**. On exams, answer **N2 = spindles and K complexes, N3 = delta (slow) waves**.

## Insomnia (pp. 409-410)
- Unhappy with sleep **quantity or quality**, with trouble **falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early**.
- At least **3 nights a week** for at least **3 months**, despite adequate opportunity to sleep, with distress or impaired functioning (pp. 409-410).
- **Treatment (her notes, Osmosis Vol. 2 pp. 744-745):**
  - Sleep hygiene: regular schedule, exercise, less alcohol, caffeine and smoking in the evening, no daytime naps.
  - **Stimulus control:** use the bed only for sleep; if not asleep in about 20 minutes, get up.
  - Behavior therapy.
  - Drugs: melatonin agonists, non-benzodiazepine sedatives, occasionally benzodiazepines.
- **MSD:** cognitive-behavioral strategies have effects that **last long after treatment ends**. Hypnotics give rapid relief but "must not be used indefinitely in most cases", and abrupt withdrawal can cause nervousness, tremors and seizures. **CBT-I is first-line for chronic insomnia** (standard teaching).
- **Sleep drugs in her notes (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 44):**
  - **Z-drugs** (zolpidem, zopiclone, zaleplon) act at the **benzodiazepine site** of the GABA-A receptor but are not benzodiazepines; used for sleep-onset insomnia.
  - **Ramelteon** is an MT1/MT2 melatonin agonist for sleep-onset insomnia.
  - **Tasimelteon** treats the sleep-wake disorder of **blind** people.
  - **Suvorexant** is an **orexin receptor antagonist**.

## Narcolepsy (pp. 422-426)
- **Irrepressible sleep or naps** at least **3 times a week for 3 months**, plus at least 1 of the following (p. 422):
  1. **Cataplexy:** brief, sudden loss of muscle tone on both sides with consciousness kept, **triggered by laughter or joking**.
  2. **Low CSF hypocretin-1 (orexin)** (110 pg/mL or less).
  3. Sleep testing showing **REM latency of 15 minutes or less**, or an **MSLT with mean sleep latency of 8 minutes or less and 2 or more SOREMPs**.
- **Type 1** has cataplexy or hypocretin deficiency; **type 2** has neither (p. 422).
- **Other symptoms:** **hypnagogic** (falling asleep) and **hypnopompic** (waking) **hallucinations**, **sleep paralysis**, and disturbed night sleep (MSD; Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 745).
- **Cause:**
  - Strongly linked to **HLA DQB1*06:02** (p. 426).
  - Thought to be **autoimmune destruction of hypocretin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus** (MSD).
  - Her notes: lack of orexin, so patients fall asleep faster and enter REM faster (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 745).
- **Prevalence:** type 1 affects 0.02-0.05% of adults; men and women about equally (p. 425).
- **Treatment (MSD):**
  - Scheduled short naps (under 30 minutes).
  - For sleepiness: **modafinil or armodafinil**, solriamfetol (an NE-dopamine reuptake inhibitor), or **pitolisant** (a histamine-3 inverse agonist).
  - Classic stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines) are **third or fourth line**.
  - For cataplexy in type 1: **oxybates or pitolisant**. Antidepressants were used in the past, but data are limited.

**Other source (narcolepsy drugs):** her notes list **SSRIs and stimulants (for example modafinil)** (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 745). MSD puts **modafinil, armodafinil, solriamfetol and pitolisant first** for sleepiness and **oxybates or pitolisant** for cataplexy. MSD says antidepressant data are limited and classic stimulants are later choices. On exams, **modafinil** is the safest first answer for daytime sleepiness.

## Breathing-related sleep disorders (pp. 429-435)
- **OSA:** sleep study shows **5 or more obstructive apneas or hypopneas per hour** plus snoring, gasping or breathing pauses, or daytime sleepiness. Alternatively, **15 or more per hour** even without symptoms (p. 429).
  - Severity: **mild under 15, moderate 15-30, severe over 30** (p. 429).
  - More common in **men (2-4:1)**, older adults and **obesity** (p. 431).
  - Risk factors (MSD): **obesity** (7 times more common), **neck over 43 cm in men or 41 cm in women**, short or retracted jaw, large tongue base or tonsils, **alcohol or sedatives**.
  - Consequences (MSD): **hypertension**, **atrial fibrillation**, car crashes from sleepiness.
  - **Polysomnography is the gold standard**. A negative home test in a symptomatic patient should be followed by polysomnography (MSD).
  - **Treatment (MSD):** **CPAP or an oral appliance** first-line. **Weight loss** helps but is not curative. Stop sedatives. Hypoglossal nerve stimulation is an option.
- **CSA:** **5 or more central apneas per hour** (no breathing effort, not blocked airway) (p. 435).
  - **Cheyne-Stokes breathing** is crescendo-decrescendo breathing with central apneas, typical of heart failure. DSM: heart failure is the condition most often linked to central sleep apnea (p. 434), and Cheyne-Stokes breathing tends to begin as heart failure develops (pp. 436-437).
  - CSA can occur **with opioid use** (opioids affect the medulla's breathing rhythm) (p. 435).

**Recent change (OSA drug):** on **20 Dec 2024** the FDA approved **tirzepatide (Zepbound)**, the first medication for **moderate to severe OSA in adults with obesity**, used with a reduced-calorie diet and more exercise ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-medication-obstructive-sleep-apnea)). CPAP is still first-line (MSD).

## Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders (pp. 443-450)
| Type | Pattern | Who | Treatment (MSD) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| **Advanced sleep phase** | Sleepy and awake **early** | **Older adults** (p. 446) | **Evening bright light** |
| **Non-24-hour** | Sleep times drift each day | **Blind people** (about 50% of blind people in the U.S.) (p. 449) | Tasimelteon (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 44) |
| **Shift work** | Insomnia in the sleep period, sleepiness at work | Night or rotating shifts (p. 444) | Bright light after waking; melatonin before sleep. Rotating shifts should go clockwise |
| Jet lag | | | **Eastward travel is worse** than westward |

## Parasomnias
| | **NREM arousal disorders** (sleepwalking, sleep terrors) | **Nightmare disorder** | **RBD** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stage | **N3** (MSD; p. 460) | **REM** (MSD; p. 460) | **REM without atonia** |
| Time of night | **First third** | **Second half** | **More than 90 minutes after sleep onset**, more in later sleep |
| On waking | Hard to wake, **confused**; **no or little dream recall**; **amnesia** for the episode | **Quickly oriented and alert**; **remembers** the vivid dream | **Fully awake and alert**, not confused; acts out dreams (shouting, punching, kicking) |
| Typical patient | **Children**; 10-30% of children have at least 1 sleepwalking episode (p. 454) | Any age | **Older adults** |
| Key fact | Sleep terrors: panicky scream, intense fear, **dilated pupils, fast heart rate and breathing, sweating**, can't be comforted | | **About 75% of idiopathic RBD develop a synucleinopathy (Parkinson disease, Lewy body disease, multiple system atrophy) within 10-15 years** (p. 462) |
| Treatment (MSD) | **Reassure parents** (children); protect from injury; clonazepam at bedtime if needed (sleepwalking) | | **Clonazepam** at bedtime or **melatonin**; remove sharp objects and warn the bed partner |
| DSM pages | p. 452 | p. 457 | pp. 461-462 |

Her notes: night terrors in deep NREM sleep, most common at ages 3-8, no memory of the episode, often resolve on their own (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 746).

**Correction (bedwetting and sleep stage):** her Osmosis notes say nocturnal enuresis "often occurs during REM sleep" (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 746). DSM-5-TR says nocturnal enuresis typically happens in the **first third of the night** (p. 399) and only **occasionally** during REM sleep, when the child may recall a dream about urinating (p. 400). If a question asks when bedwetting happens, answer **first third of the night, mostly NREM**, not REM.

## Restless legs syndrome (pp. 464-466)
- An **urge to move the legs**, usually with unpleasant sensations. It must be:
  - worse at **rest**;
  - **relieved by movement**;
  - worse in the **evening or night**.
- At least **3 times a week for 3 months** (pp. 464-465).
- **Links:** **iron deficiency** and abnormal iron metabolism, **dopamine** and opioid system changes, female sex, older age, family history (p. 466). Also kidney failure, pregnancy and neuropathy (MSD).
- **Treatment (MSD):**
  - Check iron and give **iron if ferritin is below 50 ng/mL**.
  - Then **gabapentin enacarbil** is the medication of choice.
  - Pregabalin helps RLS with pain.
  - **Dopamine agonists work but are not recommended long term** because of **augmentation**: MSD describes symptoms that worsen before the next dose and spread to other body parts such as the arms, and severity that rises as the dose rises. Earlier onset in the day is also part of the usual definition (standard teaching).
  - Stimulants, certain antidepressants and dopamine antagonists are associated with RLS.

**Recent change (RLS guideline):** the **AASM guideline (13 Nov 2024)** strongly recommends the **alpha-2-delta ligands (gabapentin enacarbil, gabapentin, pregabalin)** and **IV ferric carboxymaltose**. It **conditionally recommends against** dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine) and levodopa for most patients, because of augmentation ([AASM summary](https://aasm.org/summary-of-new-clinical-practice-guideline-for-rls-and-plmd/)). Older teaching named dopamine agonists first-line. For current answers, use **iron first, then an alpha-2-delta ligand**.

## Clinical vignette
A 52-year-old man with a BMI of 36 and hypertension that needs three drugs is brought in by his wife. She says he snores loudly and stops breathing for long periods at night. He wakes unrefreshed, falls asleep in meetings and nearly crashed his car last week. His neck circumference is 46 cm. Polysomnography shows an AHI of 34 obstructive events per hour.

- **Diagnosis:** **severe obstructive sleep apnea**. The AHI is over 30, and he has nighttime breathing symptoms and daytime sleepiness (p. 429).
- **Risk factors:** obesity and a thick neck (MSD).
- **Linked condition:** his hypertension (MSD).
- **Treatment:** **CPAP**, plus weight loss and avoiding alcohol and sedatives (MSD).

## Common traps
- **N2 = sleep spindles and K complexes (largest share of sleep); N3 = delta waves; REM = low-voltage fast EEG with muscle atonia** (MSD).
- **Older adults lose N3**; children have the most N3 (MSD; Osmosis Physiology p. 459).
- **Sleep terrors and sleepwalking: N3, first third of night, amnesia, confused. Nightmares: REM, second half, remembered, quickly oriented** (pp. 452, 457).
- **RBD in an older adult → watch for Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia** (p. 462).
- **Narcolepsy: cataplexy with laughter, low CSF hypocretin, short REM latency, SOREMPs. Treat sleepiness with modafinil** (p. 422; MSD).
- **OSA: AHI 5+ with symptoms or 15+ alone; CPAP first; avoid sedatives and alcohol** (p. 429; MSD).
- **Cheyne-Stokes and opioid-related breathing problems = central sleep apnea** (p. 435).
- **Adolescent night owl = delayed phase (morning light). Older adult early bird = advanced phase (evening light)** (p. 446; MSD).
- **RLS: check ferritin and give iron first; long-term dopamine agonists cause augmentation** (MSD; AASM 2024).
- **Insomnia: 3 nights a week for 3 months; CBT-I first, hypnotics short term** (pp. 409-410; MSD).

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 68-year-old woman has had trouble falling asleep 4-5 nights a week for 8 months since retiring. She lies in bed watching TV and worrying about sleep, naps in the afternoon and has had two falls this year. She has no depression or sleep apnea symptoms. Which is the most appropriate initial treatment?
   - **Answer:** **cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia** (stimulus control, limiting time in bed, sleep hygiene).
   - **Why:** insomnia at least 3 nights a week for 3 months (pp. 409-410). Cognitive-behavioral strategies have effects that last after treatment ends, and hypnotics should not be used indefinitely (MSD). CBT-I first-line is standard teaching. Stimulus control and avoiding naps are in her notes (Osmosis Vol. 2 pp. 744-745).
   - **Trap:** **zolpidem nightly**, a hypnotic for short-term use (MSD). It is also a poor choice in a woman who is already falling, because sedative hypnotics raise fall risk in older adults (standard teaching).

2. A 19-year-old college student falls asleep in every lecture despite 8 hours of sleep. When she laughs at a joke, her knees buckle and her jaw sags for a few seconds while she stays fully aware. She sometimes sees vivid figures as she falls asleep. Deficiency of which neuropeptide most likely explains her symptoms?
   - **Answer:** **hypocretin (orexin)**.
   - **Why:** daytime sleep attacks with cataplexy triggered by laughter = narcolepsy type 1, with low CSF hypocretin-1 (p. 422). Hypocretin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus are thought to be destroyed (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **melatonin**, the pineal hormone that signals darkness (Osmosis Physiology p. 459), not the cause of narcolepsy.

3. A 24-year-old man with narcolepsy type 2 (no cataplexy) still falls asleep while studying despite scheduled 20-minute afternoon naps. Which drug is the most appropriate first choice for his daytime sleepiness?
   - **Answer:** **modafinil** (or armodafinil).
   - **Why:** MSD lists modafinil, armodafinil, solriamfetol and pitolisant before classic stimulants, which are third or fourth line.
   - **Trap:** **mixed amphetamine salts as first-line**.

4. A 70-year-old man with heart failure (ejection fraction 25%) has a sleep study. It shows breathing that waxes and wanes in a crescendo-decrescendo pattern, with pauses that have no chest or abdominal effort, about 20 times per hour. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **central sleep apnea with Cheyne-Stokes breathing**.
   - **Why:** 5 or more central apneas per hour with periodic crescendo-decrescendo breathing (p. 435). Central sleep apnea is seen most commonly in heart failure (p. 434), and Cheyne-Stokes onset is tied to heart failure (p. 437).
   - **Trap:** **obstructive sleep apnea**, in which breathing effort continues against a blocked airway (p. 429).

5. A 5-year-old boy wakes about an hour after bedtime screaming, sweating, with a racing heart and wide pupils. He does not respond to his parents' comforting and falls back asleep after 10 minutes. In the morning he remembers nothing. Which is the most appropriate management?
   - **Answer:** **reassure the parents** (keep a regular bedtime routine and a safe environment).
   - **Why:** sleep terrors are an NREM arousal disorder: first third of the night, autonomic arousal, unresponsiveness and amnesia (p. 452). They arise from N3, and parental reassurance is the mainstay in children (MSD); they often resolve on their own (Osmosis Vol. 2 p. 746).
   - **Trap:** **treating as nightmare disorder**: nightmares come from REM in the second half of the night, and the child wakes oriented and remembers the dream (p. 457).

6. A 66-year-old man's wife says that for 2 years he has shouted and punched in his sleep in the early morning hours, once striking her. When woken, he is immediately alert and describes dreaming he was fighting an intruder. Polysomnography shows REM sleep without atonia. He is at greatest long-term risk for which condition?
   - **Answer:** **a synucleinopathy such as Parkinson disease** (or Lewy body dementia or multiple system atrophy).
   - **Why:** RBD; about 75% of idiopathic cases develop a neurodegenerative synucleinopathy within 10-15 years (pp. 461-462).
   - **Trap:** **Alzheimer disease**, which is not a synucleinopathy.

7. A 45-year-old woman has an uncomfortable crawling feeling in her legs every evening while watching TV, with an urge to move that eases when she walks. It happens most nights and has lasted 6 months. She has heavy periods. Her ferritin is 28 ng/mL. Which is the most appropriate first treatment?
   - **Answer:** **iron supplementation**.
   - **Why:** RLS (pp. 464-465) is linked to iron deficiency (p. 466). MSD gives iron when ferritin is below 50 ng/mL. The AASM 2024 guideline recommends iron studies for everyone with RLS and iron supplementation based on the iron results.
   - **Trap:** **pramipexole**, a dopamine agonist that can cause augmentation and is conditionally recommended against for most patients (MSD; AASM 2024).

8. A 61-year-old man with RLS has taken ropinirole for 3 years. His symptoms now start in the early afternoon instead of at bedtime, are more intense, and have spread to his arms. Iron studies are normal. Which is the most appropriate change in therapy?
   - **Answer:** **switch to an alpha-2-delta ligand** (gabapentin enacarbil, gabapentin or pregabalin) and taper the dopamine agonist.
   - **Why:** this is augmentation from long-term dopamine agonist use. MSD calls gabapentin enacarbil the medication of choice after iron, with much less augmentation. The AASM (2024) strongly recommends alpha-2-delta ligands.
   - **Trap:** **raising the ropinirole dose**, which worsens augmentation.

9. A 16-year-old boy can't fall asleep before 2 a.m. and struggles to wake for school at 6:30 a.m., but on weekends he sleeps from 2 a.m. to 11 a.m. and feels rested. Which is the most appropriate treatment?
   - **Answer:** **bright light exposure in the morning** (with low-dose melatonin several hours before the desired bedtime).
   - **Why:** delayed sleep phase type is most common in adolescents (pp. 443-444). MSD recommends morning bright light and melatonin 4-5 hours before the desired bedtime.
   - **Trap:** **evening bright light**, which treats advanced sleep phase in older adults (MSD).

10. A 47-year-old man who has been totally blind since a car crash finds that his sleepiness drifts later every day, so he is wide awake at night for weeks at a time. Which drug is designed for his condition?
    - **Answer:** **tasimelteon** (a melatonin receptor agonist).
    - **Why:** non-24-hour sleep-wake type is most common in blind people (p. 449), and her notes list tasimelteon for the sleep-wake disorder of the blind (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 44).
    - **Trap:** **suvorexant**, an orexin receptor antagonist for insomnia (PDF p. 44).

11. A sleep technician reviews an overnight study of a healthy 25-year-old. The stage that makes up about half of total sleep time shows bursts of rhythmic 12-14 Hz waves lasting about 1 second and occasional single high-amplitude sharp waves. Which stage is this?
    - **Answer:** **N2**.
    - **Why:** N2 is marked by sleep spindles and K complexes and is 45-55% of sleep in young adults (MSD). The spindle frequency is standard teaching.
    - **Trap:** **N3**, marked by high-amplitude delta (slow) waves (Osmosis Physiology p. 458).

12. A 40-year-old woman takes zolpidem for trouble falling asleep. At which receptor site does the drug act?
    - **Answer:** **the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor**.
    - **Why:** Z-drugs act on benzodiazepine receptors but are not benzodiazepines (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 44).
    - **Trap:** **the MT1/MT2 melatonin receptor**, the target of ramelteon (PDF p. 44).
