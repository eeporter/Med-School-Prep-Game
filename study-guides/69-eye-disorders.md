## In one line
**Aqueous is made by the ciliary body and drains through the trabecular meshwork; block the drain and the optic nerve dies (glaucoma).** **Red eye: watery + node = adenovirus; contact lens + ulcer = Pseudomonas; dendrite = HSV (no steroids); painful eye movements + proptosis = orbital cellulitis.** **Retina: ischemia → VEGF → new vessels (diabetes, prematurity, wet AMD), so anti-VEGF injections treat all three.** **White pupil in a toddler = retinoblastoma (RB1, chromosome 13)** (Osmosis pp. 585-609; Osmosis Physiology pp. 434-440).

## Eye anatomy and physiology (Osmosis Physiology pp. 434-440)
- **Tears** (p. 434): lacrimal gland (parasympathetic fibers from **CN VII**) → puncta → canaliculi → lacrimal sac → **nasolacrimal duct → inferior nasal meatus**. Your notes say tears contain "lysosomes"; this is most likely a typo for **lysozyme**, the antibacterial enzyme in tears (standard teaching).
- **Aqueous humor** (p. 435): made by the **ciliary processes** → posterior chamber → through the pupil → anterior chamber → **trabecular meshwork → canal of Schlemm** → veins. IOP depends on the balance between production and drainage.
- **Cornea** (p. 436): the main refracting surface; avascular. The **endothelium** pumps water out with sodium pumps to keep the cornea clear.
- **Correction (cornea layers):** your notes say the corneal epithelium is "derived from neural crest cells" and that Descemet membrane separates the epithelium from Bowman layer and holds the epithelial stem cells (p. 436).
  - Your own embryology page says the corneal epithelium comes from **surface ectoderm** (p. 260). A 2025 anatomy review agrees: epithelium from surface ectoderm; **stroma (keratocytes) and endothelium from neural crest**.
  - The same review: **Descemet membrane is the basement membrane of the endothelium** (the back layer), and corneal epithelial **stem cells live in the limbus**, the cornea-sclera junction (Ozkan and Utine 2025).
- **Uvea** = choroid, ciliary body, iris (p. 436). **Sphincter pupillae** constricts (parasympathetic); **dilator pupillae** dilates (sympathetic).
- **Retina** (p. 437): RPE stores vitamin A for photoreceptors. **Rods** = dim light, no color, absent from the fovea; **cones** = color and sharp vision; the **fovea** has only cones. The **optic disc** is the blind spot.
- **Blood supply** (p. 438): choroidal vessels feed the outer third; the central retinal artery feeds the inner two-thirds.
- **Phototransduction** (p. 439): light turns **11-cis retinal → all-trans retinal** → transducin → phosphodiesterase lowers **cGMP** → sodium channels close → the photoreceptor **hyperpolarizes** and releases less glutamate.
- **Near vision** (p. 439): **accommodation** (parasympathetic → ciliary muscle contracts → zonule fibers relax → the lens rounds up), **pupil constriction**, **convergence** (CN III). Distant vision: ciliary muscle relaxed, lens flat.
- **Visual pathway** (pp. 437, 438, 440): nasal retinal fibers (temporal visual field) **cross at the chiasm**; tract → lateral geniculate nucleus → optic radiations → occipital cortex.
- Pharm HY (PDF 13): **M3 receptors** on the sphincter pupillae; pilocarpine and ecothiophate cause **miosis**.

## Red eye: conjunctivitis, keratitis and corneal ulcer (Osmosis pp. 588-591, 602)

| --- | --- | --- |

- **Other source (MSD, viral conjunctivitis):** adenovirus causes "up to 90%". Treatment is mainly **cool compresses**; no antibiotics (secondary infection "is very rare"). Your notes list topical glucocorticoids for EKC (p. 589); MSD says only patients with **severe photophobia or reduced vision** "may benefit", usually prescribed by an ophthalmologist, **after ruling out HSV keratitis**, which steroids worsen. Hygiene: hand washing, no shared towels, no pools, keep young children home.
- **Newborn conjunctivitis** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 104, 164; MSD): chemical within 6-8 hours of drops; **gonococcal at 2-5 days**; **chlamydial at 5-14 days** (the most common cause, p. 164). Erythromycin ointment prevents gonococcal disease but not chlamydial; chlamydial disease gets **systemic** treatment because of pneumonia. Details are in guides 64 and 66.
- **Keratitis** (pp. 590-591) = inflamed cornea: pain, photophobia, foreign-body feeling, blurred vision.
  - Causes: bacteria (*S. aureus*, ***Pseudomonas***), HSV and zoster, fungi (*Fusarium*, *Aspergillus*), ***Acanthamoeba*** (Wessely ring).
  - **Contact lenses** (overnight wear, poor hygiene) are the big risk; El-Husseiny Micro p. 13 lists *P. aeruginosa* biofilm in **contact lens keratitis**.
  - Fluorescein stains lost epithelium green; a **dendritic** ulcer means HSV (p. 602); **Seidel sign** (aqueous leaking) means a penetrating injury (p. 591).
- **Corneal ulcer** (p. 602): open sore with stromal loss; **hypopyon** if severe; risks include contact lenses, abrasions, dry eye, steroid drops, **vitamin A deficiency**.
- **Other source (MSD, corneal ulcer, June 2026):** *Pseudomonas* ulcers "develop rapidly, causing deep and extensive corneal necrosis", mostly in contact lens wearers; fungal ulcers follow "trauma with vegetable material"; *Acanthamoeba* follows contaminated water with contact lenses. Start **empiric topical antibiotics** (moxifloxacin or gatifloxacin; fortified tobramycin + cefazolin for large ulcers). **Patching is contraindicated.** Your notes add steroid drops after the infection is treated (p. 602); MSD uses steroids only selectively and never with suspected HSV or marked thinning.
- **Other source (MSD, HSV keratitis):** topical ganciclovir or trifluridine, or oral acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir. "Topical glucocorticoids are **contraindicated** in epithelial keratitis." Repeated attacks can reduce corneal sensation.

## Orbital vs preseptal cellulitis (Osmosis pp. 591-594)
| | **Preseptal (periorbital)** | **Orbital** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | In front of the orbital septum (eyelid only) | Behind the septum: muscles and fat (not the globe) |

Sources: Osmosis pp. 591-594; MSD agrees on these features.
- **Other source (MSD, April 2026):** orbital cellulitis means **hospital admission and IV antibiotics** at meningitis doses (for example ceftriaxone or cefotaxime; vancomycin for MRSA risk after trauma), with drainage for vision loss, abscess, foreign body or no response. Preseptal: oral **amoxicillin-clavulanate** is an option "if orbital cellulitis has been definitively excluded", adding MRSA cover where it is common. CT or MRI if the findings are unclear.

## Uveitis and chorioretinitis (Osmosis pp. 587, 596-597)
- **Uveitis** = inflamed iris, ciliary body or choroid. **Anterior** (iritis) is the most common type (p. 596).
- Signs: pain, **photophobia**, **ciliary flush** (redness around the cornea), **keratic precipitates** (white cells on the back of the cornea), **hypopyon**, sluggish pupil from **posterior synechiae** (iris stuck to the lens) (p. 597).
- Causes: TB, syphilis, CMV, HSV, toxoplasmosis, candidiasis; **Crohn disease, ankylosing spondylitis**; trauma (p. 597). NBME TC p. 386: a young man with back pain and "burning in his eyes" → **anterior uveitis**.
- Complications: glaucoma, synechiae, band keratopathy, **cataract** (p. 597).
- Treatment (p. 597): **corticosteroids** (drops, injections, implants, systemic); **mydriatic-cycloplegic drops** to prevent posterior synechiae; immunomodulators or TNF inhibitors if steroids fail; antivirals for viral uveitis.
- **Other source (MSD):** spondyloarthropathies are "a common cause of anterior uveitis", most often with **ankylosing spondylitis** and also reactive arthritis; it is "classically unilateral" and recurs, and "most patients ... are **HLA-B27** positive". **Juvenile idiopathic arthritis** gives a chronic, often **painless** bilateral uveitis, so children with JIA are screened regularly. Sarcoidosis causes up to 25% of uveitis; Behçet disease gives hypopyon uveitis with oral and genital ulcers (HLA-B51). MSD lists **adalimumab** and infliximab for severe cases.
- **Chorioretinitis** (= posterior uveitis, p. 587): floaters, blurred vision. Causes: **toxoplasmosis** (scars), CMV, TB, syphilis, *Candida* (cotton-wool infiltrates), sarcoidosis, Behçet. Congenital toxoplasmosis (triad with hydrocephalus and intracranial calcifications, El-Husseiny Micro p. 207) and congenital CMV ("most common eye-related problem", p. 257) both cause it; **CMV retinitis** is the classic AIDS eye infection (El-Husseiny Micro p. 258).

## Glaucoma (Osmosis pp. 604-605; Pharm HY PDF 13)
Aqueous cannot drain → IOP rises (over 21 mm Hg) → the optic nerve rim thins (**cupping, cup-to-disc ratio over 0.5**) → **peripheral** field loss first, central vision late (p. 604).

| | **Open-angle** | **Acute angle-closure** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem | Angle open; outflow through the meshwork reduced | Iris blocks the angle; IOP rises fast |
| Who | Older age, **Black patients**, family history | Older age, family history, **Asian descent**, women, **hyperopia**, **mydriatic drops** (p. 604) |
| Symptoms | **None** until late field loss | **Severe eye pain, red eye, blurred vision, halos, headache, nausea**, **fixed mid-dilated pupil**, cloudy cornea (p. 604) |
| Treatment | Drops; laser; trabeculectomy (p. 605) | **Emergency** drops + IV/oral drugs, then **laser peripheral iridotomy** (p. 605) |

- **Normal-tension glaucoma** (p. 604): nerve damage with a normal IOP. **Other source (MSD):** in "at least one-third" of glaucoma patients the IOP is in the average range, so a normal pressure does not rule glaucoma out. MSD's open-angle risk list adds **myopia**, thin corneas, diabetes and hypertension; Black patients have earlier, more severe disease.
- **Other source (MSD, angle-closure, Mar 2025):** IOP is usually 40-80 mm Hg. Triggers include **dilating drops** and drugs with anticholinergic effects. Emergency treatment: **timolol, pilocarpine, brimonidine** drops, **acetazolamide** (oral or IV), and an osmotic agent such as **mannitol** if needed; pilocarpine works poorly while IOP is above 40-50. Then **LPI in both eyes**, because the risk of an attack in the other eye "is 80%". Pharm HY (PDF 51) lists **acute glaucoma** as a side effect of **topiramate**.

**Antiglaucoma drugs** (Pharm HY PDF 13; MSD)
| Class (example) | How it lowers IOP | Side effects to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Prostaglandin analogs** (latanoprost, bimatoprost, travoprost; Pharm HY PDF 165) | ↑ **uveoscleral outflow** | **Darker iris**, longer eyelashes (Pharm HY PDF 13; MSD); Pharm HY also lists macular edema and periorbital fat changes |
| **β-blockers** (timolol; betaxolol) | ↓ aqueous production | **Bronchospasm, bradycardia** (MSD); betaxolol is "safe in asthmatics" (Pharm HY PDF 26) |
| **α2-agonists** (brimonidine, apraclonidine) | ↓ production (and ↑ uveoscleral outflow) | **CNS depression in infants** (Pharm HY); MSD: brimonidine contraindicated under age 2 |
| **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** (acetazolamide oral; dorzolamide drops) | ↓ production | Oral: **paresthesias, kidney stones**, electrolyte changes (MSD) |
| **Cholinergic / miotics** (pilocarpine; ecothiophate) | ↑ **trabecular outflow** (Pharm HY); cause miosis (MSD) | Poor dark vision (MSD); ecothiophate → **cataract** (Pharm HY) |
| **Rho kinase inhibitor** (netarsudil) | ↑ trabecular outflow | (Pharm HY PDF 13) |
| **Osmotic** (mannitol IV) | Pulls water out of the eye | Contraindicated in pulmonary edema (Pharm HY PDF 87) |

- **Other source (MSD, open-angle, Mar 2025):** aim to lower IOP by 20-40%. **Prostaglandin analogs are the most popular first drug**; "SLT is emerging as the standard of care for the initial treatment" of newly diagnosed patients (**SLT** = selective laser trabeculoplasty). Vision already lost cannot be recovered.
- **Read more on EyeWiki:** [Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma](https://eyewiki.org/Primary_Open-Angle_Glaucoma); [Primary vs. Secondary Angle Closure Glaucoma](https://eyewiki.org/Primary_vs._Secondary_Angle_Closure_Glaucoma); [Laser Peripheral Iridotomy](https://eyewiki.org/Laser_Peripheral_Iridotomy) (links for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

## Retina: macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy and detachment (Osmosis pp. 599-606)
| | **Dry (nonexudative) AMD** | **Wet (exudative) AMD** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How common | Most cases | Less common |
| Lesion | **Drusen** between Bruch membrane and RPE; geographic atrophy | **Choroidal new vessels** through Bruch membrane → leak fluid and blood |
| Vision loss | Gradual | **Rapid, over weeks**; **metamorphopsia** (straight lines look wavy) |
| Treatment | Vitamins and antioxidants; stop smoking | **Intravitreal anti-VEGF**; photodynamic therapy with verteporfin |

Sources: Osmosis pp. 599-600 (risks: age over 65, family history, **complement factor H** variants, smoking, white race); Amsler grid finds the distortion.
- **Other source (MSD, June 2026):** about 85% dry and 15% wet, but wet AMD causes "80 to 90% of the severe vision loss". The supplement is for patients with **extensive drusen, pigment changes or geographic atrophy** (zinc, copper, vitamins C and E, **lutein and zeaxanthin**); in current or former smokers, **beta-carotene "can increase the risk of lung cancer"**. Anti-VEGF drugs named: ranibizumab, bevacizumab, aflibercept, faricimab.
- **Recent change (geographic atrophy now has a drug):** your notes say dry AMD has "no curative method" (p. 600). That is still true, but intravitreal **pegcetacoplan** (FDA approval reported 17 Feb 2023 as the first treatment for geographic atrophy) and **avacincaptad pegol** "can be used for the treatment of advanced dry AMD due to geographic atrophy"; they **inhibit the complement pathway** and slow its progression (MSD June 2026; *Ophthalmology Times* 2023). This fits the complement factor H link in your notes.

**Diabetic retinopathy** (pp. 603-604): hyperglycemia → vessel leakage and occlusion → **ischemia → VEGF → neovascularization**.
- **Nonproliferative**: microaneurysms, hemorrhages, hard exudates, **cotton-wool spots** (nerve-fiber infarcts), macular edema. **Proliferative**: new vessels → **vitreous hemorrhage**, **traction detachment**, neovascular glaucoma.
- **Macular edema is the most common cause of vision loss** (p. 603; MSD agrees).
- Treatment: glucose and blood pressure control; **anti-VEGF injections**; **laser photocoagulation of the peripheral retina**; vitrectomy for vitreous hemorrhage (pp. 603-604).
- **Other source (MSD, June 2026):** your notes suggest yearly screening for everyone with diabetes (p. 603). MSD: **type 2** = yearly dilated eye exam; **type 1** = yearly starting **3-5 years after diagnosis**; **pregnancy** = every trimester. High-risk proliferative disease gets **panretinal photocoagulation**; anti-VEGF drugs are also supported for proliferative disease.

**Retinal detachment** (pp. 605-606)
- **Rhegmatogenous** (most common): a full-thickness **retinal break** lets vitreous fluid get under the retina; often after **posterior vitreous detachment** (ages 50-75). Risks: **high myopia**, lattice degeneration, trauma, prior **cataract surgery**.
- **Tractional**: scar tissue pulls (proliferative diabetic retinopathy, ROP). **Exudative**: fluid from inflammation or choroidal tumors.
- Symptoms: sudden **floaters and flashes**, then a **curtain over the vision**; painless (MSD).
- Treatment: laser or cryotherapy to seal breaks; **pneumatic retinopexy** (gas bubble), **scleral buckle**, vitrectomy (p. 606). MSD: suspected detachment needs urgent ophthalmology review.
- **Read more on EyeWiki:** [Age-Related Macular Degeneration](https://eyewiki.org/Age-Related_Macular_Degeneration); [Diabetic Retinopathy](https://eyewiki.org/Diabetic_Retinopathy); [Retinal Detachment](https://eyewiki.org/Retinal_Detachment) (links for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

## Cataract (Osmosis pp. 600-601)
- **Painless, gradual** clouding of the lens; often both eyes (p. 600).
- Types: **nuclear** (slow; **myopic shift**, so near reading may briefly improve), **cortical** (glare and halos), **posterior subcapsular** (fast) (pp. 600-601).
- Risks: age over 60, smoking, alcohol, **glucocorticoids**, UV light, trauma, radiation, trisomies 13, 18, 21, **diabetes, Wilson disease, galactosemia, myotonic dystrophy** (p. 600).
- **Why diabetes and galactosemia cause cataracts:** **aldose reductase** in the lens turns glucose into **sorbitol** and galactose into **galactitol**, which pull in water and damage the lens (El-Husseiny Biochemistry p. 139).
- Exam: dark spots in, or loss of, the **red reflex** (p. 601). Treatment: **phacoemulsification with an artificial lens** (p. 601).
- Other lens clues: **Marfan** syndrome → lens dislocates **upward**; **homocystinuria** → **downward** (NBME TC p. 138).
- **Other source (MSD, Nov 2025):** cataract is "the leading cause of blindness worldwide". **Posterior subcapsular** cataract causes the most glare and is worse in bright light; systemic glucocorticoids are a risk. "There is no indication for removing asymptomatic cataracts." Clouding of the lens capsule after surgery is "common but treatable with laser".

## Childhood eye disease: retinoblastoma and ROP (Osmosis pp. 607-609)
- **Retinoblastoma** (pp. 607-608): the most common primary eye cancer of childhood, usually under age 2; presents with **leukocoria** (white pupil), strabismus.
  - **Both copies of RB1 (chromosome 13q14) must be lost** (two hits).
  - **Heritable (40%)**: germline mutation, early, **bilateral or multifocal**, 50% risk to children, later **sarcomas (osteosarcoma)**. **Nonheritable (60%)**: two somatic hits, later, **unilateral**.
  - Pinealoblastoma = "trilateral" retinoblastoma. Calcification on ultrasound. Survival over 95% with prompt treatment.
  - **Other source (MSD, June 2024):** MSD splits it as 25-40% bilateral (always heritable) plus about 15% heritable unilateral, and **60% nonhereditary unilateral**, close to your notes' 40/60; it says "> 90% of patients can be cured" when the tumor is still inside the eye (your notes: over 95%). Bilateral disease "is always heritable"; in hereditary cases about half of second cancers arise in the radiation field (sarcomas, osteosarcoma, melanoma). Children with a parent or sibling who had retinoblastoma are screened.
- **Retinopathy of prematurity** (p. 609): preterm birth interrupts normal retinal vessel growth; **supplemental oxygen** disrupts angiogenesis → abnormal vessels and fibrous tissue → **traction detachment** and blindness. Risk: **under 30 weeks, 1.5 kg or less**, excess oxygen. Most cases regress. Treatment: **laser ablation of the avascular retina** or **intravitreal anti-VEGF**.
  - **Other source (MSD, April 2026):** screen all infants "< 1500 g or < 30 weeks' gestation at birth", starting about 31 weeks' gestational age. Later problems: myopia, strabismus, amblyopia.
  - **Recent change (first FDA-approved drug for ROP):** in February 2023 the FDA approved **aflibercept** for preterm infants with ROP; before that, laser was the main approved treatment (*Ophthalmology Times*, 9 Feb 2023). MSD still describes **bevacizumab** as widely used.
- **Read more on EyeWiki:** [Retinoblastoma](https://eyewiki.org/Retinoblastoma); [Leukocoria](https://eyewiki.org/Leukocoria) (links for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

## Clinical vignette
A 64-year-old woman of Chinese descent, who is farsighted, comes to the emergency department 2 hours after leaving a dark movie theater with severe pain in her right eye, a frontal headache, nausea and vomiting. She sees colored halos around lights. The right eye is red with a hazy cornea, and the pupil is **mid-dilated and does not react to light**. The eye feels rock hard.

- **Diagnosis:** **acute angle-closure glaucoma**. In dim light the pupil dilates, the iris bunches into a narrow angle and blocks drainage through the trabecular meshwork, so IOP rises fast (Osmosis pp. 604-605; MSD).
- **Now:** pressure-lowering drops (timolol, brimonidine, pilocarpine) plus **acetazolamide**, and an osmotic agent such as mannitol if needed (MSD; Osmosis p. 605; Pharm HY PDF 87).
- **Then:** **laser peripheral iridotomy in both eyes**, since the other eye has a high risk (MSD).
- **Why not dilate to look at the retina:** dilating drops can trigger or worsen angle closure (Osmosis p. 604; MSD).

## Common traps
- **Corneal epithelium = surface ectoderm; stroma and endothelium = neural crest** (Osmosis Physiology p. 260; Ozkan and Utine 2025).
- **Painless lid lump = chalazion; painful = stye**; a chalazion that keeps coming back needs a biopsy (Osmosis p. 586; MSD).
- **Proptosis or pain on eye movement = orbital, not preseptal** → admit for IV antibiotics (Osmosis p. 592; MSD).
- **No steroid drops for HSV epithelial keratitis** (MSD).
- **Contact lens + rapidly worsening ulcer = *Pseudomonas*** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 13; MSD). **Never patch a corneal ulcer** (MSD).
- **Watery + preauricular node = adenovirus**: no antibiotics (Osmosis p. 589; MSD).
- **Young man with back pain and a red, photophobic eye = anterior uveitis (HLA-B27)** (NBME TC p. 386; MSD).
- **Glaucoma can occur with a normal IOP** (Osmosis p. 604; MSD).
- **Angle closure: fixed mid-dilated pupil; avoid dilating drops; treat both eyes with iridotomy** (MSD).
- **Latanoprost darkens the iris; timolol slows the heart and tightens the airways; brimonidine is unsafe in infants** (Pharm HY PDF 13; MSD).
- **Wet AMD = rapid, wavy lines → anti-VEGF; no beta-carotene for smokers** (MSD).
- **Macular edema is the most common cause of vision loss in diabetic retinopathy** (Osmosis p. 603; MSD).
- **Flashes + floaters + curtain = retinal detachment** (Osmosis p. 606; MSD).
- **Steroids → posterior subcapsular cataract** (Osmosis p. 600; MSD).
- **Bilateral retinoblastoma = germline RB1 → osteosarcoma later** (Osmosis p. 607; MSD).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 24-year-old woman who sleeps in her extended-wear contact lenses has had increasing pain, redness and light sensitivity in her left eye for 2 days. Slit-lamp examination shows a large central corneal ulcer with surrounding necrosis and a layer of white cells in the anterior chamber. Which organism is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** ***Pseudomonas aeruginosa.***
   - **Why:** contact lens wear is the major risk for bacterial keratitis; *Pseudomonas* forms biofilms on lenses (El-Husseiny Micro p. 13) and its ulcers "develop rapidly, causing deep and extensive corneal necrosis" (MSD). Hypopyon marks a severe ulcer (Osmosis p. 602).
   - **Trap:** ***Acanthamoeba***, which also affects lens wearers but follows exposure to contaminated water and causes a ring infiltrate (Osmosis p. 590; MSD).

2. A 31-year-old man has a painful, red, watering right eye with photophobia. He had a cold sore last week. Fluorescein staining shows a branching ulcer on the corneal surface. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **a topical antiviral (trifluridine or ganciclovir) or oral acyclovir.**
   - **Why:** a dendritic ulcer is HSV epithelial keratitis (Osmosis p. 602); MSD lists topical ganciclovir or trifluridine, or oral antivirals.
   - **Trap:** **topical corticosteroid drops** to calm the inflammation. MSD: "Topical glucocorticoids are contraindicated in epithelial keratitis."

3. A 7-year-old boy with a week of nasal congestion now has fever and a swollen, red left upper eyelid. The left eye bulges forward, and he has pain and double vision when looking to the side. Visual acuity is slightly reduced. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **hospital admission with IV antibiotics and CT of the orbits and sinuses.**
   - **Why:** proptosis, painful and limited eye movement and reduced vision mean **orbital** cellulitis, usually spread from ethmoid sinusitis; it can cause cavernous sinus thrombosis and vision loss (Osmosis pp. 591-592; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **oral amoxicillin-clavulanate at home**, which MSD allows only for preseptal cellulitis once orbital cellulitis "has been definitively excluded" (Osmosis p. 594).

4. A 66-year-old man has a firm, painless nodule in the right upper eyelid. It is the third time in 2 years that a lump has appeared in the same spot; earlier lumps were drained. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **excision with histologic examination (biopsy).**
   - **Why:** a chalazion is a lipogranuloma (MSD: noninfectious), but a recurring chalazion may signal eyelid carcinoma, so tissue should be examined (Osmosis pp. 586-587; MSD: chronic chalazia that do not respond "require biopsy").
   - **Trap:** **another intralesional steroid injection**, which treats an ordinary persistent chalazion but would miss a tumor (p. 587).

5. A 9-year-old girl's teacher has pink eye. Two days later the girl has a red, watery left eye that spreads to the right eye. She has a tender lump in front of her left ear. There is no itching and no pus. Which is the most appropriate management?
   - **Answer:** **cool compresses, hand washing and keeping her home from school until it settles.**
   - **Why:** watery discharge, spread to the other eye and a preauricular node suggest **adenoviral conjunctivitis**, which is self-limited (2-3 weeks); MSD recommends cool compresses and hygiene, and says secondary bacterial infection "is very rare" (Osmosis pp. 589-590).
   - **Trap:** **topical antibiotic drops**, which do not treat a viral infection (MSD).

6. A 26-year-old man has had 3 months of low back pain and morning stiffness that improve with exercise. He now has a painful, red left eye and cannot tolerate bright light. There is redness around the edge of the cornea, white cell deposits on the back of the cornea, and an irregular, sluggish pupil. Which test result is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **HLA-B27 positive.**
   - **Why:** inflammatory back pain with acute **anterior uveitis** suggests ankylosing spondylitis (NBME TC p. 386). MSD: anterior uveitis is common with spondyloarthropathies, and "most patients ... are HLA-B27 positive". Keratic precipitates and synechiae are signs of anterior uveitis (Osmosis p. 597).
   - **Trap:** **HLA-B51**, which goes with Behçet disease (oral and genital ulcers) (MSD).

7. A 68-year-old Black man has a routine eye examination. He has no symptoms. Intraocular pressure is 27 mm Hg in both eyes, the drainage angles are open on gonioscopy, and both optic discs have a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.7. Visual field testing shows early peripheral loss. Which is the most appropriate initial treatment?
   - **Answer:** **a prostaglandin analog eye drop (such as latanoprost).**
   - **Why:** this is **open-angle glaucoma** (Osmosis p. 604). MSD: prostaglandin analogs are the most popular first drug, and prostaglandin analogs increase uveoscleral outflow (Pharm HY PDF 13); MSD adds that selective laser trabeculoplasty is becoming a standard first treatment. Pharm HY PDF 165 lists latanoprost for open-angle glaucoma.
   - **Trap:** **laser peripheral iridotomy**, which treats angle closure, not an open angle (Osmosis p. 605; MSD).

8. A 72-year-old man starts timolol eye drops for open-angle glaucoma. Two weeks later he feels tired and lightheaded; his pulse is 46/min. Which feature of tear drainage best explains this effect?
   - **Answer:** **tears drain through the nasolacrimal duct into the nose, where the drug is absorbed into the blood.**
   - **Why:** tears flow through the puncta, canaliculi, lacrimal sac and **nasolacrimal duct to the inferior nasal meatus** (Osmosis Physiology p. 434); timolol absorbed from there blocks cardiac β1 receptors (standard teaching). Pharm HY lists "systemic S/E" for timolol (PDF 13), and MSD lists bradycardia and bronchospasm.
   - **Trap:** **timolol crosses the cornea into the heart muscle**. There is no direct route; systemic exposure comes from drainage and absorption (standard teaching).

9. A 58-year-old man with glaucoma takes an oral drug that lowers intraocular pressure. Over several months he notices tingling in his fingers and then has an episode of renal colic from a kidney stone. Which drug is he most likely taking?
   - **Answer:** **acetazolamide.**
   - **Why:** carbonic anhydrase inhibitors reduce aqueous humor production (Pharm HY PDF 13); oral forms cause **paresthesias and kidney stones** and electrolyte changes (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **timolol**, which also reduces aqueous production but causes bradycardia and bronchospasm, not stones (MSD).

10. A 74-year-old woman who smoked for 40 years notices that over 3 weeks the door frames look bent with her right eye and that a gray spot blocks the center of what she reads. Examination shows subretinal fluid and blood in the macula. Which treatment is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **intravitreal anti-VEGF injections.**
    - **Why:** rapid central loss with **metamorphopsia** and subretinal fluid or blood means **wet AMD** from choroidal new vessels (Osmosis pp. 599-600); anti-VEGF drugs are the main treatment (MSD).
    - **Trap:** **an antioxidant vitamin supplement**, which only slows progression of intermediate or advanced dry disease (MSD).

11. A 70-year-old man who quit smoking 5 years ago has many large drusen in both maculas and mild central blur. His vision is 20/30. He asks about vitamins. Which supplement ingredient should be avoided?
    - **Answer:** **beta-carotene.**
    - **Why:** the supplement for extensive drusen uses zinc, copper, vitamins C and E, and **lutein/zeaxanthin**; in current or former smokers, beta-carotene "can increase the risk of lung cancer" (MSD). Your notes recommend vitamin and antioxidant supplements and smoking cessation (Osmosis p. 600).
    - **Trap:** **zinc**, which is part of the recommended formula (MSD).

12. A 52-year-old woman has had type 2 diabetes for 15 years with poor control. Dilated examination shows fine new blood vessels on the optic disc and a small vitreous hemorrhage. Which process most directly drives the new vessel growth?
    - **Answer:** **retinal ischemia causing release of VEGF.**
    - **Why:** chronic hyperglycemia damages and blocks retinal vessels → ischemia → VEGF → neovascularization, which defines proliferative retinopathy (Osmosis p. 603); this is why anti-VEGF injections and panretinal photocoagulation are used (MSD).
    - **Trap:** **osmotic damage from sorbitol**, which explains diabetic cataracts in the lens, not retinal new vessels (El-Husseiny Biochemistry p. 139).

13. A 61-year-old man with high myopia had cataract surgery 3 months ago. He suddenly sees many new floaters and flashes of light in his left eye, and a dark shadow spreading down from the top of his vision. There is no pain. Which mechanism best explains his condition?
    - **Answer:** **a full-thickness retinal tear letting vitreous fluid pass under the retina (rhegmatogenous detachment).**
    - **Why:** high myopia and prior cataract surgery are risks; floaters, flashes and a curtain are classic, often after posterior vitreous detachment (Osmosis pp. 605-606; MSD). It needs urgent ophthalmology review (MSD).
    - **Trap:** **fibrous membranes pulling the retina off (tractional)**, which is seen with proliferative diabetic retinopathy or ROP (Osmosis p. 606).

14. A 45-year-old woman has taken prednisone daily for 4 years for autoimmune hepatitis. She has trouble with glare from headlights and sees worse in bright sunlight, though her vision is fine in dim light. Which lens change is most likely?
    - **Answer:** **posterior subcapsular cataract.**
    - **Why:** glucocorticoids are a cataract risk, and posterior subcapsular cataracts progress fast and cause the most glare, worse in bright light (Osmosis pp. 600-601; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **nuclear cataract**, which progresses slowly and causes a myopic shift ("second sight") rather than glare (Osmosis pp. 600-601; MSD).

15. A 15-month-old girl has a white pupil in both eyes in family photographs. Her father lost one eye to a tumor as a baby. Examination under anesthesia shows several white tumors in each retina. Later in life, this girl is at highest risk for which cancer?
    - **Answer:** **osteosarcoma.**
    - **Why:** bilateral, early, multifocal retinoblastoma with an affected parent means a **germline RB1** mutation (chromosome 13q14); heritable cases have a higher risk of later sarcomas, especially osteosarcoma (Osmosis p. 607; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **Wilms tumor**, which goes with WT1 (WAGR), not RB1 (standard teaching).

16. A boy born at 26 weeks weighing 780 g needed supplemental oxygen for 8 weeks. At 35 weeks' corrected age, an eye exam shows a fibrovascular ridge with new vessels growing into the vitreous at the edge of the vascularized retina. Which mediator most directly drives this growth?
    - **Answer:** **VEGF.**
    - **Why:** preterm birth leaves the peripheral retina avascular; oxygen disrupts normal vessel development, and the ischemic retina then drives abnormal vessel growth, so treatment is laser ablation of the avascular retina or an intravitreal **anti-VEGF** drug (Osmosis p. 609; MSD). Aflibercept was FDA approved for ROP in 2023.
    - **Trap:** **TGF-β**, which drives fibrosis in many tissues but is not the target of ROP treatment (standard teaching).

17. Before a retinal exam, a 40-year-old woman receives cyclopentolate eye drops. An hour later she can see distant objects but cannot read her phone. Blockade of which receptor explains her near-vision problem?
    - **Answer:** **muscarinic M3 receptors on the ciliary muscle.**
    - **Why:** near vision needs parasympathetic contraction of the ciliary muscle to relax the zonule fibers and round the lens (Osmosis Physiology p. 439); Pharm HY shows parasympathetic M3 receptors in the eye on the sphincter pupillae (PDF 13); the ciliary muscle uses the same M3 receptors (standard teaching). Cyclopentolate is a cycloplegic (MSD lists it with homatropine for uveitis and as a dilating drop that can trigger angle closure), so it blocks this step.
    - **Trap:** **α1 receptors on the dilator pupillae**, which widen the pupil (sympathetic, Osmosis Physiology p. 436; the α1 subtype is standard teaching) but do not control lens focusing.

18. After cataract surgery, a 78-year-old man's cornea becomes swollen and cloudy because the cells lining its inner surface can no longer pump fluid out. From which embryonic tissue do these cells come?
    - **Answer:** **neural crest.**
    - **Why:** the corneal endothelium pumps water out to keep the cornea clear (Osmosis Physiology p. 436); the stroma and endothelium come from neural crest, and the epithelium from surface ectoderm (Ozkan and Utine 2025; Osmosis Physiology p. 260). MSD lists bullous keratopathy as a late complication of cataract surgery.
    - **Trap:** **surface ectoderm**, which forms the corneal epithelium and the lens, not the endothelium (Osmosis Physiology p. 260).
