# Autonomic drugs and toxicology

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: pending
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials: General Principles (autonomic nervous system pp. 54-58, cholinergic drugs pp. 59-63, adrenergic drugs pp. 65-70, phosphodiesterase inhibitors p. 71, toxins and antidotes pp. 72-73, drug reactions pp. 73-77). Each fact is also checked against StatPearls and the MSD Manual; where a different source adds to your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Learn the receptor first and the drug follows. **Gq** is α1 and M1/M3, **Gi** is α2 and M2, and **Gs** is β. Too much acetylcholine causes a wet, slow patient with small pupils (organophosphates, treated with atropine and pralidoxime). Too little causes a hot, dry, confused patient with big pupils (atropine toxicity, treated with physostigmine).

## Core mechanism
1. **Wiring:** all preganglionic fibers and all motor neurons use acetylcholine (ACh). Most sympathetic postganglionic fibers use norepinephrine (NE) (El-Husseiny General Principles, p. 55). MSD agrees. [MSD Manual: Overview of the Autonomic Nervous System](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/autonomic-nervous-system/overview-of-the-autonomic-nervous-system)
2. **Two exceptions (p. 55):** sweat glands are sympathetic but use ACh on muscarinic receptors. The adrenal medulla gets preganglionic sympathetic fibers directly, through nicotinic receptors.
3. **Ganglia:** sympathetic ganglia sit in chains beside the spine; most parasympathetic ganglia sit in the organ (p. 54).
4. **Receptors:** nicotinic NN is in ganglia and the adrenal medulla, and NM is at the neuromuscular junction. Muscarinic receptors are G-protein coupled (p. 55).
5. **Ending the signal:** acetylcholinesterase (AChE) breaks down ACh (p. 60). NE is mainly taken back up into the nerve ending, and is metabolized by COMT and MAO (p. 65).
6. **Rate-limiting step of NE synthesis:** tyrosine hydroxylase (p. 65).
7. **Blocking ACh release:** botulinum toxin (p. 60). Hemicholinium blocks choline uptake and vesamicol blocks ACh storage (p. 60).

## High-yield facts

### G-protein receptor classes
| G protein | Second messenger | Receptors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gq | Phospholipase C, then IP3, DAG and calcium | α1, M1, M3, H1, V1 |
| Gi | Lowers cAMP | α2, M2, D2 |
| Gs | Raises cAMP | β1, β2, β3, D1, H2, V2 |

Source: El-Husseiny, pp. 56, 58. [StatPearls: Physiology, Catecholamines](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507716/) and [StatPearls: Muscarinic Agonists](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553130/) give the same coupling. StatPearls adds M5 to Gq and M4 to Gi.

### What each receptor does (p. 58)
- **α1:** vessel contraction, pupil dilation, bladder sphincter contraction.
- **α2:** less sympathetic outflow, less insulin, less aqueous humor.
- **β1:** faster heart, stronger contraction, more renin.
- **β2:** vasodilation, bronchodilation, uterine relaxation, potassium moved into cells.
- **M2:** slower heart. **M3:** more gland secretion, gut movement, bladder contraction, bronchoconstriction and pupil constriction.

### Muscarinic antagonists (p. 62)
| Drug | Target | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atropine, homatropine, tropicamide | Eye | Dilate the pupil and paralyze accommodation |
| Atropine | Heart | Bradycardia |
| Benztropine, trihexyphenidyl | CNS | Parkinson disease, acute dystonia |
| Glycopyrrolate | GI, airway | Reduce airway secretions before surgery |
| Hyoscyamine, dicyclomine | GI | Irritable bowel spasm |
| Ipratropium, tiotropium | Airway | COPD, asthma |
| Oxybutynin, solifenacin, tolterodine | Bladder | Urge incontinence |
| Scopolamine | CNS | Motion sickness |

**Atropine toxicity (p. 62):** high temperature (no sweating), fast heart, dry and flushed skin, blurred near vision, constipation and confusion. It can trigger acute angle-closure glaucoma in older adults and urinary retention in men with an enlarged prostate.

### Bladder drugs (p. 63)
- **Oxybutynin:** blocks M3, so the detrusor relaxes. Used for urge incontinence.
- **Bethanechol:** activates M3, so the bladder empties. Used for urinary retention.
- **Mirabegron:** activates β3, so the detrusor relaxes. Used for urge incontinence.
- **Tamsulosin:** blocks α1 at the bladder neck and prostate. Used for benign prostatic hyperplasia.

### Sympathomimetics
| Drug | Receptors | Key effect or use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Albuterol, salmeterol, terbutaline | β2 > β1 | Asthma and COPD; terbutaline also relaxes the uterus |
| Dobutamine | β1 > β2, α | Stronger contraction in heart failure and cardiogenic shock |
| Dopamine | D1 = D2 > β > α | Effects rise in steps with dose |
| Epinephrine | β > α | Anaphylaxis; α effects dominate at high doses |
| Fenoldopam | D1 | Hypertensive crisis; vasodilates |
| Isoproterenol | β1 = β2 | Lowers BP, raises HR; can worsen ischemia |
| Midodrine | α1 | Raises BP in orthostatic hypotension |
| Norepinephrine | α1 > α2 > β1 | Septic shock; reflex slowing of the heart |
| Phenylephrine | α1 > α2 | Raises BP; decongestant; dilates the pupil |
| Amphetamine, ephedrine | Indirect; release stored catecholamines | ADHD, narcolepsy; nasal congestion |
| Cocaine | Indirect; blocks reuptake | Vasoconstriction and local anesthesia |

Source: El-Husseiny, p. 66. **Other source:** StatPearls describes epinephrine as having essentially comparable α1 and β activity, and describes norepinephrine as having slightly more α1 than β activity, with β1 more than β2. [StatPearls: Inotropes and Vasopressors](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482411/)

- **Norepinephrine vs isoproterenol (p. 67):** NE raises systolic and diastolic pressure through α1, and the higher mean pressure slows the heart by reflex. Isoproterenol lowers mean pressure through β2 vasodilation and speeds the heart through β1 and reflex. [StatPearls: Isoproterenol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK526042/)
- **Epinephrine reversal (pp. 68-69):** after an α blocker, epinephrine lowers blood pressure because β2 vasodilation is left unopposed. Phenylephrine has no β effect, so its pressure rise is only blunted.
- **Nasal decongestants (p. 53):** tachyphylaxis and rebound congestion appear after more than 3 days of topical use.

### β-blockers (p. 70)
- **β1 selective:** acebutolol, atenolol, betaxolol, bisoprolol, esmolol, metoprolol. [StatPearls: Selective Beta-1 Blockers](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK499982/)
- **Nonselective:** nadolol, pindolol, propranolol, timolol.
- **α and β:** carvedilol, labetalol.
- **Partial agonists:** acebutolol (β1) and pindolol.
- **Lower mortality in heart failure:** bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol.
- **Nebivolol:** β1 blockade plus β3 stimulation that raises nitric oxide.
- **Timolol:** lowers aqueous humor in glaucoma.
- **Side effects:** erectile dysfunction, bradycardia, AV block, heart failure, and worse asthma or COPD.
- **Overdose antidote:** your notes list atropine, glucagon and saline (p. 73). MSD lists glucagon and IV lipid emulsion. [MSD Manual: General Principles of Poisoning](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/general-principles-of-poisoning)

## Compare and contrast: two toxidromes
| | Cholinergic excess (organophosphate) | Anticholinergic (atropine) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pupils | Small (miosis) | Large |
| Skin | Sweaty | Dry, flushed |
| Heart rate | Usually slow | Fast |
| Secretions | Heavy | Dry |
| Bowel and bladder | Diarrhea, urination | Constipation, retention |
| Antidote | Atropine, then pralidoxime | Physostigmine (controversial, per MSD) |

### Organophosphate poisoning
- **Mechanism:** irreversibly inhibits AChE, so ACh builds up at muscarinic and nicotinic sites. Usually from insecticides in farm workers (p. 61). [MSD Manual: Organophosphate and Carbamate Poisoning](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/organophosphate-poisoning-and-carbamate-poisoning)
- **Muscarinic signs (p. 61):** diarrhea, urination, miosis, bronchospasm, bradycardia, vomiting, tears, sweating, salivation.
- **Nicotinic signs:** fasciculations, then weakness and paralysis (p. 61). StatPearls and MSD also list mydriasis and tachycardia, so the heart rate and pupils can vary. [StatPearls: Organophosphate Toxicity](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470430/)
- **Atropine** treats muscarinic effects only, and it can cross into the CNS (p. 61). It does not fix muscle weakness.
- **Pralidoxime** regenerates AChE and acts mainly at nicotinic sites. It must be given early (p. 61). StatPearls explains why: it works only before the enzyme "ages", when the bond becomes irreversible. [StatPearls: Pralidoxime](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558908/), [StatPearls: Organophosphate Toxicity](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470430/)
- **Death** usually comes from respiratory failure, from bronchorrhea and bronchospasm. [StatPearls: Organophosphate Toxicity](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470430/)

### Tyramine and MAO inhibitors (p. 69)
MAO in the gut normally breaks down tyramine. With an MAO inhibitor, tyramine from aged cheese or red wine pushes stored NE out of nerve endings, causing a hypertensive crisis. Phentolamine treats it (p. 69). MSD calls this "the cheese reaction". [MSD Manual: Drug Treatment of Depression](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/mood-disorders/drug-treatment-of-depression)

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

### Beers criteria (p. 72)
Drugs that older adults should generally avoid or use with care:
- **α-blockers:** low blood pressure.
- **Anticholinergics, antidepressants, antihistamines, opioids:** delirium, sedation, falls, constipation, urinary retention.
- **Benzodiazepines:** delirium, sedation, falls.
- **NSAIDs:** GI bleeding, especially with anticoagulants.
- **PPIs:** *C. difficile* infection.

**Recent change:** the current edition is the **2023** American Geriatrics Society update, which MSD cites. It lists benzodiazepines and Z-drugs as linked to delirium, falls and fractures. [MSD Manual: Drug Categories of Concern in Older Adults](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/geriatrics/pharmacologic-therapy-in-older-adults/drug-categories-of-concern-in-older-adults)

## Clinical vignette
A 44-year-old farm worker is brought in after spraying a field. He is drooling, wheezing, sweating and has muscle twitching. His pupils are pinpoint and his pulse is 44/min. Atropine dries his secretions, but his weakness continues. Which drug addresses his weakness, and how does it work?

**Answer:** pralidoxime; it regenerates acetylcholinesterase, mainly helping nicotinic sites.
**Why:** atropine blocks only muscarinic receptors. Pralidoxime has to be given before the enzyme ages. (El-Husseiny, p. 61; [StatPearls: Pralidoxime](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558908/))
**Trap:** giving more atropine for the weakness. Skeletal muscle uses nicotinic receptors.

## Common traps
- **Sweat glands use ACh**, so atropine causes hot, dry skin even though sweating is a sympathetic response.
- **Physostigmine enters the brain; neostigmine and pyridostigmine do not.**
- **Atropine does not treat nicotinic signs.**
- **Clonidine stopped suddenly** causes rebound hypertension.
- **Isoproterenol lowers mean pressure.** Norepinephrine raises it.
- **Tamsulosin relaxes the prostate and bladder neck.** It is not used for hypertension (p. 69).
- **PDE-5 inhibitors plus nitrates** can cause severe hypotension (p. 71).
- **Scombroid looks like an allergy**, but it is caused by histamine in the fish itself.
- **Hot-cold reversal after eating reef fish** means ciguatera.

## Practice questions
1. A 70-year-old man is given an antihistamine with strong antimuscarinic effects. He becomes confused and cannot pass urine. His skin is hot and dry, and his pupils are large. What is the mechanism?
   - **Answer:** competitive block of muscarinic receptors.
   - **Why:** this matches the atropine picture in your notes (p. 62). MSD notes that diphenhydramine has anticholinergic effects. [MSD Manual: Drug Categories of Concern in Older Adults](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/geriatrics/pharmacologic-therapy-in-older-adults/drug-categories-of-concern-in-older-adults)
   - **Trap:** cholinergic excess, which causes sweating and small pupils.
2. A patient is given an α blocker and then epinephrine. What happens to the mean arterial pressure?
   - **Answer:** it falls.
   - **Why:** α1 vasoconstriction is blocked, leaving β2 vasodilation unopposed. This is epinephrine reversal. (El-Husseiny, pp. 68-69)
   - **Trap:** expecting a smaller rise, which is what happens with phenylephrine.
3. A patient with a pheochromocytoma needs treatment before surgery. Which drug is used, and how does it act?
   - **Answer:** phenoxybenzamine; it irreversibly blocks α1 and α2 receptors.
   - **Why:** (El-Husseiny, p. 69; [StatPearls: Alpha-Blockers](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK556066/))
   - **Trap:** starting a β-blocker first, which leaves α vasoconstriction unopposed.
4. A patient on phenelzine eats aged cheese and has a blood pressure of 220/120 mm Hg. Which drug in your notes treats this?
   - **Answer:** phentolamine, a reversible α blocker.
   - **Why:** tyramine releases stored NE, which acts on α receptors. (El-Husseiny, p. 69)
   - **Trap:** phenoxybenzamine, which is irreversible and used before pheochromocytoma surgery.
5. Soon after eating tuna left out for hours, a woman has facial flushing, hives and a burning mouth. Others at the table are affected too. What is the cause?
   - **Answer:** scombroid poisoning: histamine made by bacteria in the fish.
   - **Why:** [MSD Manual: Fish Poisoning and Shellfish Poisoning](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/poisoning/fish-poisoning-and-shellfish-poisoning); El-Husseiny, p. 72.
   - **Trap:** a true fish allergy, which would not affect several diners at once.
6. Which receptor does bethanechol act on to treat urinary retention, and which G protein does it use?
   - **Answer:** M3, through Gq.
   - **Why:** (El-Husseiny, pp. 58, 63; [StatPearls: Muscarinic Agonists](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553130/))
   - **Trap:** M2, which uses Gi and slows the heart.
