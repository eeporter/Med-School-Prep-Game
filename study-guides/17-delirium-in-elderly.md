## In one line
Sudden, fluctuating confusion with poor attention in an older adult is delirium until proven otherwise; look first for drugs (anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids), infection (urine, lungs) and dehydration.

## Core mechanism
1. Delirium is an acute, usually reversible, fluctuating disturbance mainly of **attention**, plus cognition and level of consciousness. [MSD Manual: Delirium](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
2. Older brains have less reserve and are more sensitive to drugs, especially sedatives, anticholinergics and antihistamines, so a small insult tips them over. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
3. It can be **hyperactive** (agitated, hyperalert) or **hypoactive** (quiet, withdrawn, lethargic). The quiet form is common in the very old and is often mistaken for depression. DSM-5-TR adds a third specifier, **mixed level of activity**, and says the hypoactive state "may be more frequent in older adults and is often unrecognized" in emergency departments and hospitals; women more often have the hypoactive form (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Specifiers; Sex- and Gender-Related Diagnostic Issues). [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)

## The DSM-5-TR definition (your notes)
All five criteria are needed (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Diagnostic Criteria):
1. **A. Attention:** a disturbance in attention (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain and shift it) with reduced awareness of the environment.
2. **B. Time course:** develops over hours to a few days, is a change from baseline, and fluctuates during the day.
3. **C. One more cognitive problem:** memory, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability or perception (illusions and hallucinations, usually visual).
4. **D. Not explained by** a pre-existing dementia (major neurocognitive disorder), and not occurring in **coma**.
5. **E. A cause:** another medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, a medication, a toxin, or several of these.

Other high-yield points from the same chapter:
- It is often **worse in the evening and at night**, when orienting cues fade, and the sleep-wake cycle is often disturbed or even reversed (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Diagnostic Features; Associated Features).
- **EEG:** often generalized irregular **theta slowing**; fast activity is occasionally seen, as in alcohol withdrawal delirium (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Diagnostic Markers).
- **Risk factors:** older age, pre-existing cognitive impairment, sensory impairment (vision, hearing), illness severity, infection, depression, prior stroke, alcohol use and male sex. Falls can result from delirium but are not a risk factor (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Risk and Prognostic Factors).
- **Outlook:** most people recover fully, but 38%-41% of people with delirium die within 1 year; in hospital it usually lasts about 1 week (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Development and Course; Specifiers).

## Common causes
| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Medications (one of the most common) | Anticholinergics, opioids, sedatives, antihistamines; recent dose changes. Your notes list delirium as the CNS adverse effect of anticholinergic drugs such as benztropine and trihexyphenidyl (Ninja Nerd Pharmacology, PDF pp. 104-105) |
| Infection (one of the most common) | Urinary tract infection, pneumonia, viral illness |
| Fluids and metabolism | Dehydration (one of the most common), electrolyte problems, glucose problems |
| Oxygen and circulation | Hypoxia, anemia, shock |
| Other | Pain, urinary retention, bladder catheter |

[MSD Manual: Delirium](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)

**Other source (anticholinergics):** DSM-5-TR notes that "in a meta-analysis of studies from 1990 through 2016, anticholinergic use was not a validated predictor of delirium" (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Risk and Prognostic Factors). The MSD Manual and your pharmacology notes still list anticholinergics as a classic cause. For exams, an older patient who becomes confused after starting diphenhydramine, oxybutynin or benztropine still points to the drug (standard teaching).

## Screening tool
The Confusion Assessment Method (CAM): (1) acute onset or fluctuating course **and** (2) inattention, plus either (3) disorganized thinking or (4) altered level of consciousness. MSD names the CAM and lists criteria 3 and 4 ([MSD Manual: Delirium](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)); the full four-part rule is standard teaching.

## Initial evaluation
1. Vital signs and hydration; look for infection sites; neurologic exam.
2. Medication review, focusing on anticholinergic and other CNS-active drugs and recent changes.
3. Labs: electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose; oxygen (pulse oximetry or ABG).
4. Infection tests when suspected: CBC, blood cultures, chest X-ray, urinalysis.
5. Head CT or MRI and urine drug screen when indicated.

[MSD Manual: Delirium](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)

## Compare and contrast: delirium vs dementia
| Feature | Delirium | Dementia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Acute (hours to days) | Slower onset |
| Main deficit | Attention | Memory |
| Course | Fluctuating, usually reversible | Generally irreversible |
| Usual cause | Acute illness or drug toxicity | Anatomic changes in the brain |

## Clinical vignette
An 84-year-old woman in a nursing home becomes drowsy and confused over 2 days, worse at night. She was started on diphenhydramine for sleep last week. Temperature is 37.8°C and her urine is cloudy. What are the two most likely contributors?

**Answer:** an anticholinergic drug (diphenhydramine) and a urinary tract infection.
**Why:** drugs (especially anticholinergics and antihistamines), infection and dehydration are the most common causes of delirium, and older brains are especially sensitive to these drugs. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
**Trap:** diagnosing new dementia; the onset is days, attention is impaired and it fluctuates.

## Common traps
- Quiet, withdrawn older patients can have delirium; it is not always agitation.
- Always review the medication list first.
- Urinary retention (for example from anticholinergics) can itself cause delirium.
- Dementia is a risk factor for delirium; the two often coexist. DSM-5-TR calls separating them "the most common differential diagnostic issue when evaluating confusion in older adults", and says to treat the delirium first when both are present (DSM-5-TR, Delirium: Risk and Prognostic Factors; Differential Diagnosis).
- A patient in coma cannot be diagnosed with delirium (DSM-5-TR criterion D).

## Practice questions
1. An 84-year-old nursing home resident becomes inattentive and drowsy over 2 days, with confusion that is worse at night. Five days ago she started diphenhydramine for sleep. Temperature, oxygen saturation, urinalysis and chest x-ray are normal, and she is drinking well. What is the most likely cause of her delirium?
   - **Answer:** the new anticholinergic drug (diphenhydramine).
   - **Why:** MSD lists drugs, dehydration and infection together as the most common causes of delirium, and anticholinergics are classic culprits; infection and dehydration are not supported here. Diphenhydramine is anticholinergic (standard teaching). [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
   - **Trap:** looking only for infection; always review the medication list and hydration too.
2. Which cognitive domain is mainly affected in delirium, and which in dementia?
   - **Answer:** attention in delirium; memory in dementia.
   - **Why:** this is the core clinical distinction. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
   - **Trap:** both can have memory problems; attention is the discriminator.
3. A confused 79-year-old man after hip surgery has a distended, tender bladder. What is a reversible cause to fix?
   - **Answer:** urinary retention.
   - **Why:** urinary retention and pain are recognized precipitants. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
   - **Trap:** sedating the patient, which can worsen delirium.
4. An elderly patient is withdrawn and sleepy, and her family thinks she is depressed. What should you rule out first?
   - **Answer:** hypoactive delirium.
   - **Why:** very old patients tend toward a quiet form that may be mistaken for depression. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium)
   - **Trap:** starting an antidepressant without a medical workup.
