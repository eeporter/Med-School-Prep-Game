## In one line
**Twins: count the placentas and sacs**, because sharing a placenta brings twin-twin transfusion. **Preterm labor (20-37 weeks): steroids for the lungs, magnesium for the brain under 32 weeks, a short tocolytic window and GBS cover.** **Ruptured membranes: confirm with a sterile speculum, then the plan depends on gestational age and infection.** **Past 41-42 weeks: the placenta ages, so deliver** (El-Husseiny OB pp. 57-72; MSD).

## Twins (El-Husseiny OB pp. 57-60)
- **Dizygotic (fraternal) twins** are most common. Risk factors: race, geography, family history and **ovulation induction** (twins in up to 10% with clomiphene and up to 30% with human menopausal gonadotropin). They are **always dichorionic, diamniotic** (pp. 57-58).
- **Monozygotic (identical) twins** have no known risk factors (p. 57). Their sharing depends on **when the zygote splits** (pp. 58-59):

| Split | Placentas / sacs | Main extra risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Up to 72 hours** (morula) | 2 placentas, 2 sacs (**di-di**) | Lowest risk of monozygotic twins |
| **Days 4-8** (blastocyst) | **1 placenta, 2 sacs (mono-di)** | **Twin-twin transfusion** (your notes: 15% of mono-di twins) |
| **Days 9-12** (embryonic disc) | 1 placenta, 1 sac (**mono-mono**) | TTTS and especially **cord entanglement** → fetal death; highest risk |
| **After day 12** | Conjoined | Usually lethal |

- Osmosis Physiology (p. 219) gives slightly different cut-offs (di-di 2-3 days, mono-di 3-8 days, mono-mono 8-13 days), the same order. It adds that fraternal twins occur in about 10 per 1,000 births (mothers often over 35, with high FSH) and identical twins in about 4 per 1,000.
- **Twin-twin transfusion** (p. 58): the twins share one placenta unequally through vessel connections.
  - **Donor:** less blood → **growth restriction, oligohydramnios, anemia**;
  - **Recipient:** more blood → **overgrowth, polyhydramnios, polycythemia**;
  - treatment: **fetoscopic laser** of the connecting vessels on the placental surface.
  - **Other source (SMFM 2024):** start ultrasound checks for TTTS at **16 weeks** and repeat at least every 2 weeks until delivery; fetoscopic laser is the standard treatment for stage II-IV TTTS found at 16-26 weeks.
- **Clues** (p. 59): **uterus larger than dates**, more **hyperemesis** (high hCG), and a very high maternal serum AFP (guide 36).
- **Complications** (p. 59): iron and folate anemia, **preeclampsia**, **preterm labor (about 50%)**, malpresentation (50%), cesarean delivery (50%), **postpartum hemorrhage**.
- **Care** (p. 60): iron and folate; watch blood pressure; teach preterm labor signs; serial ultrasound for TTTS (fluid difference between sacs).
  - **Delivery:** **vaginal if both twins are head-first** (about 50%); **cesarean if the first twin is not head-first** (p. 60). MSD agrees.
  - **After delivery:** watch for **postpartum hemorrhage from atony** of the overstretched uterus (p. 60).
- **Other source (MSD, Jul 2024):** about 31 twin births per 1,000 US births (2021); assisted reproduction raises the rate; twins deliver at about **35 weeks** on average; consider **aspirin** to prevent preeclampsia; fetal testing from 32 weeks.

## Preterm labor (El-Husseiny OB pp. 65-68)
- **Preterm birth** is the most common cause of perinatal illness and death; about 12% of pregnancies (p. 65).
- **Preterm labor needs all three** (p. 65):
  1. **20 to under 37 weeks**;
  2. **at least 3 contractions in 30 minutes**;
  3. **cervical change** on repeat exams, or dilation over 2 cm on one exam.
- **Risk factors** (p. 66): **prior spontaneous preterm birth (the strongest)**, a **short cervix** (under 25 mm on transvaginal ultrasound), PROM, twins, cervical surgery, uterine anomalies.
- **Fetal fibronectin** (p. 67): the "glue" between the placenta and the uterine lining leaks into the vagina when birth is near. Its value is a **negative** result (birth unlikely soon); a positive result means about a 50% chance.

### The management bundle (pp. 66-67)
| Step | Why | Your notes | Other source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Betamethasone IM** | Fetal type II pneumocytes make **surfactant**; less respiratory distress, brain bleeding, necrotizing enterocolitis and death | One course at 24-34 weeks if birth is likely within 7 days (p. 67) | **Recent change** below |
| **IV MgSO₄** | **Fetal neuroprotection**: less cerebral palsy | If birth is expected before **32 weeks** (pp. 66-67) | MSD: consider under 32 weeks |
| **Tocolytic** | Buys up to 48 hours for steroids and transfer to a center with a neonatal ICU | Not more than 48 hours (p. 66) | MSD: a calcium channel blocker or a prostaglandin inhibitor; "No tocolytic is clearly the first-line choice" |
| **GBS prophylaxis** | Protects against newborn GBS sepsis | IV penicillin G after cultures (p. 66) | MSD: penicillin G, ampicillin, cefazolin, clindamycin or vancomycin, pending negative cultures |

- NBME TC p. 100 shows why lung maturity matters: a 32-week newborn of a diabetic mother with respiratory distress from surfactant deficiency. Pharm HY (PDF 148): betamethasone is the steroid for fetal lung maturity.
- **Correction (GBS drug):** your notes say "IV penicillin G (or erythromycin)" (p. 66). The CDC's 2010 guideline says **erythromycin "is no longer an acceptable alternative for intrapartum GBS prophylaxis"** (Am Fam Physician 2011). Penicillin-allergic women get **cefazolin** if their anaphylaxis risk is low, and **clindamycin** (if the strain is susceptible) or **vancomycin** if it is high. MSD's list has no erythromycin either. (Erythromycin is still correct as a **latency antibiotic** in preterm ruptured membranes, below; that is a different job.)
- **Recent change (steroids beyond 24-34 weeks):** MSD now lists corticosteroids
  - at **34 0/7 to 36 6/7 weeks** (late preterm) if birth is likely within 7 days;
  - from **23 0/7 weeks** if birth is likely within 7 days, and at **22 weeks** if newborn resuscitation is planned.
  - Dose: **betamethasone 12 mg IM every 24 hours for 2 doses** (or dexamethasone 6 mg IM every 12 hours for 4 doses). A repeat course only under 34 weeks and at least 7 days after the first.

### Tocolytic drugs (pp. 67-68)
| Drug | How it works | Side effects | Avoid if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **MgSO₄** | Competes with calcium | Weakness, breathing depression, pulmonary edema; overdose → **IV calcium gluconate**; follow reflexes (guide 37) | Kidney failure, myasthenia gravis |
| **Terbutaline** (β₂ agonist) | Relaxes myometrium | **β₁ effects**: tachycardia, hypertension; also **hyperglycemia, hypokalemia**, pulmonary edema | Heart disease, diabetes, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism |
| **Nifedipine** (calcium channel blocker) | Lowers calcium in muscle cells | Reflex tachycardia, **hypotension** | Hypotension |
| **Indomethacin** (prostaglandin inhibitor) | Less prostaglandin → weaker contractions | **Oligohydramnios**, **ductus arteriosus closure in the womb**, newborn necrotizing enterocolitis | **Over 32 weeks** |

- Pharm HY lists atosiban (an oxytocin antagonist), isoxsuprine and ritodrine as tocolytics, and notes MgSO₄ can be used in heart disease (PDF 147). It shows indomethacin closing the ductus (PDF 148).
- **Other source (MSD):** prostaglandin inhibitors "may cause transient oligohydramnios and fetal renal damage if used for more than 48 consecutive hours" and are contraindicated after 32 weeks.
- **Other source (FDA 2011, terbutaline):** your notes list terbutaline as a short tocolytic option for up to 48 hours (p. 66), and the FDA still allows that. Its Drug Safety Communication (17 Feb 2011) says injectable terbutaline "should not be used in pregnant women for prevention or prolonged treatment (beyond 48-72 hours) of preterm labor" because of serious maternal heart problems and death, and **"oral terbutaline should not be used for prevention or any treatment of preterm labor"**. The FDA's petition response says short (48-72 hours or less) in-hospital use under the skin "has clinical utility". It still appears on exams as the β₂-agonist example, so know its side effects.

### Preventing a repeat preterm birth (p. 66)
- **Your notes:** weekly IM **17-OHPC** after a prior spontaneous preterm birth (plus **cerclage** if the cervix is under 25 mm before 24 weeks); **daily vaginal progesterone** if the cervix is under 20 mm before 24 weeks with no prior preterm birth. For twins, no intervention has proven benefit (p. 67).
- **Recent change (17-OHPC withdrawn):** on **6 April 2023** the FDA withdrew approval of **Makena (17-OHPC) and its generics**, because the confirmatory study "did not verify clinical benefit". MSD: injectable progestins are no longer recommended; with a prior preterm birth and a cervix **under 25 mm before 24 weeks**, it is reasonable to offer **cerclage or vaginal progesterone**.

## Premature (prelabor) rupture of membranes (El-Husseiny OB pp. 69-70)
- **Definition:** membranes rupture **before labor starts**, at term or preterm (p. 69). MSD: **PPROM** = before 37 weeks.
- **Risk factors** (p. 69): **ascending infection** (most common), local membrane defects, **smoking**.
- **Picture** (p. 69): a sudden **gush of clear fluid**; low fluid (oligohydramnios) on ultrasound.
- **Diagnosis by sterile speculum** (p. 69):
  - **pooling** of fluid in the posterior fornix;
  - **Nitrazine positive**: fluid turns pH paper **blue** (amniotic fluid is alkaline);
  - **ferning** when dried on a slide.
  - **Other source (MSD):** avoid digital (finger) exams unless delivery is imminent. Nitrazine can be falsely positive with blood, semen, antiseptics, urine or bacterial vaginosis.
- **Correction (diagnosing infection):** your notes say chorioamnionitis needs "all the following criteria" (maternal fever, uterine tenderness, maternal and fetal tachycardia, foul fluid, purulent discharge) (p. 69). MSD (intraamniotic infection): **maternal fever plus one or more** of a high maternal white count, **fetal tachycardia** or **purulent cervical discharge**. Waiting for every sign delays treatment.
  - Treatment (MSD): **IV ampicillin + gentamicin**; after a cesarean, add one dose of clindamycin or metronidazole. Infection alone "is rarely an indication for cesarean delivery". Your notes: start broad IV antibiotics and **deliver promptly** (p. 69).
- **Tocolysis:** your notes say contractions after PROM are a contraindication (p. 69). MSD calls tocolysis in PPROM "controversial", decided case by case.

### Management by gestational age (your notes p. 70, with MSD)
| Weeks | Your notes | MSD (following ACOG) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Before viability (under 23-24)** | Poor outcome; high risk of **pulmonary hypoplasia**; induce or manage at home | Counsel; expectant care or induction; antibiotics can be considered from 20 6/7 weeks; no GBS prophylaxis, steroids, tocolysis or MgSO₄ before viability |
| **24-33** | Hospital, **betamethasone if under 32 weeks**, cultures, **7 days of ampicillin + erythromycin**; deliver for infection, worsening status, or at **34 weeks** | Steroids, **latency antibiotics** (48 h IV ampicillin + erythromycin, then 5 days oral amoxicillin + erythromycin; one oral azithromycin 1 g dose can replace erythromycin), GBS care, **MgSO₄ if under 32 weeks** |
| **34-36 6/7** | (Your notes call over 34 weeks "term") | Delivery or expectant care, decided together; steroids if none given and delivery is likely within 1-7 days |
| **37 and over** | Prompt delivery: oxytocin or prostaglandins, or cesarean | Delivery; GBS prophylaxis if indicated |

- **Correction (steroid cut-off and "term"):** your notes' PROM page gives betamethasone only "if <32 weeks" and calls over 34 weeks "term" (p. 70). Your own preterm labor page gives steroids at **24-34 weeks** (p. 67), and MSD gives them for rupture at **23-34 weeks** (with a late-preterm option). **Term starts at 37 weeks**; 34-36 weeks is late preterm.
- **Pulmonary hypoplasia:** Osmosis (p. 876) lists severe oligohydramnios and **mid-trimester rupture of membranes** as causes; too little fluid means too little lung stretch, so the lungs stay small. MSD: prolonged PPROM before 24 weeks can cause limb deformities and pulmonary hypoplasia (**Potter sequence**).

## Post-term pregnancy (El-Husseiny OB pp. 71-72)
- **Definition:** **over 42 weeks** from the first day of the last period (p. 71). MSD: **late-term = 41 0/7 to 41 6/7 weeks**; **postterm = 42 weeks or more**.
- **Cause:** usually unknown; more common in young first-time mothers (p. 71).
- **Why it matters** (p. 71): perinatal death rises **2-3 times** as the placenta ages.
  - **Macrosomia (80%)**: the placenta keeps working → a large baby → **shoulder dystocia** and brachial plexus injury;
  - **Dysmaturity (20%)**: the placenta fails (infarcts, scarring) → loss of fat under the skin, **asphyxia**, **oligohydramnios** → **cord compression**, and hypoxia → **meconium** passage.
- **Your notes' plan** (pp. 71-72):
  - **sure dates, favorable cervix:** induce with **IV oxytocin** and artificial rupture of membranes;
  - **sure dates, unfavorable cervix:** ripen with **prostaglandin E₂** then oxytocin, **or** wait with twice-weekly NST and AFI;
  - **unsure dates:** twice-weekly NST and AFI, and deliver if the fetus is in danger;
  - **oligohydramnios means delivery** even if other tests are normal.
- **Other source (MSD, Jul 2024):** start fetal surveillance at **41 weeks** (NST, modified BPP or BPP). "Induction of labor should be considered after 41 weeks and is **recommended after 42 weeks and no later than 42 6/7 weeks**." Waiting with testing alone past 42 weeks is no longer the advised plan.

## Clinical vignette
A 31-year-old woman at 20 weeks with a **monochorionic-diamniotic** twin pregnancy has a fundal height of 26 cm. Ultrasound shows one twin with a **very large amount of fluid** and a large bladder, and the other twin **small, with almost no fluid** around it and a bladder that cannot be seen; it looks stuck against the uterine wall (a "stuck twin", standard teaching).

- **Diagnosis:** **twin-twin transfusion syndrome** (El-Husseiny OB p. 58).
- **Why:** the twins share one placenta, and vessel connections send blood from the **donor** (growth restriction, oligohydramnios, anemia) to the **recipient** (overgrowth, polyhydramnios, polycythemia) (p. 58).
- **When it happens:** monozygotic twins that split on **days 4-8** (blastocyst) (p. 58).
- **Treatment:** **fetoscopic laser** of the connecting vessels (p. 58); SMFM: the standard treatment for stage II-IV TTTS at 16-26 weeks.
- **Prevention of late discovery:** ultrasound every 2 weeks from 16 weeks in monochorionic twins (SMFM 2024).

## Common traps
- **Dizygotic twins are always di-di**; the timing table applies only to monozygotic twins (p. 58).
- **Mono-mono = cord entanglement**, the highest-risk twins (p. 59).
- **TTTS donor = small and dry; recipient = big, wet and polycythemic** (p. 58).
- **Preterm labor needs cervical change**, not just contractions (p. 65).
- **MgSO₄ under 32 weeks protects the fetal brain**; it is not mainly a tocolytic (pp. 66-67; MSD).
- **Indomethacin: no more than 48 hours and not after 32 weeks** (oligohydramnios, ductus closure) (p. 68; MSD).
- **17-OHPC (Makena) is withdrawn** (FDA 2023); use vaginal progesterone or cerclage (MSD).
- **Steroids now reach 34-36 weeks** in some cases (MSD).
- **Suspected PROM: speculum, not fingers** (MSD).
- **Infection = fever plus one sign**, not all of them (MSD).
- **Term is 37 weeks**; late-term 41 weeks; postterm 42 weeks (MSD).
- **Post-term: induce by 42 6/7 weeks** (MSD).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 29-year-old woman who conceived with ovulation induction has an ultrasound at 11 weeks showing twins of different sexes. Which arrangement of placentas and sacs is expected?
   - **Answer:** **two chorions and two amnions (dichorionic-diamniotic).**
   - **Why:** twins of different sexes must be **dizygotic**, and dizygotic twins are always dichorionic-diamniotic (El-Husseiny OB pp. 57-58). Ovulation induction raises the dizygotic twin rate (p. 57).
   - **Trap:** **monochorionic-diamniotic**, which happens only with monozygotic twins that split on days 4-8.

2. A 27-year-old woman with a spontaneous twin pregnancy has an ultrasound at 12 weeks showing two fetuses of the same sex, one placenta and a thin membrane between two sacs. Which complication is this pregnancy specifically at risk for, compared with twins that each have their own placenta?
   - **Answer:** **twin-twin transfusion syndrome.**
   - **Why:** one placenta with two sacs is **monochorionic-diamniotic**. The shared placenta can connect the twins' circulations unequally (El-Husseiny OB p. 58). SMFM advises ultrasound every 2 weeks from 16 weeks.
   - **Trap:** **umbilical cord entanglement**, which is the special risk of **monoamniotic** twins (one sac) (p. 59).

3. A 33-year-old woman at 36 weeks with dichorionic-diamniotic twins is in active labor. Ultrasound shows the first twin in breech presentation and the second twin head-down. There are no other complications. Which delivery plan is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **cesarean delivery.**
   - **Why:** twins deliver vaginally only if the first (presenting) twin is head-first; a nonvertex first twin means cesarean (El-Husseiny OB p. 60; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **vaginal delivery because the second twin is head-down**. The presenting twin decides the route.

4. A 25-year-old woman at 30 weeks has regular painful contractions, 4 in 30 minutes. Her cervix has gone from 1 cm to 3 cm dilated in 2 hours. Membranes are intact, and she has no fever. She is given betamethasone and nifedipine. Which additional drug most reduces the baby's risk of cerebral palsy?
   - **Answer:** **IV magnesium sulfate.**
   - **Why:** this is preterm labor (20-37 weeks, contractions and cervical change) (El-Husseiny OB p. 65). Before 32 weeks, maternal MgSO₄ gives **fetal neuroprotection** (pp. 66-67; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **a second tocolytic such as indomethacin** to stop contractions longer. Tocolysis only buys about 48 hours (p. 66) and does not protect the brain (p. 67).

5. A 28-year-old woman at 35 weeks has preterm labor that is not progressing quickly; delivery is expected within the next few days. She has not had corticosteroids before and has no diabetes. Which is the most appropriate medication to give now?
   - **Answer:** **betamethasone (12 mg IM, 2 doses 24 hours apart).**
   - **Why:** MSD now lists corticosteroids at 34 0/7-36 6/7 weeks when delivery within 7 days is likely, to reduce newborn breathing problems.
   - **Trap:** **no steroids because she is past 34 weeks**. That was the older cut-off in your notes (p. 67).

6. A 26-year-old woman at 29 weeks in preterm labor receives indomethacin for 4 days because contractions keep returning. Which finding is most likely on her next ultrasound?
   - **Answer:** **low amniotic fluid (oligohydramnios).**
   - **Why:** prostaglandin inhibitors reduce fetal kidney output and can narrow the ductus arteriosus; MSD: they "may cause transient oligohydramnios and fetal renal damage if used for more than 48 consecutive hours" (El-Husseiny OB p. 68).
   - **Trap:** **polyhydramnios**, the opposite of what reduced fetal urine does.

7. A 30-year-old woman at 31 weeks in preterm labor receives a β₂-agonist tocolytic. Two hours later her pulse is 132/min, she has palpitations, her serum glucose is 210 mg/dL, and her potassium is 3.1 mEq/L. Which drug most likely caused these findings?
   - **Answer:** **terbutaline.**
   - **Why:** terbutaline relaxes the uterus through β₂ receptors, but also causes tachycardia and hypertension (β₁ effects), hyperglycemia and hypokalemia (El-Husseiny OB p. 68). The FDA warns against using it for prolonged tocolysis because of serious heart harm.
   - **Trap:** **nifedipine**, which causes hypotension and reflex tachycardia, not high glucose and low potassium (p. 68).

8. A 31-year-old woman had a spontaneous preterm birth at 31 weeks in her last pregnancy. Now at 20 weeks, a transvaginal ultrasound shows a cervical length of 18 mm. She has no contractions. Which intervention is most appropriate to reduce her risk of another preterm birth?
   - **Answer:** **cerclage or daily vaginal progesterone.**
   - **Why:** a prior preterm birth plus a cervix under 25 mm before 24 weeks: MSD says it is reasonable to offer cerclage or vaginal progesterone. Prior preterm birth is the strongest risk factor (El-Husseiny OB p. 66).
   - **Trap:** **weekly IM 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate**, which your notes list (p. 66) but which the FDA withdrew in April 2023.

9. A 24-year-old woman at 32 weeks has a sudden gush of clear fluid from the vagina. She has no contractions or fever. Which is the most appropriate first examination?
   - **Answer:** **a sterile speculum exam** to look for pooling and test the fluid with Nitrazine paper and for ferning.
   - **Why:** PROM is diagnosed by pooling, a blue Nitrazine test and ferning (El-Husseiny OB p. 69). MSD: avoid digital exams unless delivery is imminent, because they raise infection risk.
   - **Trap:** **a digital cervical exam** to check dilation.

10. A 27-year-old woman at 29 weeks has confirmed rupture of membranes. She has no contractions, fever or uterine tenderness, and the fetal heart tracing is reassuring. She receives betamethasone and magnesium sulfate. Which antibiotic plan is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **IV ampicillin plus erythromycin for 48 hours, then oral amoxicillin plus erythromycin for 5 days** (latency antibiotics).
    - **Why:** latency antibiotics lengthen the time to delivery and lower infection while steroids work (El-Husseiny OB p. 70; MSD). One oral azithromycin dose can replace erythromycin (MSD).
    - **Trap:** **no antibiotics until she has a fever**. Waiting misses the benefit of prophylaxis.

11. A 23-year-old woman at 33 weeks with membranes ruptured for 2 days develops a temperature of 38.7°C. The fetal heart rate baseline is 175/min. She has no other symptoms, her uterus is not tender, and the fluid has no odor. Which is the most appropriate next step?
    - **Answer:** **IV ampicillin plus gentamicin and delivery.**
    - **Why:** maternal fever plus fetal tachycardia is enough to diagnose **intraamniotic infection** (MSD). Treat with ampicillin + gentamicin and deliver (your notes p. 69: broad IV antibiotics and prompt delivery).
    - **Trap:** **watch and wait until uterine tenderness and foul fluid appear**. Your notes' "all criteria" rule (p. 69) is stricter than current criteria.

12. A woman's membranes ruptured at 18 weeks, and she chose to continue the pregnancy. Amniotic fluid stayed very low. The baby is born at 30 weeks with a small, bell-shaped chest and severe breathing failure despite surfactant. Which mechanism best explains the breathing failure?
    - **Answer:** **pulmonary hypoplasia from lack of amniotic fluid** during lung development.
    - **Why:** severe oligohydramnios and mid-trimester rupture of membranes reduce lung stretch and growth (Osmosis p. 876). Your notes warn that previable PROM carries a high risk of pulmonary hypoplasia (El-Husseiny OB p. 70).
    - **Trap:** **surfactant deficiency alone**. That causes newborn respiratory distress in preterm babies (NBME TC p. 100), but it responds to surfactant, while small lungs do not (standard teaching).

13. A healthy 24-year-old woman in her first pregnancy is at 42 weeks and 1 day by a first-trimester ultrasound. Her cervix is closed, long and firm. A nonstress test is reactive, and amniotic fluid is normal. Which is the most appropriate management?
    - **Answer:** **induce labor, ripening the cervix first with a prostaglandin, then oxytocin.**
    - **Why:** MSD: induction "is recommended after 42 weeks and no later than 42 6/7 weeks". With an unfavorable cervix, ripen with prostaglandin E₂ and then give oxytocin (El-Husseiny OB p. 72).
    - **Trap:** **wait for spontaneous labor with twice-weekly testing**. Your notes allow this (p. 72), but current guidance recommends induction past 42 weeks.

14. A boy is born at 43 weeks. He is long and thin, with dry, peeling skin, long fingernails and green-stained skin and cord (classic postmaturity findings, standard teaching). Amniotic fluid was low before delivery. Which process best explains his appearance?
    - **Answer:** **declining placental function late in pregnancy (dysmaturity syndrome).**
    - **Why:** in about 20% of post-term pregnancies, an aging, scarred placenta cannot support the fetus: the baby loses fat under the skin, low fluid allows cord compression, and hypoxia causes meconium passage (El-Husseiny OB p. 71; MSD postmaturity syndrome).
    - **Trap:** **fetal macrosomia**, the other post-term pattern (80%), where the placenta keeps working and the baby is large (p. 71).
