# Acute decompensated heart failure and cardiogenic shock

## In one line
A failing pump drops cardiac output; the body answers with sympathetic and renin-angiotensin activation that holds blood pressure but adds fluid and afterload, which makes the heart fail more. Cardiogenic shock is the end of that spiral: low output, high wedge pressure, poor perfusion.

## Core mechanism
1. Cardiac output (CO) falls, so the kidneys and baroreceptors sense low perfusion. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
2. The sympathetic system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) switch on: vasoconstriction (more afterload) and sodium and water retention (more preload and congestion). [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
3. Angiotensin II also drives myocyte hypertrophy and fibrosis; the higher workload and oxygen demand lead to myocyte death, which further activates the same systems. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
4. Natriuretic peptides (BNP, ANP) rise to fight sodium retention but cannot keep up. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
5. In cardiogenic shock, low output and low blood pressure reduce coronary perfusion, which worsens ischemia and contractility (the "vicious spiral"). Acute myocardial infarction is the most common cause. [StatPearls: Cardiogenic Shock](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482255/)
6. **Your notes' version of the vicious circle (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 159):** low renal perfusion means less stretch of the afferent arteriole, so renin rises; angiotensin II raises afterload, which the weak heart cannot pump against, so perfusion falls further and even more renin is released. Aldosterone adds volume (preload). Baroreceptors also switch on the sympathetic system, which speeds and strengthens the heart but raises afterload too.
7. **Natriuretic peptides (your notes, p. 78):** ANP comes from the atria (volume and pressure) and BNP from the ventricles (wall tension). Both act through **cyclic GMP**: they dilate the afferent and constrict the efferent arteriole (more GFR), block proximal sodium reabsorption and suppress renin and aldosterone. A BNP test has a very good **negative predictive value** for heart failure.

## High-yield facts
- Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF): EF 40% or less. Preserved (HFpEF): EF 50% or more. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/) Your notes use the same cutoffs (Osmosis HY Cardiovascular, p. 55). Systolic failure = low EF and a big end-diastolic volume (after MI, dilated cardiomyopathy); diastolic failure = normal EF and normal volume but a stiff ventricle with high filling pressure (hypertrophy, hypertension, obesity, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis) (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, pp. 57, 157).
- **Left-sided failure in your notes:** dyspnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (more venous return lying flat), orthopnea and crackles; small lung capillaries burst and macrophages eat the blood, giving hemosiderin-laden **"heart failure cells"** that stain with Prussian blue. **Right-sided failure:** JVD, a painful **"nutmeg" liver** (can lead to cardiac cirrhosis) and pitting edema. The most common cause of right failure is left failure (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, pp. 157-158).
- Chest X-ray in acute decompensation: prominent vessels, patchy airspace opacities, pleural effusions and **Kerley B lines** (edema in the septa between lobules) (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 161).
- An S3 is normal in children, athletes and pregnancy; in older adults it means a volume-overloaded ventricle (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 51).
- **Osmosis mnemonics:** **FAILURE** for what tips stable heart failure into decompensation: Forgot medication, Arrhythmia or Anemia, Ischemia, Infarction or Infection, Lifestyle (too much salt), Upregulation of output (pregnancy, hyperthyroidism), Renal failure, Embolism. **POND** for the first steps in acute decompensation: Position upright (with or without positive-pressure ventilation), Oxygen, Nitrates, Diuretics (Osmosis HY Cardiovascular, pp. 56-57).
- **Warm/cold and wet/dry profiles (Osmosis, p. 57):** warm and dry = compensated; warm and wet = congested (high wedge pressure, normal cardiac index); cold and dry = low flow (low cardiac index, low or normal wedge); cold and wet = decompensated (low cardiac index, high wedge), the picture of cardiogenic shock.
- Left-sided failure backs up into the lungs: rales, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, S3. Right-sided failure backs up into the veins: jugular venous distention, leg edema, congested liver. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
- Cardiogenic shock definition: systolic BP 90 mmHg or less for 30 minutes or more, cardiac index 2.2 L/min/m² or less, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) above 15 mmHg. [StatPearls: Cardiogenic Shock](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482255/)
- BNP is falsely high in kidney dysfunction and atrial fibrillation and falsely low in obesity. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/); the obesity point is also on [MSD Manual: Acute Heart Failure](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/heart-failure/acute-heart-failure)

## Drug mechanisms in acute and chronic heart failure
| Drug | Mechanism | Hemodynamic effect | Key adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Furosemide (loop diuretic) | Blocks the Na-K-2Cl (NKCC2) cotransporter in the thick ascending limb | Lowers plasma volume and preload, relieves congestion | Low K, low Mg, metabolic alkalosis, ototoxicity, sulfa allergy caution, calcium loss |
| Dobutamine | Beta-1 > beta-2 > alpha agonist | More contractility and cardiac output; mild vasodilation lowers blood pressure a little (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, pp. 206-207) | Tachycardia, arrhythmia; raises oxygen demand, so it can worsen ischemia; your notes say not for routine use in decompensated failure, but worth it in cardiogenic shock |
| Milrinone | Phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE-3) inhibitor, raises cyclic AMP | More contractility and output; peripheral vasodilation; lower filling pressures. Your notes: acute heart failure only (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, pp. 205-206) | Hypotension, arrhythmia |
| Digoxin | Blocks the Na-K ATPase, so less calcium leaves through the Na-Ca exchanger and contraction strengthens; also raises vagal tone (slows the AV node) | Symptom relief in systolic heart failure; rate control in atrial fibrillation (second line) | Narrow therapeutic index: nausea, confusion, **yellow vision**, any arrhythmia, **hyperkalemia** in toxicity; worse with **low potassium**, kidney failure, verapamil, amiodarone, quinidine; treat with anti-digoxin Fab (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, pp. 203-205) |
| Norepinephrine | Mainly alpha-1, plus beta-1; negligible beta-2 | Raises blood pressure mainly by vasoconstriction (more afterload); preferred over dopamine in severe hypotension | Heart rate changes little or can fall, because the baroreflex slows the heart as pressure rises (reflex bradycardia, standard teaching); arrhythmia; limb or gut ischemia; tissue necrosis if it leaks out of the vein |
| ARNI, ACE inhibitor or ARB | Block RAAS (ARNI adds neprilysin inhibition: sacubitril stops the breakdown of ANP and BNP, and valsartan is added because neprilysin also breaks down angiotensin II; El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 208) | Lower afterload, slow remodeling | Hyperkalemia, kidney function drop; cough with ACE inhibitors; angioedema (ACE inhibitors; never combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARNI) |
| Beta-blockers (carvedilol, bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate) | Block sympathetic overdrive | Slow remodeling; reduce mortality | Avoid starting in shock or decompensation |
| Mineralocorticoid antagonist (spironolactone) | Blocks aldosterone | Less sodium retention and fibrosis | Hyperkalemia (avoid if K above 5.0); gynecomastia with spironolactone (eplerenone avoids it) |
| SGLT2 inhibitor | See [07-sglt2-inhibitors-and-statins.md](07-sglt2-inhibitors-and-statins.md) | Reduces HF hospitalization and death, with or without diabetes | Genital infections, euglycemic DKA |

The bottom four rows reduce mortality in HFrEF ("the four pillars"); the top rows treat the acute crisis. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)

## Compare and contrast: shock types
| Shock type | Cardiac output | Filling pressure (PCWP / CVP) | Systemic vascular resistance | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hypovolemic | Down | Down (PAOP below 8 mmHg) | Up (compensatory vasoconstriction) | Hemorrhage, vomiting |
| Cardiogenic | Down | Up (PCWP above 15 mmHg) | Up (sympathetic vasoconstriction) | Large anterior MI |
| Distributive (septic) | Often up early | Down or normal (blood pools in veins) | Down | Sepsis, anaphylaxis |
| Obstructive | Down | Impaired filling (tamponade, tension pneumothorax); central venous pressure up; wedge pressure up in tamponade, low in massive pulmonary embolism (standard teaching) | Up | Cardiac tamponade |

Your notes' shock table (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 162) gives the same pattern: hypovolemic (cold, clammy; wedge ↓↓, output ↓, SVR ↑; fluids), cardiogenic (cold, clammy; wedge ↑, output ↓↓, SVR ↑; inotropes and diuresis), distributive (warm; wedge ↓, output ↑, SVR ↓↓; fluids and pressors, epinephrine for anaphylaxis), obstructive (relieve the obstruction), and a **neurogenic** row for CNS injury (dry skin; wedge, output and SVR all low).

Sources: [StatPearls: Shock](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK531492/), [MSD Manual: Shock](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/critical-care-medicine/shock-and-fluid-resuscitation/shock), [StatPearls: Cardiogenic Shock](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482255/). SVR "up" in obstructive shock is standard teaching; the cited pages state the low output but not SVR.

## Clinical vignette
A 66-year-old man, 2 days after a large anterior myocardial infarction, has BP 82/50 mmHg, cool mottled limbs, crackles at both lung bases and low urine output. A pulmonary artery catheter shows cardiac index 1.8 L/min/m² and PCWP 24 mmHg. What type of shock is this, and which beta-1 agonist increases contractility?

**Answer:** cardiogenic shock; dobutamine (a beta-1 agonist inotrope).
**Why:** low cardiac index (at or below 2.2) with high wedge pressure (above 15) after an MI means the pump has failed and blood backs up into the lungs. Dobutamine stimulates beta-1 receptors to raise contractility. [StatPearls: Cardiogenic Shock](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482255/), [MSD Manual: Acute Heart Failure](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/heart-failure/acute-heart-failure) Its beta-2 effect dilates vessels and can lower blood pressure further, so norepinephrine is often added when the patient is hypotensive (standard teaching).
**Trap:** giving large fluid boluses as in hypovolemic shock; the wedge pressure is already high, so fluid worsens pulmonary edema.

## Common traps
- High PCWP separates cardiogenic from hypovolemic shock; both have low output and cold skin.
- Septic shock is the one with **low** SVR and often warm skin early.
- Beta-blockers save lives in chronic HFrEF but are not started during shock or acute decompensation (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 157).
- Digoxin toxicity gives **high** potassium, but **low** potassium (for example from diuretics) makes toxicity more likely (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 204).
- Older patients can have a normal creatinine with poor kidney function (less muscle), so renally cleared drugs like digoxin still need lower doses (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 205).
- Never combine sacubitril-valsartan with an ACE inhibitor: angioedema (El-Husseiny Cardiovascular, p. 208).
- Loop diuretics waste calcium ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK546656/)); thiazides lower urine calcium and raise blood calcium ([StatPearls: Thiazides](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532918/)).

## Practice questions
1. Which hormone system explains edema and weight gain in worsening heart failure?
   - **Answer:** the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.
   - **Why:** low renal perfusion triggers renin; aldosterone retains sodium and water. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
   - **Trap:** natriuretic peptides are high in heart failure but act to *remove* sodium; they lose the battle.
2. A patient in shock has cardiac output 8 L/min, low SVR, and warm extremities. Which shock type?
   - **Answer:** distributive (septic) shock.
   - **Why:** vasodilation lowers SVR; output can be high early. [StatPearls: Shock](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK531492/), [MSD Manual: Shock](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/critical-care-medicine/shock-and-fluid-resuscitation/shock)
   - **Trap:** assuming all shock has low cardiac output.
3. Furosemide is started for pulmonary edema. Which ion transporter does it block, and in which segment?
   - **Answer:** NKCC2 (Na-K-2Cl cotransporter), thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle.
   - **Why:** blocking it stops sodium and chloride reabsorption, so water follows into the urine. [StatPearls: Loop Diuretics](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK546656/)
   - **Trap:** thiazides act on the sodium-chloride cotransporter in the distal convoluted tubule. [StatPearls: Thiazides](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532918/)
4. Which drug classes reduce mortality in HFrEF?
   - **Answer:** ARNI (or ACE inhibitor/ARB), beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid antagonist and SGLT2 inhibitor. Add hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate for patients of African ancestry, where it "may reduce mortality and hospitalization" on top of standard therapy. [MSD Manual: Medications for Heart Failure](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/heart-failure/medications-for-heart-failure)
   - **Why:** each blocks a harmful neurohormonal pathway or, for SGLT2 inhibitors, reduces HF hospitalization and death. [StatPearls: Heart Failure](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430873/)
   - **Trap:** loop diuretics relieve symptoms but have no proven mortality benefit (standard teaching; the cited page does not state it).
