## In one line
**Match the exposure to the bug, then the bug to the drug:**
- **Water:** *Giardia*, *E. histolytica* (cysts), *Cryptosporidium* (oocysts).
- **Warm fresh water up the nose:** *Naegleria*.
- **Insects:**
  - **mosquito:** malaria, *Wuchereria*;
  - **tick:** *Babesia*;
  - **tsetse fly:** African sleeping sickness;
  - **kissing bug:** Chagas;
  - **sandfly:** *Leishmania*;
  - **blackfly:** river blindness;
  - **deer fly:** *Loa loa*.
- **Bare feet in soil:** hookworm, *Strongyloides*.
- **Snail water on skin:** *Schistosoma*.
- **Undercooked meat or fish:** *Trichinella* and pork tapeworm (meat); fish tapeworm and liver fluke (fish).
- **Swallowed eggs:** pinworm, *Ascaris*, *Toxocara*, cysticercosis, hydatid cyst.
- **Gut and vaginal protozoa** (*Giardia*, amebae, *Trichomonas*): metronidazole (El-Husseiny Micro p. 357).
- **Most intestinal roundworms:** mebendazole or pyrantel pamoate (p. 358).
- **Most tapeworms and flukes:** praziquantel (p. 358).

## Life cycle words (El-Husseiny Micro p. 202)
- **Definitive host:** where the adult or sexual stage lives.
- **Intermediate host:** where larval or asexual stages develop.
- Parasites are protozoa (one cell) or metazoa (worms and arthropods).

## Intestinal protozoa (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 202-205)

| Organism | How you get it | Key clue | Diagnosis | Drug (your notes) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ***Giardia lamblia*** | Cysts in water (campers, hikers) | Bloating, gas, foul-smelling **fatty diarrhea**; trophozoites attach by a **ventral adhesive disk** and flatten villi | Cysts in stool | Metronidazole |
| ***Entamoeba histolytica*** | Cysts in water | Bloody diarrhea, **flask-shaped ulcers**, **liver abscess** | Trophozoites with **ingested RBCs** ("*Entamoeba* eats erythrocytes"), cysts with up to 4 nuclei, serology | Metronidazole; iodoquinol for cyst passers |
| ***Cryptosporidium*** | Oocysts in water | Mild watery diarrhea in healthy people, **severe chronic diarrhea in AIDS** | **Acid-fast oocysts** in stool | Nitazoxanide in immunocompetent hosts; prevent by filtering water |

- **Why *Giardia* gets chronic:** secretory **IgA** blocks the parasite from sticking, so IgA deficiency predisposes to chronic giardiasis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 202).
- **Other source (*Giardia* drugs):** MSD lists tinidazole, metronidazole, secnidazole or nitazoxanide. It adds that **acquired lactose intolerance** often accompanies giardiasis.
- **Other source (amebiasis drugs):** the CDC treats **invasive disease and liver abscess** with metronidazole or tinidazole, **immediately followed by** a luminal drug (paromomycin or iodoquinol). For symptom-free cyst passers, paromomycin or iodoquinol alone (CDC DPDx). Your notes name iodoquinol only. MSD explains why the second drug is needed: metronidazole and tinidazole "are not sufficient to eradicate cysts", so a second oral drug clears the cysts left in the intestine.
- **Liver abscess** is the most common form of invasive amebiasis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 204).

## CNS protozoa (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 206-209)

**Toxoplasma gondii**
- **Hosts:** the **cat** is the definitive host, where the sexual cycle happens. Humans are infected by:
  - oocysts from cat feces;
  - **undercooked meat** containing tissue cysts;
  - the placenta.
- **Two forms:** **tachyzoites**, which divide fast in acute infection, and **bradyzoites**, which grow slowly in tissue cysts.
- **AIDS:** encephalitis with **multiple ring-enhancing lesions in both hemispheres** (p. 206).
  - Your HIV table lists *Toxoplasma* as a brain abscess cause, with multiple ring-enhancing lesions on MRI (p. 283).
  - The main alternative is **primary CNS lymphoma**, a B-cell tumor linked to EBV. It can be **solitary** (pp. 208, 283).
- **Prevention in AIDS:** TMP-SMX when CD4 is below 100 cells/mm³ covers both *Pneumocystis* and *Toxoplasma* (p. 367).
- **Treatment:** **pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine** (pp. 208, 357).

**Congenital toxoplasmosis**
- **Classic triad:** **chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus and intracranial calcifications** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 207, 318; MSD).
- **Prevention:** pregnant women should avoid undercooked meat and should not handle cat litter (p. 207).
- **Correction (timing):** your notes say the fetus "is affected only if the mother is infected ... during the first six months" (p. 207). MSD says the opposite pattern:
  - **transmission is more likely the later in pregnancy** the mother is infected;
  - **disease is more severe the earlier** the fetus is infected.
- **Treatment in pregnancy (MSD):**
  - **Spiramycin** is used to **prevent transmission** when the mother is infected **before 18 weeks**.
  - **Pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine and folinic acid** are used at or after 18 weeks, or when fetal infection is confirmed.
  - Infants get pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine and folinic acid, usually for 12 months.
- **Correction (Pharm HY):** PDF 123 calls spiramycin the "DOC for Toxoplasmosis". It is the drug for the **pregnant woman before 18 weeks** (your Pharm HY PDF 126 says "Toxoplasma in pregnancy"). The treatment of toxoplasmosis itself is pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine (El-Husseiny Micro p. 208; MSD).

***Naegleria fowleri* (primary amebic meningoencephalitis)**
- **Exposure:** swimming in fresh water. The amoeba enters the brain through the **cribriform plate** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 208).
- **Course:** acute, fulminant and almost always fatal, the "brain-eating amoeba". **Motile trophozoites in CSF** (p. 209).
- **Other source (treatment):** your notes say amphotericin B helped a few survivors (p. 209). The CDC now recommends a **combination**: amphotericin B (IV and intrathecal), **miltefosine**, azithromycin, posaconazole, rifampin and dexamethasone. It counts **five well-documented survivors in North America** (CDC, reviewed Nov 2025).

***Trypanosoma brucei* (African sleeping sickness)** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 209)
- **Spread:** **tsetse fly** bite.
- **Signs:** painful chancre at the bite and big lymph nodes. Then **recurring fevers** from **antigenic variation** of the surface coat, then somnolence and coma.
- **Diagnosis:** trypomastigotes in blood or CSF.
- **Your notes' drugs:** suramin (early) or melarsoprol (late, CNS); "arsenicals" for African disease (p. 357).
- **Recent change:**
  - **Oral fexinidazole** is now WHO's first-line treatment for *T. b. rhodesiense* (June 2024). DNDi also reports that its access activities for *T. b. gambiense* disease were completed at the end of 2024 (DNDi 2024 review).
  - On **27 Feb 2026** the EMA gave a positive opinion for **acoziborole**, a **single oral dose** for *T. b. gambiense*, first and second stage. It went through the EU-M4all route, for use outside the EU.

## Blood protozoa

**Malaria** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 210-212)
- **Spread:** female ***Anopheles*** mosquito.
- **Life cycle:**
  - sporozoites infect **liver cells**, where they multiply asexually into merozoites;
  - merozoites invade RBCs and become ring trophozoites, then schizonts;
  - schizonts burst the RBCs: **fever, anemia** and more merozoites.
- **Species:**
  - ***P. vivax / P. ovale***: **48-hour (tertian)** fevers. Dormant liver **hypnozoites** cause relapses (p. 211).
  - ***P. falciparum***: severe disease, **irregular fever**. Infected RBCs stick to the endothelium and block small vessels in the **brain (cerebral malaria), kidneys and lungs** (pp. 211-212).
  - ***P. malariae***: **72-hour (quartan)** fevers (p. 212).
- **Diagnosis:** usually a peripheral blood thin smear (p. 212).
- **Protection:** your NBME notes give sickle cell trait's genetic advantage as **protection against *P. falciparum*** (NBME TC p. 218). MSD adds that people who lack the **Duffy blood group** are resistant to *P. vivax*.
- **Correction (sequestration):** your notes say schizont-carrying RBCs are "sequestered in the spleen" when "sequestrins" bind endothelium in that organ (p. 211).
  - MSD: infected RBCs **adhere to vascular endothelium**, blocking microvessels in the **brain, kidneys, lungs and gut**. The spleen enlarges because it **clears** damaged RBCs.
  - The parasite protein that does the sticking is **PfEMP1**. It binds endothelial receptors such as **CD36 and ICAM-1**, which pulls infected cells out of the circulation. The usual explanation is that this lets them **avoid being cleared by the spleen** (Fonager et al., *J Exp Med* 2012).
  - For exams: falciparum sticks in **deep microvessels (brain)**, not in the spleen.

**Malaria drugs**
- **Your notes:**
  - **chloroquine** blocks heme detoxification into hemozoin. It is used for non-falciparum species because falciparum resistance is so common (El-Husseiny Micro p. 358);
  - **primaquine** is added for *vivax/ovale* to kill **hypnozoites** (p. 212). It causes **hemolysis in G6PD deficiency** (p. 358; Pharm HY PDF 139);
  - **mefloquine prophylaxis** continues **4 weeks after return** (p. 212; MSD agrees). MSD: atovaquone-proguanil continues 7 days after return, doxycycline 4 weeks;
  - **quinine** causes **cinchonism** (p. 358; Pharm HY PDF 139);
  - **chloroquine** can cause **retinopathy** (p. 359; Pharm HY PDF 139 "bull's eye maculopathy");
  - **artemisinin combinations** such as artemether + lumefantrine treat falciparum (Pharm HY PDF 141; El-Husseiny Micro p. 358).
- **Recent change (severe malaria):** your notes say to use "quinidine in US (quinine elsewhere) or artesunate" (El-Husseiny Micro p. 359).
  - IV quinidine was **discontinued in 2019**, and since April 2019 **IV artesunate** has been first-line for severe malaria in the US (CDC, 28 Mar 2019).
  - The CDC's 2026 algorithm treats severe malaria of any species with **IV artesunate** (CDC, updated Aug 2026). It does not mention quinidine.
- **Recent change (uncomplicated falciparum):** your notes give several options: "Treat P. falciparum with artemether/lumefantrine or atovaquone/proguanil" (p. 358), but also "Treatment: quinine" for chloroquine-resistant malaria (p. 358) and mefloquine for resistant species (p. 212).
  - The CDC now puts **artemether-lumefantrine first**, with atovaquone-proguanil second.
  - Mefloquine is used **only if nothing else is available**.
  - Chloroquine is still used for falciparum from Central America west of the Panama Canal, Haiti and the Dominican Republic (CDC 2026; MSD table).
- **Other source (relapse prevention):** your Pharm HY notes mention **tafenoquine** for radical cure of *P. vivax* (PDF 140).
  - CDC: give primaquine or tafenoquine only after a **quantitative G6PD test** shows the patient is not deficient.
  - Tafenoquine is only for patients 16 or older who got chloroquine (CDC 2026; MSD).

***Babesia*** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 213; MSD)
- **Spread:** ***Ixodes*** tick, the same tick as Lyme disease. Coinfection happens. Mostly in the **northeastern US**.
- **Signs:** fever and **hemolytic anemia**. **Asplenia** raises the risk of severe disease. MSD adds older age and advanced HIV.
- **Smear:** ring forms. The **"Maltese cross"** tetrad is not common, but it is unique to *Babesia* (MSD).
- **Treatment:** **atovaquone + azithromycin**. The alternative is quinine + clindamycin. Exchange transfusion is used for severe disease with parasitemia over 10% (MSD).

## Visceral protozoa

***Trypanosoma cruzi* (Chagas disease)** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 214-215; MSD)
- **Spread:** **reduviid (kissing) bug feces** rubbed into a painless bite. It is endemic in rural Central and South America. MSD adds transfusion, transplant, mother to baby, and contaminated food or juice.
- **Acute:**
  - **Romaña sign** (one-sided swelling around the eye);
  - a chagoma (an indurated skin lesion where the parasite entered) (MSD);
  - **myocarditis**, with amastigotes inside heart muscle cells (El-Husseiny Micro p. 214; Osmosis p. 90).
- **Chronic:**
  - **cardiomyopathy** with conduction problems (MSD);
  - destruction of the **myenteric plexus**, causing **megaesophagus (secondary achalasia), megacolon and megaureter** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 215; Osmosis p. 216).
- **Treatment:** **benznidazole or nifurtimox** (p. 215). MSD: treat all acute, congenital and reactivated cases. Do not use the drugs once advanced heart or gut disease has set in, or in pregnancy.
- **Correction (toxic megacolon):** your NBME notes answer "T. cruzi" to "what infectious etiology is most related to toxic megacolon?" in a patient with ulcerative colitis (NBME TC p. 162).
  - MedlinePlus lists the causes of **toxic megacolon** as poorly controlled ulcerative colitis or Crohn disease, **colon infections such as *Clostridioides difficile***, and ischemic colitis.
  - Chagas causes a **chronic megacolon** from nerve loss, with long periods of constipation and volvulus (MSD; El-Husseiny Micro p. 215).
  - For exams: **toxic megacolon + infection = *C. difficile***; **chronic megacolon in a patient from Latin America = Chagas**.

***Leishmania donovani*** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 215-216)
- **Spread:** **sandfly**.
- **Visceral (kala-azar):** spiking fevers, **hepatosplenomegaly**, **pancytopenia**.
- **Cutaneous:** a chronic pink papule that becomes a nodule or plaque.
- **Diagnosis:** **macrophages containing amastigotes** (p. 216).
- **Your notes' drugs:** amphotericin B or sodium stibogluconate (pp. 216, 357).
- **Other source:**
  - **Liposomal amphotericin B** is FDA-approved for visceral disease.
  - Oral **miltefosine** is FDA-approved for cutaneous, mucosal and visceral disease caused by certain species.
  - Sodium stibogluconate is **not FDA-approved or sold in the US** (CDC, reviewed Mar 2024).

## Sexually transmitted protozoan: *Trichomonas vaginalis* (El-Husseiny Micro p. 216; MSD)
- **Spread:** sexual only. It cannot form cysts, so it does not survive outside the body.
- **Signs:** foul-smelling, greenish discharge with itching and burning; **"strawberry cervix"**. Do not confuse it with *Gardnerella* (bacterial vaginosis).
- **Diagnosis:** **motile trophozoites on wet mount** (p. 216). MSD: **nucleic acid amplification tests** are more sensitive than microscopy. Look at the wet mount quickly, because trichomonads stop moving within minutes.
- **Treatment:** metronidazole for the patient and partner (p. 216).
- **Recent change (dose):**
  - **Women:** **metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days**. This replaced the single 2 g dose in the CDC's 2021 STI guidelines (UNMC review, 2021).
  - **Men:** a **single 2 g dose** is still used.
  - **Partners** from the past 60 days are treated (MSD).

## Roundworms (nematodes) (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 218-225)

| Worm | How you get it | Key clue | Drug (your notes) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ***Enterobius*** (pinworm) | Swallowed eggs; **autoinfection** from scratching | Most common US helminth, children 5-10. Females lay eggs on perianal skin **at night**: **perianal itching**. **Scotch tape test** | Albendazole or mebendazole; pyrantel pamoate as alternative |
| ***Ascaris*** (giant roundworm) | Swallowed eggs | Most common helminth worldwide. Larvae pass through the **lungs (cough)**. Can block the **ileocecal valve** or bile duct | Bendazoles |

| **Hookworms** (*Ancylostoma*, *Necator*) | Larvae penetrate **bare feet** | Suck blood: **microcytic (iron deficiency) anemia**; can cause pneumonitis | Bendazoles or pyrantel pamoate |
| ***Trichinella*** | Undercooked meat, especially pork (see Correction) | Larvae encyst in **striated muscle**: fever, **periorbital edema, myalgia** | Bendazoles |
| ***Onchocerca*** | **Blackfly** | Skin nodules, hyperpigmentation, **river blindness** | Ivermectin |
| ***Loa loa*** | Deer fly, horse fly, mango fly | **Eye worm** crossing the conjunctiva | Diethylcarbamazine |
| ***Wuchereria*** | Mosquito | Blocked lymphatics: **elephantiasis**, 9 months to a year after the bite | Diethylcarbamazine |
| ***Toxocara*** | Eggs from puppies (dog feces) | **Visceral larva migrans** | Bendazoles |

- **Your mnemonics (p. 225):**
  - **Swallowed** ("you'll get sick if you **EATT** these"): *Enterobius, Ascaris, Toxocara, Trichinella*.
  - **Through the skin** ("from the **SAN**d"): *Strongyloides, Ancylostoma, Necator*.
  - **Bites** ("lay **LOW**"): *Loa loa, Onchocerca, Wuchereria*.
- **Strongyloides hyperinfection** (p. 221): autoinfection multiplies the worm burden, and larvae spread through the body, causing organ failure and **septic shock**. It happens mostly with **corticosteroids** or **HTLV-1**, which impair the Th2 (eosinophil) response.
- **Other source (*Strongyloides* drug):** the CDC lists **ivermectin as first-line therapy** and albendazole as the alternative. It advises considering *Strongyloides* in patients **on or about to start corticosteroids** or other immunosuppressants, and in HTLV-1 infection (CDC, reviewed Feb 2024).
- **Other source (pinworm):** the CDC gives **two doses, two weeks apart**, because the first dose does not kill eggs. It says the drugs' **safety in pregnancy has not been studied**: if treatment is needed, generally wait for the third trimester (CDC, reviewed Feb 2024). Your notes say pyrantel pamoate is preferred in pregnancy (p. 218).
- **Correction (*Trichinella*):** your notes give the route as "fecal-oral; undercooked meat (esp. pork)" (p. 223). The CDC says people get trichinellosis from **eating raw or undercooked meat** that contains the parasite, not from feces. The CDC lists **swelling of the face and eyes** and muscle pain.
- *Trichinella* can also cause **myocarditis** (Osmosis p. 90).
- **Recent change (river blindness):** in **2018** the FDA approved **moxidectin**, the first new river blindness drug in 20 years. In a phase 3 trial it lowered skin microfilariae more than ivermectin (WHO TDR, 14 Jun 2018).

## Tapeworms and flukes (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 226-230)

**Taenia solium (pork tapeworm)** (p. 226)
- **Two diseases from one worm:**
  - **eating larval cysts in undercooked pork** gives an **intestinal tapeworm** (human = definitive host);
  - **swallowing eggs** from a human carrier's feces gives **cysticercosis**. Larvae enter the blood and lodge in the brain, heart and lungs (human = intermediate host).
- **Neurocysticercosis:** think of it in anyone from **Central or South America** with seizures or neurologic symptoms.
- **Your notes' drugs:** praziquantel; albendazole for neurocysticercosis.
- **Other source (CDC, reviewed Mar 2024):**
  - **albendazole alone** for 1-2 live brain cysts, **albendazole + praziquantel** for more than two;
  - **steroids** (such as dexamethasone) for the inflammation as cysts die;
  - **antiseizure drugs** are the mainstay for seizures;
  - antiparasitic drugs **do not help dead (calcified) cysts**;
  - controlling hydrocephalus is one focus of initial therapy (with seizures and brain swelling); a shunt is often needed in subarachnoid disease.

**Diphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm)** (p. 227)
- From **raw freshwater fish**.
- The worm competes for **vitamin B12**, causing **megaloblastic anemia**.
- Praziquantel.

**Echinococcus granulosus** (p. 227)
- From **eggs in dog feces**; sheep are an intermediate host.
- **Hydatid cysts in the liver**. Leaked cyst antigens can cause **anaphylaxis**, so before the cyst is taken out, a scolicidal agent (hypertonic saline or ethanol) is instilled to kill the daughter cysts.
- Albendazole.

**Schistosoma** (pp. 228-229)
- **Exposure:** freshwater snails release larvae (cercariae) that **penetrate intact skin**. The worms mature in the liver.
- **Where adults live:**
  - ***S. mansoni* and *S. japonicum***: mesenteric venules. Eggs go to the bowel and the liver, causing **periportal "pipestem" fibrosis** and **portal hypertension** (presinusoidal).
  - ***S. haematobium***: vesical (bladder) venous plexus. Eggs cause **hematuria** and are passed in the urine. Chronic infection is linked to **squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder** (Egypt, Africa) (p. 229; Osmosis p. 776).
- **Damage** comes from a **Th2 granulomatous** reaction to the eggs, then fibrosis.
- **Treatment:** **praziquantel**.
- **Correction (egg shape and where to look):** your notes say "eggs in stool. *S. mansoni* and *japonicum* have subterminal spine ... *S. haematobium* has a terminal spine" (p. 229). The CDC says:
  - ***S. mansoni***: a **prominent lateral spine**, in stool;
  - ***S. japonicum***: a **smaller, inconspicuous spine**, in stool;
  - ***S. haematobium***: a **conspicuous terminal spine**, **shed in urine**, so **examine urine** when it is suspected (CDC DPDx).

**Clonorchis sinensis (liver fluke)** (p. 230)
- From **undercooked fish**.
- Biliary inflammation, **pigmented gallstones** and **cholangiocarcinoma**.
- Praziquantel.

| Biliary disease, cholangiocarcinoma | *Clonorchis sinensis* |

## Mites and lice (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 231-233, 359)

**Scabies (*Sarcoptes scabiei*)**
- **Spread:** a burrowing mite spread by **direct skin contact**, sometimes by bedding or clothing.
- **Signs:**
  - intensely itchy rash on the **finger webs, sides of the fingers and flexor wrists**, **worse at night**;
  - **linear burrows** are the most specific finding;
  - the itch is a **type IV hypersensitivity** to the mite, its feces and its eggs.
- **Diagnosis:** skin scrapings show mites, eggs or feces.
- **Treatment:**
  - **permethrin** cream from the neck to the soles, left on 8-14 hours. It blocks the mite's sodium channels;
  - **oral ivermectin** (binds invertebrate chloride channels) is an alternative, and is added to permethrin for **crusted scabies**;
  - wash bedding and **treat close contacts**.

**Lice**
- **Body lice** carry ***Rickettsia prowazekii*** (epidemic typhus) and ***Borrelia recurrentis*** (relapsing fever).
- **Pubic lice** (*Phthirus pubis*) spread by skin-to-skin sexual contact, so **condoms do not prevent** spread.
- **Diagnosis:** seeing lice or **nits** on the hair shaft.
- **Treatment:** **permethrin**.
- **Your drug list (p. 359):**
  - permethrin (sodium channels);
  - ivermectin (glutamate-gated chloride channels);
  - malathion (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor);
  - lindane (GABA channels).
- **Other source (lindane):** it is limited to patients who have **not responded to, or cannot tolerate, other treatments**. Seizures and deaths have been reported, with higher risk in infants, children, older adults and patients under 50 kg (AHFS lindane monograph via Drugs.com).

## Clinical vignette
A 34-year-old man with untreated HIV infection has had watery diarrhea for 6 weeks and has lost 7 kg. His CD4 count is 40 cells/mm³. He is not taking any prophylaxis. A modified acid-fast stain of his stool shows small round oocysts.

- **Diagnosis:** **cryptosporidiosis**. Your HIV table lists *Cryptosporidium* as chronic watery diarrhea with **acid-fast oocysts in stool** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 205, 283).
- **Why so severe:** in healthy people it causes mild, self-limited watery diarrhea. In AIDS it causes severe chronic diarrhea (p. 205).
- **Treatment:** your notes give nitazoxanide **in immunocompetent hosts** (p. 205). In advanced AIDS, the key step is restoring immunity with ART (standard teaching).
- **Prevention:** filtering city water supplies (p. 205).
- **The other parasite to prevent at this CD4 count:** *Toxoplasma*. **TMP-SMX** below 100 cells/mm³ covers *Toxoplasma* and *Pneumocystis* (p. 367).

## Common traps
- **Pork tapeworm:** pork meat gives the intestinal worm; **eggs from human feces** give **cysticercosis** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 226).
- **Falciparum sticks to blood vessel walls** (brain, kidneys, lungs). It does not "sequester in the spleen" (MSD; Fonager 2012).
- **Severe malaria in the US:** **IV artesunate**. IV quinidine is gone (CDC 2019, 2026).
- **Primaquine or tafenoquine:** check **G6PD** first (CDC; MSD; El-Husseiny Micro p. 358).
- **Congenital toxoplasmosis:** later infection means **more transmission**; earlier infection means **worse disease** (MSD).
- **Spiramycin** is for the pregnant woman before 18 weeks. **Pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine** treat toxoplasmosis (MSD; El-Husseiny Micro p. 208).
- **Toxic megacolon + infection = *C. difficile*.** Chagas gives a chronic megacolon (MedlinePlus; MSD).
- ***S. haematobium*: look in the urine** for a terminal spine (CDC DPDx).
- ***Trichinella* comes from meat**, not feces (CDC).
- **Trichomoniasis in women:** a **7-day** metronidazole course, not one 2 g dose (MSD).
- **Steroids and *Strongyloides*:** think of it before giving steroids to someone from an endemic area. **Ivermectin** is first-line (CDC).
- **Ring-enhancing brain lesions in AIDS:** multiple usually means *Toxoplasma*; solitary raises **primary CNS lymphoma (EBV)** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 206, 208, 283).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 29-year-old man has had recurrent sinus and lung infections since childhood and once had an anaphylactic reaction to a blood transfusion. For 3 months he has had bloating, flatulence and foul-smelling, greasy stools that began after a backpacking trip where he drank stream water. Stool shows oval cysts on iodine stain. A deficiency of which immune component best explains why his infection has become chronic?
   - **Answer:** **secretory IgA.**
   - **Why:** this is chronic giardiasis. Secretory IgA blocks *Giardia* from sticking to the gut wall, so IgA deficiency predisposes to chronic infection (El-Husseiny Micro p. 202). Anaphylaxis to blood products fits selective IgA deficiency (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **CD4 T cells**. Low CD4 counts explain severe *Cryptosporidium* diarrhea in AIDS (p. 205), not this picture.

2. A 38-year-old man returns from 6 months of volunteer work in rural Mexico with fever and right upper quadrant pain. Ultrasound shows a single 6-cm fluid-filled lesion in the right lobe of the liver. *Entamoeba histolytica* serology is positive. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **metronidazole, followed by a luminal drug such as paromomycin.**
   - **Why:** amebic liver abscess is the most common form of invasive amebiasis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 204). The CDC treats invasive disease with metronidazole or tinidazole, immediately followed by paromomycin or iodoquinol. MSD: metronidazole is "not sufficient to eradicate cysts", so the second drug clears cysts left in the gut.
   - **Trap:** **metronidazole alone**. It treats the tissue infection but does not clear the intestinal cysts (MSD Amebiasis).

3. A 41-year-old woman with AIDS (CD4 count 35 cells/mm³) who stopped all medications a year ago has 2 weeks of headache, fever and right arm weakness. MRI shows multiple ring-enhancing lesions in both cerebral hemispheres. *Toxoplasma* IgG is positive. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **start pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine.**
   - **Why:** multiple ring-enhancing lesions in both hemispheres in AIDS most often mean toxoplasmosis, and pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine is first-line (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 206, 208).
   - **Trap:** **stereotactic brain biopsy**. That is considered when a lesion is solitary or fails to respond to toxoplasmosis treatment, because a solitary lesion raises primary CNS lymphoma (pp. 208, 283; biopsy timing is standard teaching).

4. A 27-year-old woman at 11 weeks' gestation has *Toxoplasma* IgG and IgM antibodies that were negative at 6 weeks. She is well. Fetal ultrasound is normal. Which drug is most appropriate now?
   - **Answer:** **spiramycin.**
   - **Why:** she was infected in the first trimester. MSD: spiramycin is used to prevent mother-to-fetus transmission when infection occurs before 18 weeks.
   - **Trap:** **pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine** (with folinic acid). MSD reserves it for infection at or after 18 weeks, or confirmed fetal infection.

5. A 14-year-old boy develops severe headache, fever, vomiting and confusion 5 days after swimming and diving in a warm freshwater lake in July. CSF shows a high neutrophil count, low glucose and motile organisms on wet mount. By which route did the organism most likely reach his brain?
   - **Answer:** **through the nose, along the olfactory nerves through the cribriform plate.**
   - **Why:** this is primary amebic meningoencephalitis from *Naegleria fowleri*. It enters through the cribriform plate, and motile trophozoites are seen in CSF (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 208-209).
   - **Trap:** **blood spread from swallowed water in the gut**. *Naegleria* reaches the brain directly through the cribriform plate. (The CSF profile is standard teaching.)

6. A 45-year-old woman was treated with chloroquine for *Plasmodium vivax* malaria after a trip to Honduras. Her fever resolved, and repeat smears are negative. Her physician wants to prevent a relapse. Which test should be done before the drug is given?
   - **Answer:** **a quantitative G6PD activity test.**
   - **Why:** relapses come from dormant liver hypnozoites, which need primaquine or tafenoquine (El-Husseiny Micro p. 212; CDC). These drugs cause hemolysis in G6PD deficiency, so the CDC requires quantitative G6PD testing first.
   - **Trap:** **hemoglobin electrophoresis**. Sickle trait matters for falciparum protection (NBME TC p. 218), not for choosing an anti-relapse drug.

7. A 24-year-old man becomes drowsy and then has a seizure 10 days after returning from Nigeria, where he took no malaria prophylaxis. A thin smear shows many ring forms, with parasites in 8% of red cells. Which mechanism best explains his brain injury?
   - **Answer:** **infected red cells adhering to the endothelium of small cerebral vessels, blocking blood flow.**
   - **Why:** *P. falciparum* makes adhesion proteins (PfEMP1) that bind endothelial receptors such as CD36 and ICAM-1. The cells stick in microvessels of the brain, kidneys and lungs (MSD; Fonager 2012; El-Husseiny Micro pp. 211-212).
   - **Trap:** **trapping of infected cells in the spleen**. Sticking to vessel walls is thought to help the parasite *avoid* splenic clearance (Fonager 2012).

8. A 52-year-old woman who returned from Ghana 9 days ago is confused, jaundiced and oliguric. Her smear shows *Plasmodium falciparum* in 12% of red cells. She is admitted to the ICU in a US hospital. Which drug should be started?
   - **Answer:** **intravenous artesunate.**
   - **Why:** severe malaria of any species is treated with IV artesunate (CDC 2026 algorithm; MSD table).
   - **Trap:** **IV quinidine gluconate**, which your notes list (El-Husseiny Micro p. 359). It was discontinued in 2019 (CDC).

9. A 67-year-old man from Massachusetts who had a splenectomy after a car crash has had fever, fatigue and dark urine for a week in July. He hikes often. Hemoglobin is 8.1 g/dL, LDH is high and haptoglobin is low. A blood smear shows intraerythrocytic ring forms, some arranged as tetrads. He has not traveled outside the US. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **atovaquone plus azithromycin.**
   - **Why:** this is babesiosis: an *Ixodes* tick, the northeastern US, hemolysis, asplenia and tetrads (El-Husseiny Micro p. 213; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **chloroquine**. The ring forms can look like malaria, but tetrads and no travel point to *Babesia*.

10. A 49-year-old man who grew up in rural Bolivia has had trouble swallowing both solids and liquids for 2 years. His ECG shows right bundle branch block. A barium swallow shows a dilated esophagus that narrows smoothly at the gastroesophageal junction. Which mechanism best explains his dysphagia?
    - **Answer:** **destruction of ganglion cells in the esophageal myenteric plexus by *Trypanosoma cruzi*.**
    - **Why:** chronic Chagas disease destroys the myenteric plexus, causing secondary achalasia (El-Husseiny Micro p. 215; Osmosis p. 216). Conduction disease fits Chagas heart disease (MSD).
    - **Trap:** **esophageal squamous cell carcinoma**. It can also cause secondary achalasia (Osmosis p. 216), but it wouldn't explain the conduction disease, and a smooth narrowing for 2 years with dysphagia to liquids from the start fits a motility problem (standard teaching).

11. A 58-year-old man who emigrated from Ecuador 20 years ago is given high-dose prednisone for a COPD flare. Two weeks later he has abdominal pain, wheezing, and gram-negative bacteremia. Sputum shows motile larvae. Which measure would most likely have prevented this?
    - **Answer:** **testing and treating for *Strongyloides* (ivermectin) before steroids were started.**
    - **Why:** steroids trigger *Strongyloides* hyperinfection. Larvae spread through the body, and patients can go into septic shock (El-Husseiny Micro p. 221). Larvae carrying gut bacteria into the blood explain the gram-negative bacteremia (standard teaching). The CDC advises considering *Strongyloides* in patients about to start corticosteroids, and lists ivermectin as first-line therapy.
    - **Trap:** **TMP-SMX prophylaxis**, which prevents *Pneumocystis* and *Toxoplasma* (El-Husseiny Micro p. 367), not *Strongyloides*.

12. A 31-year-old man who moved from rural Guatemala 2 years ago has a first generalized seizure. He does not eat pork. CT shows several small cystic brain lesions, some with a bright dot inside. Which exposure most likely caused his condition?
    - **Answer:** **swallowing *Taenia solium* eggs from food contaminated by a human tapeworm carrier.**
    - **Why:** cysticercosis comes from eggs passed in human feces. Here the human is the intermediate host (El-Husseiny Micro p. 226). Neurocysticercosis should be considered in patients from Central or South America with seizures (p. 226).
    - **Trap:** **eating undercooked pork**. That gives the intestinal tapeworm, not brain cysts (p. 226). (The "dot" is the scolex, standard teaching.)

13. A 55-year-old farmer from the Nile delta in Egypt has painless hematuria. Cystoscopy shows a bladder mass, and biopsy shows keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma. Which finding in a urine sample would identify the underlying infection?
    - **Answer:** **eggs with a terminal spine.**
    - **Why:** *Schistosoma haematobium* lives in the bladder venous plexus, its eggs are shed in urine, and chronic infection is linked to squamous cell bladder cancer (El-Husseiny Micro p. 229; Osmosis p. 776; CDC DPDx). Treat with praziquantel (p. 229).
    - **Trap:** **eggs with a prominent lateral spine**, which are *S. mansoni* eggs. Those are found in stool and cause liver fibrosis and portal hypertension (CDC DPDx; El-Husseiny Micro pp. 228-229).

14. A 7-year-old girl in a rural area who usually goes barefoot is pale and tired. Hemoglobin is 7.9 g/dL, MCV is 66 fL and ferritin is low. Eosinophils are mildly raised. Stool shows thin-shelled eggs. How did she most likely acquire the parasite?
    - **Answer:** **larvae in the soil penetrated the skin of her feet.**
    - **Why:** hookworms (*Ancylostoma*, *Necator*) enter through bare feet and suck blood from the intestinal wall, causing microcytic anemia (El-Husseiny Micro p. 223).
    - **Trap:** **swallowing eggs from contaminated soil**, which is how *Ascaris* is acquired (p. 220). Your notes link *Ascaris* to cough and bowel or bile duct blockage, not blood loss.

15. A 26-year-old woman has a frothy, foul-smelling, yellow-green vaginal discharge with vulvar itching. The cervix shows punctate red spots. A saline wet mount shows motile, pear-shaped organisms. She is not pregnant. Which treatment is most appropriate for her?
    - **Answer:** **metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, with treatment of her partner.**
    - **Why:** this is trichomoniasis: strawberry cervix and motile trophozoites (El-Husseiny Micro p. 216). MSD: women get the 7-day course, and partners are treated.
    - **Trap:** **a single 2 g dose of metronidazole**. That is still the regimen for men, but not for women since the CDC's 2021 guidelines (MSD; UNMC review).

16. Six members of a family develop fever, swelling around the eyes and severe muscle pain 2 weeks after eating sausage made from a wild boar they hunted. Their eosinophil counts are high. How did they acquire the infection?
    - **Answer:** **eating larvae encysted in the undercooked meat.**
    - **Why:** *Trichinella* larvae encyst in striated muscle, causing myalgia and periorbital edema (El-Husseiny Micro p. 223). The CDC says it comes from eating raw or undercooked meat that contains the parasite.
    - **Trap:** **fecal-oral spread from an infected family member**. Your notes list "fecal-oral", but the CDC gives meat as the route (see Correction).
