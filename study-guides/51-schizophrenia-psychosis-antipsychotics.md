# Schizophrenia, other psychotic disorders and antipsychotic drugs

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the chapter on schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders (pp. 101-137). Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers. Drug facts come from Pharmacology High Yield (PDF pp. 52, 54) and are checked against the MSD Manual and the FDA. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied; check exact wording in the book. Where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **EPS** = extrapyramidal symptoms; **NMS** = neuroleptic malignant syndrome; **TD** = tardive dyskinesia; **FGA/SGA** = first-/second-generation (typical/atypical) antipsychotic; **D2** = dopamine-2 receptor; **5-HT2A** = serotonin-2A receptor; **ANC** = absolute neutrophil count; **VMAT2** = vesicular monoamine transporter 2; **CK** = creatine kinase; **REMS** = Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (an FDA safety program).

## In one line
Psychotic disorders are sorted mostly by **how long** the illness has lasted: **under 1 month = brief psychotic disorder; 1-6 months = schizophreniform; 6 months or more = schizophrenia**. Then ask **how mood fits in**: psychosis **only during** mood episodes = mood disorder with psychotic features; psychosis for **2 weeks or more without** a mood episode **plus** mood episodes for most of the illness = **schizoaffective disorder**.

## The five symptom domains (pp. 101-103)
- **Delusions:** fixed beliefs that do not change with conflicting evidence (persecutory, referential, somatic, religious, grandiose) (p. 101).
- **Hallucinations:** perceptions without a stimulus; **auditory (voices) are the most common** in schizophrenia. Hallucinations while falling asleep (**hypnagogic**) or waking (**hypnopompic**) are considered normal (p. 102).
- **Disorganized thinking (speech):** derailment, incoherence (p. 102).
- **Grossly disorganized or abnormal motor behavior,** including catatonia (p. 102).
- **Negative symptoms:** mainly **diminished emotional expression** and **avolition** (less self-started purposeful activity); also **alogia** (less speech), **anhedonia** and **asociality** (pp. 102-103).

## The disorders by duration and mood
| Disorder | What you need | Duration | Key details | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Delusional disorder** | **One or more delusions**; never met schizophrenia criterion A (any hallucinations are not prominent and fit the delusion) | **1 month or more** | Apart from the delusion, the person functions fairly well and does not act strangely. Types: **erotomanic** (someone, often of higher status, loves them), grandiose, **jealous**, **persecutory** (most common), **somatic** (for example infestation or foul odor), mixed | pp. 104-105, 107 |
| **Brief psychotic disorder** | **1 or more** of: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior (at least one of the first three) | **At least 1 day, under 1 month**, then **full return** to previous functioning | Specifiers: with marked stressor ("brief reactive psychosis"), without, **with peripartum onset** (p. 109) | pp. 108-109 |
| **Schizophreniform disorder** | Same symptom criteria as schizophrenia (2 of 5) | **1 month to under 6 months** | "Provisional" if still ill. Good prognostic features specifier (2 or more of: psychosis within 4 weeks of first change, confusion or perplexity, good prior functioning, no flat affect). About **1/3 recover**; most of the rest go on to schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (p. 113) | pp. 111, 113 |
| **Schizophrenia** | **2 or more of 5** symptoms for a significant part of 1 month (at least one is delusions, hallucinations or disorganized speech), **plus functional decline** | **6 months** of continuous signs, including at least **1 month of active symptoms**; the rest can be prodromal or residual (negative or mild symptoms) | Mood episodes absent during active symptoms or present only for a minority of the illness; not due to substances; with autism, prominent delusions or hallucinations are also needed | pp. 113-114 |
| **Schizoaffective disorder** | A major mood episode (manic, or depressive **with depressed mood**) at the same time as schizophrenia criterion A | Delusions or hallucinations for **2 weeks or more with no mood episode**; mood episodes present for the **majority** of the illness | Bipolar or depressive type | p. 121 |

**Catatonia (p. 135):** a specifier for many disorders, needing **3 or more of 12** signs: stupor, **catalepsy**, **waxy flexibility**, **mutism**, negativism, **posturing**, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, **echolalia**, **echopraxia**. Treatment is usually a **benzodiazepine (lorazepam)**, with **ECT** if that fails (standard teaching).

## Who gets schizophrenia (DSM-5-TR)
- **Lifetime prevalence 0.3-0.7%** (p. 116).
- **Onset** late teens to mid-30s; **peak early to mid-20s in men, late 20s in women**, with a second midlife peak in women (pp. 117, 119).
- **Men** tend to have more negative symptoms and worse outcomes; women have more mood-laden symptoms and better social functioning (pp. 117, 119).
- **Risk factors (p. 118):** strong genetic contribution, though **most people with schizophrenia have no family history of psychosis**; **late winter/early spring birth** in some places; urban upbringing, migration and social adversity; pregnancy and birth complications with **hypoxia**; **older paternal age**; prenatal infection, stress, malnutrition and maternal diabetes.
- **Suicide (p. 119):** about **5-6% die by suicide** and about **20% attempt** it; risk is highest in young men with substance use, and after a psychotic episode or hospital discharge.
- **Cannabis:** MSD names substance use, "especially cannabis", as a stressor that can trigger psychosis in vulnerable people ([MSD: Schizophrenia, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/schizophrenia-and-related-disorders/schizophrenia)).
- **Prognosis (MSD, same page):** better with good premorbid functioning, late or sudden onset and few negative symptoms; worse with young onset, poor premorbid functioning, family history, many negative symptoms and long untreated psychosis.

**Other psychotic disorders (DSM-5-TR):** delusional disorder lifetime prevalence about **0.2%**, mostly persecutory type (p. 107). Brief psychotic disorder is 2-7% of first-onset psychosis, average onset in the mid-30s, and more than half later relapse (p. 110). Schizoaffective disorder is about **one-third as common** as schizophrenia (p. 123). Lifetime suicide risk in schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder is about 5% (p. 125).

## Antipsychotic drugs
| | **First-generation (typical)** | **Second-generation (atypical)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | Haloperidol, chlorpromazine, thioridazine, fluphenazine | Clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, ziprasidone, aripiprazole |
| Main action | MSD: "act primarily by blocking the dopamine-2 receptor" | MSD: "block dopamine and serotonin receptors, decreasing the likelihood of extrapyramidal (motor) adverse effects" |
| Movement side effects | More EPS | **Less EPS**, with "a much lower risk of tardive dyskinesia" (MSD) |
| Signature side effects (her notes, PDF p. 54; MSD) | **Chlorpromazine:** corneal and lens deposits, obstructive jaundice. **Thioridazine:** irreversible **retinal pigmentation**, QT prolongation | **Clozapine:** **agranulocytosis**, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, weight gain, **drooling at night**; MSD adds **myocarditis** and dose-related **seizures**. **Olanzapine:** metabolic syndrome, diabetes, weight gain. **Quetiapine:** cataract. **Ziprasidone:** QT prolongation. **Risperidone** raises prolactin "as much as FGAs" (MSD). **Aripiprazole:** low prolactin |

**Correction (atypical antipsychotics and D2):** Pharmacology High Yield says atypical antipsychotics have "No action on D2, that's why EPS is not seen" (PDF p. 52). MSD says second-generation antipsychotics "block dopamine and serotonin receptors", which only **decreases** the likelihood of motor side effects ([MSD: Antipsychotic Medications, updated Dec 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/schizophrenia-and-related-disorders/antipsychotic-medications)). Atypicals **do** block D2 (risperidone even raises prolactin as much as typicals), and they **can** cause EPS and TD, just less often. The only antipsychotic with no D2 effect is the new drug in the next note.

**Recent change (new kind of antipsychotic):** on **26 Sep 2024** the FDA approved **Cobenfy (xanomeline and trospium chloride)**, "the first antipsychotic drug approved to treat schizophrenia that targets cholinergic receptors as opposed to dopamine receptors". Common side effects include nausea, indigestion, constipation, vomiting and **hypertension**; it should not be used with **urinary retention**, moderate or severe kidney or liver disease, gastric retention or untreated narrow-angle glaucoma ([FDA press release, 26 Sep 2024](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-drug-new-mechanism-action-treatment-schizophrenia)). MSD describes it as a **muscarinic M1/M4 receptor agonist** and "the first antipsychotic with no effect on dopamine D2 receptors". Trospium is a peripheral anticholinergic that limits xanomeline's side effects outside the brain (standard teaching). Her notes do not include it.

**Clozapine (MSD):** the only second-generation antipsychotic shown to work in about **40%** of patients who fail first-generation drugs; it **reduces suicidality** and negative symptoms and has few motor side effects. Agranulocytosis occurs in **under 1%**.

**Recent change (clozapine monitoring):** the FDA **removed the clozapine REMS** program, effective **13 Jun 2025**. Prescribers and pharmacies no longer have to report ANC results to a registry before dispensing, but the FDA still "recommends that prescribers monitor patients' ANC" as the label describes, because "Severe neutropenia remains a serious, potentially fatal risk", greatest in the first several months ([FDA Drug Safety Communication, 27 Aug 2025](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-removes-risk-evaluation-and-mitigation-strategy-rems-program-antipsychotic-drug-clozapine)).

### Movement and other side effects
| Problem | Usual timing (standard teaching, except NMS from MSD) | What it looks like | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Acute dystonia** | Hours to days | Sustained muscle spasm: twisted neck (torticollis), eyes rolled up (oculogyric crisis), jaw or tongue spasm | MSD: **benztropine** or **diphenhydramine** IV or IM. Her notes: benzhexol (trihexyphenidyl) or benztropine (PDF p. 52) |
| **Akathisia** | Days to weeks | Inner restlessness, cannot sit still | **Propranolol** (MSD; PDF p. 52). Her notes call it the most common EPS (PDF p. 52) |
| **Drug-induced parkinsonism** | Weeks | Slowness, rigidity, tremor, masked face | MSD: oral **benztropine** or **diphenhydramine**; may need to stop the drug or switch to a lower-potency one |
| **Tardive dyskinesia** | Months to years | Involuntary **lip smacking, tongue movements, choreoathetoid** limb movements | **VMAT2 inhibitors: valbenazine** (needs liver monitoring at the start) or **deutetrabenazine** (MSD; PDF p. 52) |
| **NMS** | Usually the **first 2 weeks** (MSD) | **Fever** (often > 40 °C), **"lead-pipe" rigidity**, altered mental status, autonomic instability; **high CK** or rhabdomyolysis | Stop the drug, **rapid cooling**, ICU care; **benzodiazepines** for agitation and mild rigidity; **dantrolene** for worsening rigidity and fever; **bromocriptine** or amantadine to restore dopamine activity. Mortality about **5% at 30 days** ([MSD: NMS, updated Nov 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/injuries-poisoning/heat-illness/neuroleptic-malignant-syndrome)). Her notes: dantrolene (PDF p. 52) |
| **Hyperprolactinemia** | Any time | Galactorrhea, amenorrhea, gynecomastia, sexual dysfunction | Most with risperidone and FGAs (MSD). Dopamine normally inhibits prolactin release; blocking D2 in the **tuberoinfundibular** pathway removes that brake (standard teaching) |

**Other source (drug-induced parkinsonism):** her notes list **promethazine** for drug-induced parkinsonism (PDF p. 52). MSD lists **benztropine** or **diphenhydramine**. Both promethazine and diphenhydramine are antihistamines with anticholinergic action (standard teaching), so the principle is the same: give a **central anticholinergic**. For the exam, choose **benztropine**.

**Long-acting injectables:** MSD says "Long-acting injectable forms are an option for some patients" (Schizophrenia page).

## Clinical vignette
A 22-year-old man is brought in by his parents. Over the past 9 months he dropped out of college, stopped showering and stays in his room. For the past 2 months he has heard voices commenting on his actions and believes his neighbors put cameras in his walls. He speaks little and shows almost no facial expression. Drug screen, thyroid tests and brain MRI are normal, and he has had no mood episodes. **Two or more** symptom domains (hallucinations, delusions, negative symptoms) with **functional decline**, and **6 months or more** of illness including more than 1 month of active symptoms, is **schizophrenia** (pp. 113-114). Onset in the early 20s in a man fits the typical pattern (p. 117). Treatment is an antipsychotic plus psychosocial support (MSD). If two adequate antipsychotic trials fail, use **clozapine** (standard teaching).

## Common traps
- **Duration ladder: 1 day to under 1 month = brief psychotic; 1 to under 6 months = schizophreniform; 6 months or more = schizophrenia.** The 6 months include prodrome and residual phases.
- **Schizoaffective = 2 weeks of psychosis without mood symptoms + mood episodes for most of the illness.** Psychosis **only** during mood episodes = mood disorder with psychotic features.
- **Delusional disorder: 1 month, normal functioning apart from the delusion.**
- **Hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations are normal.**
- **Atypicals still block D2**; they just cause less EPS (see Correction).
- **EPS timeline: dystonia (hours) → akathisia (days to weeks) → parkinsonism (weeks) → tardive dyskinesia (months to years).**
- **Acute dystonia → benztropine or diphenhydramine. Akathisia → propranolol. TD → valbenazine or deutetrabenazine. NMS → stop the drug, cool, dantrolene or bromocriptine.**
- **Anticholinergics treat dystonia and parkinsonism but not TD** (they can worsen TD; standard teaching).
- **NMS vs serotonin syndrome:** NMS = lead-pipe rigidity and slow onset after a dopamine blocker; serotonin syndrome = clonus and hyperreflexia, fast onset after serotonergic drugs (guide 47).
- **Clozapine: treatment-resistant schizophrenia and suicidality. Watch ANC (agranulocytosis), seizures, myocarditis, weight.** The REMS is gone, but ANC monitoring is still recommended.
- **Cobenfy (xanomeline-trospium) = muscarinic agonist, no D2 action.**

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 24-year-old woman has had auditory hallucinations, a belief that her coworkers are poisoning her coffee, and disorganized speech for the past 3 months. Before that she functioned well. Drug screen and thyroid tests are normal, and she has had no mood episodes. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **schizophreniform disorder**.
   - **Why:** schizophrenia-type symptoms (2 or more of 5) lasting at least 1 month but less than 6 months (p. 111).
   - **Trap:** **schizophrenia**, which needs 6 months of continuous signs (p. 114).

2. A 33-year-old man whose wife died in a car crash 4 days ago becomes convinced that the government caused the crash to punish him and hears her voice telling him to hide. He is admitted and treated. Two weeks after onset his symptoms are gone, and he returns to work at his previous level. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **brief psychotic disorder, with marked stressor**.
   - **Why:** delusions and hallucinations lasting from 1 day to under a month, then a complete recovery to previous functioning, in response to a severe stressor (pp. 108-109).
   - **Trap:** **prolonged grief disorder**, which requires at least 12 months since the death and is not a psychotic disorder (pp. 322-323; guide 47).

3. A 26-year-old man with schizophrenia for 4 years answers questions with one or two words, rarely starts a conversation, and his family says he "has nothing to say". His speech is coherent when he does talk. Which negative symptom does this describe?
   - **Answer:** **alogia** (diminished speech output).
   - **Why:** DSM lists alogia as a negative symptom of schizophrenia (p. 103).
   - **Trap:** **disorganized speech** (derailment or incoherence), which is a positive symptom (p. 102); his speech is coherent.

4. A 38-year-old woman has had three manic episodes and two major depressive episodes over 6 years, each with delusions that she is being watched. Mood episodes have been present for most of her illness. Between episodes, while her mood is normal, she has continued to hear voices and believe she is being watched for about a month at a time. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type**.
   - **Why:** psychotic symptoms for 2 weeks or more without a mood episode, and mood episodes for most of the illness (p. 121).
   - **Trap:** **bipolar I disorder with psychotic features**, in which psychosis occurs **only** during mood episodes.

5. A 45-year-old accountant is arrested for repeatedly trying to enter a television studio. For 8 months she has been certain that a famous news anchor is secretly in love with her and sends her coded messages during broadcasts. She works effectively, has no hallucinations and her speech is normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **delusional disorder, erotomanic type**.
   - **Why:** a delusion lasting 1 month or more, functioning not markedly impaired, no other schizophrenia symptoms; the theme is that another person (often of higher status) loves her (pp. 104-105).
   - **Trap:** **schizophrenia**, which needs 2 or more symptom domains and functional decline (p. 113).

6. A 19-year-old man is given IM haloperidol in the emergency department for agitation. Six hours later his neck is twisted to the left, his eyes are fixed upward, and he is frightened. Temperature and CK are normal. Which drug is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **benztropine** (IV or IM).
   - **Why:** this is an acute dystonic reaction; MSD lists IV or IM benztropine or diphenhydramine.
   - **Trap:** **dantrolene**, which is for NMS (fever, rigidity, high CK) (MSD).

7. A 28-year-old woman started haloperidol 10 days ago. She now paces the room, cannot sit still during the interview, and says she feels "jittery inside". She has no rigidity or tremor. Which drug is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **propranolol**.
   - **Why:** this is akathisia; MSD says it can be treated with propranolol (also her notes, PDF p. 52).
   - **Trap:** **increasing the haloperidol dose**, mistaking the restlessness for worsening psychosis (standard teaching).

8. A 35-year-old man was started on haloperidol 6 days ago. He now has a temperature of 40.6 °C, severe "lead-pipe" rigidity, confusion, a heart rate of 128/min, labile blood pressure and a CK of 18,000 U/L. Haloperidol has been stopped and cooling started. Which drug is most appropriate for his persisting rigidity and hyperthermia?
   - **Answer:** **dantrolene**.
   - **Why:** this is NMS; MSD lists dantrolene for worsening or persistent rigidity and hyperthermia (her notes, PDF p. 52).
   - **Trap:** **cyproheptadine**, which treats serotonin syndrome (clonus and hyperreflexia, not lead-pipe rigidity) (guide 47).

9. A 62-year-old woman has taken fluphenazine for schizophrenia for 15 years. Her family notices constant lip smacking, tongue thrusting and writhing finger movements. Which drug is most appropriate for these movements?
   - **Answer:** **valbenazine** (a VMAT2 inhibitor; deutetrabenazine is the other choice).
   - **Why:** this is tardive dyskinesia; MSD and her notes (PDF p. 52) list valbenazine and deutetrabenazine.
   - **Trap:** **benztropine**, which treats dystonia and parkinsonism but does not help, and may worsen, TD (standard teaching).

10. A 30-year-old man with schizophrenia has failed adequate trials of risperidone and olanzapine and has made two suicide attempts in the past year. His psychiatrist starts the antipsychotic shown to reduce suicidality in this setting. Which laboratory value must be monitored most closely during the first months of treatment?
    - **Answer:** **absolute neutrophil count**.
    - **Why:** the drug is **clozapine**, which works in treatment-resistant schizophrenia and reduces suicidality (MSD). Severe neutropenia is greatest in the first several months, and the FDA still recommends ANC monitoring after removing the REMS (FDA, Aug 2025).
    - **Trap:** **serum lithium level**, which is for lithium (guide 47).

11. A 27-year-old woman taking risperidone for schizophrenia has had no periods for 4 months and has milky nipple discharge. Pregnancy test is negative, and MRI of the pituitary is normal. Which mechanism best explains her symptoms?
    - **Answer:** **D2 blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway**, removing dopamine's inhibition of prolactin release.
    - **Why:** risperidone raises prolactin as much as first-generation drugs (MSD); dopamine is the main brake on prolactin (standard teaching).
    - **Trap:** **5-HT2A blockade**, which does not raise prolactin (standard teaching).

12. A 34-year-old man with schizophrenia has had repeated weight gain and movement side effects on dopamine-blocking drugs. His psychiatrist starts a combination drug approved by the FDA in 2024 that acts on cholinergic rather than dopamine receptors. The capsule also contains trospium. On which receptors does the active antipsychotic component mainly act?
    - **Answer:** **muscarinic M1 and M4 receptors (as an agonist).**
    - **Why:** MSD describes xanomeline as a muscarinic M1/M4 agonist with no effect on D2 receptors; the FDA calls Cobenfy the first antipsychotic targeting cholinergic receptors.
    - **Trap:** **D2 receptors**, the target of all earlier antipsychotics (MSD).
