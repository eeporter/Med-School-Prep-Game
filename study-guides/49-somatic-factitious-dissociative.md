# Somatic symptom, factitious and dissociative disorders, and malingering

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the chapters on dissociative disorders (pp. 329-348) and somatic symptom and related disorders (pp. 349-370), plus malingering (p. 835). Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers. Treatment facts come from the MSD Manual. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied; check exact wording in the book.

Abbreviations: **SSD** = somatic symptom disorder; **FND** = functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder); **DID** = dissociative identity disorder; **CBT** = cognitive-behavioral therapy; **SSRI** = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor; **CPS** = child protective services.

## In one line
Ask two questions. **Is the person faking?** If not, it is a somatic symptom disorder (the symptoms are real to the patient). If yes, **why?** No obvious outside reward = **factitious disorder**; an outside reward (money, drugs, time off, avoiding jail) = **malingering**, which is not a mental disorder.

## Is it intentional, and why?
| | **Somatic symptom disorders** (SSD, illness anxiety, FND) | **Factitious disorder** | **Malingering** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Symptoms produced on purpose? | **No.** "The symptoms are not intentionally produced or feigned" (MSD); the suffering is authentic (p. 351) | **Yes**: falsifying signs or symptoms, or causing injury or disease, with deception (p. 367) | **Yes**: intentionally producing false or grossly exaggerated symptoms (p. 835) |
| Motive | Not relevant | **No obvious external reward**; the person presents as ill (p. 367) | **External incentive**: avoiding military duty or work, money, avoiding prosecution, getting drugs (p. 835) |
| A mental disorder? | Yes | Yes | **No**; listed under "other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention" (p. 835) |

**Clues to malingering (p. 835):** a medicolegal setting (for example a lawyer referral or pending lawsuit or charges), a big gap between claimed disability and what is observed, poor cooperation with evaluation or treatment, and antisocial personality disorder.

## Somatic symptom and related disorders (pp. 349-366)
| Disorder | What you need | Key details | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Somatic symptom disorder** | One or more **distressing physical symptoms**, plus **excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviors** about them (at least 1 of: thoughts out of proportion to how serious they are, high health anxiety, too much time and energy spent on them) | Being symptomatic lasts **typically more than 6 months**, even if a single symptom comes and goes. A medical explanation does not rule it out; SSD and a real medical illness often occur together (p. 351). "With predominant pain" specifier | pp. 351-352 |
| **Illness anxiety disorder** | **Preoccupation with having or getting a serious illness**, with **no or only mild physical symptoms** | High health anxiety; either repeated checking (care-seeking type) or avoiding doctors (care-avoidant type). **At least 6 months**, though the feared illness can change | p. 357 |
| **Functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder)** | One or more symptoms of **changed voluntary movement or sensation** (weakness, abnormal movements, functional seizures, numbness, vision loss, speech or swallowing problems) | Exam findings show the symptom is **incompatible with recognized neurological disease**. A stressor is a specifier, not a requirement | pp. 360-361 |
| **Psychological factors affecting other medical conditions** | A real medical condition is made worse by psychological or behavioral factors (for example anxiety worsening asthma, denying chest pain, manipulating insulin) | Severity runs from mild (inconsistent adherence) to extreme (life-threatening, such as ignoring heart attack symptoms) | pp. 364-365 |
| **Factitious disorder imposed on self** | Falsifying symptoms or causing injury or disease **with deception**, presenting oneself as ill, **without obvious external rewards** | Single or recurrent episodes | p. 367 |
| **Factitious disorder imposed on another** | The same deception done **to another person** (a child, an adult or a pet), who is presented as ill | **The perpetrator, not the victim, gets the diagnosis**; the victim may get an abuse diagnosis | pp. 367-368 |

**FND: a positive diagnosis, not just "nothing found".** DSM gives "positive" examination signs (p. 361):
- **Hoover's sign:** weakness of hip extension returns to normal strength when the patient flexes the opposite hip against resistance.
- **Hip abductor sign:** weak thigh abduction becomes normal during abduction of the other hip against resistance.
- **La belle indifférence** (lack of concern about the symptom) has been linked to FND, but DSM says it is not specific and must not be used as a basis for the diagnosis (p. 362). Secondary gain is likewise not diagnostic (p. 362).
- **Who and when:** **2-3 times more common in women** (p. 363). Functional (nonepileptic) attacks start on average at age 20-29, and motor symptoms at 30-39 (p. 362). A history of childhood abuse and neglect may be present; stressful events are common but not universal triggers (p. 362). About **1 in 5 people with functional seizures also have epilepsy** (p. 363). In neurology clinics, about 5-15% have FND (p. 362).

**How factitious disorder is done (p. 368):** exaggerating, making up, simulating or inducing illness, for example adding **blood to urine**, falsifying records, taking **insulin or warfarin** to produce abnormal results, or **injecting fecal material** to cause an abscess or sepsis. About **1% of general hospital inpatients** referred for psychiatric consultation meet criteria (p. 369).

**Names to know:** DSM-5-TR lists FND with "conversion disorder" in parentheses (p. 360). Factitious disorder imposed on another was previously called **factitious disorder by proxy** (p. 367); MSD adds that it was also called **Munchausen syndrome by proxy**, and that factitious disorder imposed on self was previously called **Munchausen syndrome** when dramatic and severe.

**Who gets SSD and illness anxiety:** SSD prevalence in questionnaire studies using DSM-5 criteria is 6.7-17.4% (pp. 352-353); women report more somatic symptoms than men (pp. 353, 354). Illness anxiety is about equally common in men and women (p. 358).

## Dissociative disorders (pp. 329-348)
| Disorder | What you need | Key details | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dissociative identity disorder** | **Two or more distinct personality states** (in some cultures described as possession), with a break in sense of self and agency, plus **recurrent memory gaps** for everyday events, personal information or trauma | Not part of accepted cultural or religious practice; in children not just imaginary playmates; not due to substances (alcohol blackouts) or seizures. Patients may find possessions they don't remember buying, and **fugues are common** (p. 332) | pp. 330, 332 |
| **Dissociative amnesia** | Inability to recall important personal information, **usually traumatic or stressful**, beyond ordinary forgetting | Usually **localized or selective** (a specific event); sometimes **generalized** (identity and life history). Rule out substances, seizures, **transient global amnesia** and head injury. **"With dissociative fugue"** specifier: purposeful travel or bewildered wandering with amnesia for identity | pp. 337-338 |
| **Depersonalization/derealization disorder** | Persistent or recurrent **depersonalization** (feeling detached from or an outside observer of one's own thoughts, body or actions) and/or **derealization** (surroundings feel unreal, dreamlike, foggy) | **Reality testing stays intact**: the person knows it is not real. Not better explained by schizophrenia, panic disorder, depression, PTSD and others | p. 343 |

- **DID and trauma:** about **90%** of people with DID report multiple types of early **childhood abuse and neglect** (p. 333). MSD gives >70% to 100%.
- **Prevalence:** DID 1.5% (12-month, small US study) (p. 333); dissociative amnesia 1.8% (p. 339); depersonalization/derealization disorder 1-2% (1-month, UK), though about **half of all adults** have had at least one brief episode (p. 344). Mean onset of depersonalization/derealization disorder is **16 years** (p. 344).
- **Dissociative amnesia risk:** severe or repeated trauma, especially childhood physical and sexual abuse (p. 339).

## Treatment (MSD)
| Disorder | Management |
| --- | --- |
| **Somatic symptom disorder** | **CBT** is first-line. A supportive **primary care clinician who coordinates care, sees the patient regularly and protects them from unnecessary tests and procedures**. Newer antidepressants are second-line, mainly for coexisting depression ([MSD: Somatic Symptom Disorder, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/somatic-symptom-and-related-disorders/somatic-symptom-disorder)) |
| **Illness anxiety disorder** | A trusting relationship with a caring, reassuring clinician; **SSRIs** "may be helpful, as may cognitive-behavioral therapy" ([MSD: Illness Anxiety Disorder, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/somatic-symptom-and-related-disorders/illness-anxiety-disorder)) |
| **FND** | **Explaining the diagnosis** is "a critical therapeutic intervention"; physical therapy and CBT help some people. The diagnosis should rest on positive findings ([MSD: Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/somatic-symptom-and-related-disorders/functional-neurological-symptom-disorder)) |
| **Factitious disorder imposed on self** | Recognize it early and get psychiatric consultation early, so risky tests, surgery and unneeded drugs are avoided. Use a **nonpunitive, nonconfrontational** approach. No clearly effective treatment exists ([MSD: Factitious Disorder Imposed on Self, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/somatic-symptom-and-related-disorders/factitious-disorder-imposed-on-self)) |
| **Factitious disorder imposed on another** | Perpetrators are usually caregivers such as a parent; when a child has been injured, **contact child protective services** ([MSD: Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/somatic-symptom-and-related-disorders/factitious-disorder-imposed-on-another)) |
| **DID** | **Long-term psychotherapy** (sometimes with drugs for coexisting depression or anxiety); the best outcome is **integration** of the identity states ([MSD: Dissociative Identity Disorder, updated Jun 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/dissociative-disorders/dissociative-identity-disorder)) |

## Clinical vignette
A 34-year-old nurse is admitted for the third time this year with confusion and sweating. Her glucose is 38 mg/dL. Serum insulin is high, but **C-peptide is low**. She denies having diabetes or access to insulin, but a syringe is found in her bag. She has no lawsuit or disability claim pending. Insulin made by the pancreas is released with an equal amount of C-peptide, so **high insulin with low C-peptide means injected insulin** (standard teaching). Secretly producing illness with **no external reward** is **factitious disorder imposed on self** (p. 367); DSM lists taking insulin to induce illness as one method (p. 368). Management is early psychiatric consultation and a nonpunitive approach (MSD).

## Common traps
- **SSD = real symptoms + excessive worry about them. Illness anxiety = few or no symptoms + fear of having a disease.**
- **SSD is not "medically unexplained symptoms".** It can coexist with a real disease (p. 351); the key is the excessive response.
- **FND is diagnosed by positive signs (Hoover's sign), not only by normal tests.** La belle indifférence is not diagnostic.
- **Factitious = no external reward; malingering = external reward** (money, drugs, time off, avoiding jail). Malingering is not a mental disorder.
- **Factitious disorder imposed on another: the caregiver gets the diagnosis**; call child protective services.
- **High insulin + low C-peptide = injected insulin** (standard teaching).
- **DID = separate identity states + memory gaps**, strongly linked to childhood abuse. **Dissociative amnesia** has memory loss without identity states; **fugue** is a specifier of dissociative amnesia.
- **Depersonalization/derealization: reality testing is intact.** If the person believes the unreality, think psychosis.

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 42-year-old woman has had abdominal pain, back pain and fatigue for 2 years. Extensive testing, including endoscopy, CT and blood tests, is normal. She spends several hours a day reading about her symptoms, is convinced they mean something serious, and has seen eight doctors. The pain is real and limits her work. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **somatic symptom disorder**.
   - **Why:** distressing physical symptoms plus excessive thoughts, anxiety and time spent on them, lasting more than 6 months (p. 351).
   - **Trap:** **illness anxiety disorder**, which requires that physical symptoms be absent or only mild (p. 357).

2. A 36-year-old man has no physical complaints. After a coworker died of colon cancer 9 months ago, he became convinced he also has cancer. He checks his stool daily, has had two normal colonoscopies, and is not reassured. Last month he worried instead about a brain tumor. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **illness anxiety disorder**.
   - **Why:** preoccupation with having a serious illness, no or mild symptoms, high health anxiety and excessive checking for 6 months or more; the feared illness can change (p. 357).
   - **Trap:** **somatic symptom disorder**, which needs distressing physical symptoms (p. 351).

3. A 24-year-old woman cannot move her right leg after a heated argument with her partner. Reflexes and tone are normal, and MRI of the brain and spine is normal. When asked to raise her left leg against resistance while lying on her back, the examiner feels strong downward pressure from her right heel. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder)**.
   - **Why:** this is a positive **Hoover's sign**: hip extension in the "weak" leg returns to normal when the opposite hip is flexed against resistance, which is incompatible with neurological disease (pp. 360-361).
   - **Trap:** **malingering**, which requires intentional production of symptoms for an external reward (p. 835); she has no external incentive and nothing suggests she is producing the weakness on purpose.

4. A 27-year-old respiratory therapist has had five abscesses on the front of her thighs in a year. Each one grows a mix of bowel organisms (*E. coli*, *Bacteroides* and *Enterococcus*). She has normal immune function tests, does not inject drugs, and is not seeking disability or compensation. The abscesses stop appearing while one leg is in a cast, then reappear on the other leg. She is distressed and asks for more tests. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **factitious disorder imposed on self**.
   - **Why:** secretly inducing illness with deception and no obvious external reward (p. 367); injecting fecal material to cause an abscess is a DSM example (p. 368). Mixed bowel flora in skin abscesses at reachable sites that stop when the site is covered point to self-injection (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **malingering**, which needs an external incentive such as money or time off (p. 835); she has none.

5. A 45-year-old man injured at work requests a disability evaluation. His lawyer referred him. He reports severe, constant back pain and says he cannot walk more than a few steps, but he is later seen walking briskly across the parking lot. He refuses physical therapy. Which is the most likely explanation for his presentation?
   - **Answer:** **malingering**.
   - **Why:** intentionally exaggerated symptoms for an external incentive (financial compensation), with a medicolegal referral, a gap between claimed and observed disability, and poor cooperation (p. 835).
   - **Trap:** **factitious disorder**, in which there is no obvious external reward (p. 367).

6. A 3-year-old boy has had 11 hospital admissions for recurrent vomiting and blood in his urine. All tests have been normal. His mother, who has medical training, stays at the bedside and is very attentive. The bleeding stops whenever she is away, and a nurse sees her adding something to the urine collection bag. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **report to child protective services.**
   - **Why:** this is factitious disorder imposed on another; the mother (the perpetrator) gets the diagnosis (p. 367). MSD: when a child has been injured, contact child protective services.
   - **Trap:** **more diagnostic tests for the child**, which continue the harm.

7. A 29-year-old woman says she often "loses time": she finds clothes in her closet she does not remember buying, and coworkers say she sometimes introduces herself by another name and acts like a child. She was severely abused as a child. She does not drink or use drugs, and an EEG is normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **dissociative identity disorder**.
   - **Why:** two or more distinct personality states noticed by others, plus recurrent memory gaps for everyday events (p. 330), with the typical history of childhood abuse (p. 333).
   - **Trap:** **dissociative amnesia**, which has memory loss but not separate identity states (p. 337).

8. A 47-year-old man is found in a bus station 300 miles from home. He cannot say who he is or where he lives. He seems calm and otherwise alert, neurological examination and CT are normal, and toxicology is negative. His family later reports that his business went bankrupt the week before. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **dissociative amnesia with dissociative fugue**.
   - **Why:** amnesia for identity, after a major stressor, with purposeful travel, and no substance or neurological cause (pp. 337-338).
   - **Trap:** **transient global amnesia**, which DSM lists as a medical cause to rule out (p. 337); in it, patients keep their sense of identity and repeatedly ask the same questions (standard teaching).

9. A 19-year-old college student has had episodes for a year in which he feels as if he is watching himself in a movie and that his hands do not belong to him. He says, "I know it's just a feeling; I'm really here." He has no hallucinations and does not use drugs. Which feature best distinguishes his condition from a psychotic disorder?
   - **Answer:** **intact reality testing** during the episodes.
   - **Why:** in depersonalization/derealization disorder, the person knows the experiences are not real (p. 343).
   - **Trap:** **the presence of detachment from the body**, which is the symptom itself and does not separate it from psychosis.

10. A 38-year-old woman with somatic symptom disorder has had many emergency department visits and normal tests for chest and abdominal pain. Her primary care physician wants to reduce unnecessary testing. Which approach is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **regularly scheduled visits with one primary care clinician, plus CBT.**
    - **Why:** MSD: CBT is first-line, and a primary care clinician who coordinates care, sees the patient regularly and protects her from unnecessary tests and procedures helps.
    - **Trap:** **telling her the symptoms are imaginary**; her suffering is authentic (p. 351).

11. A 31-year-old man has episodes in which he falls, shakes all four limbs from side to side with his eyes tightly closed, and is unresponsive for 5 minutes. Video EEG during three typical events shows no epileptic activity, and he has no history of epilepsy. He is frightened that he has a brain disease. Which is the most appropriate next step in management?
    - **Answer:** **explain the diagnosis of functional (nonepileptic) seizures to him clearly**, based on the positive findings, and refer him for psychotherapy such as CBT.
    - **Why:** this is FND with functional seizures (p. 360). MSD calls the diagnostic explanation "a critical therapeutic intervention"; CBT and physical therapy help some people.
    - **Trap:** **starting an antiseizure drug**, which treats epilepsy, not functional seizures. (Remember that about 1 in 5 people with functional seizures also have epilepsy, p. 363, so each new type of event still needs evaluation.)
