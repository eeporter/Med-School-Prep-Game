# Gastric mucosal protection, ulcer drugs and fecal occult blood testing

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27

Based on your notes: Osmosis High-Yield Gastrointestinal Pathology (GERD drugs p. 30, gastritis and H. pylori pp. 38-41, peptic ulcer pp. 42-43, colorectal cancer pp. 49-50) and Pharmacology High Yield (ulcer drugs PDF pp. 160-161, prostaglandin analogs PDF p. 165). Each fact is also checked against StatPearls, the MSD Manual and the 2024 ACG guideline; where a different source disagrees with your notes, newer guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
The stomach survives its own acid because prostaglandins keep up mucus, bicarbonate and blood flow; NSAIDs remove the prostaglandins and *H. pylori* undermines the bicarbonate, and every ulcer drug either lowers acid or restores protection.

## Core mechanism
1. Peptic ulcer disease is an imbalance between protective and destructive factors. [StatPearls: Peptic Ulcer Disease](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534792/)
2. Prostaglandins maintain mucus, bicarbonate and mucosal blood flow. NSAIDs block COX-1, so prostaglandins fall and all three defenses weaken (standard teaching). [StatPearls: PUD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534792/)
3. *H. pylori* makes urease, which turns urea into ammonia to shield itself from acid, and it raises gastric acid while lowering duodenal bicarbonate secretion (Osmosis GI, p. 42). [StatPearls: PUD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534792/), [MSD Manual: H. pylori Infection](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/helicobacter-pylori-infection)
4. Where it lives changes acid output. **Antrum-predominant** infection lowers somatostatin, so gastrin and acid rise: duodenal ulcers. **Body-predominant** infection causes atrophy and **low** acid: gastric ulcers and gastric adenocarcinoma. [MSD Manual: H. pylori Infection](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/helicobacter-pylori-infection)

## What your notes add (Osmosis GI, pp. 42-43)
- Peptic ulcer is the **most common cause of upper GI bleeding**; ulcers sit mostly in the proximal duodenum and gastric antrum.
- Risk factors: *H. pylori* (most common), NSAIDs (especially low-dose aspirin), corticosteroids, smoking, COPD, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (high gastrin), and physiologic stress: **Cushing ulcer** with raised intracranial pressure and **Curling ulcer** with severe burns.
- Up to 70% have no symptoms. Pain is epigastric burning, a few hours after meals and worse at night, relieved by food or antacids; it can mimic a heart attack.
- Complications: bleeding (hematemesis, melena, positive guaiac with a slow bleed), perforation (acute abdomen) and gastric outlet obstruction. Treatment: stop NSAIDs, stop smoking, PPI.
- *H. pylori* can be found by serology, stool antigen, urea breath test or biopsy (Osmosis GI, p. 39). Antral infection brings neutrophils, peptic ulcers and **MALT lymphoma**; autoimmune gastritis hits the body and fundus with lymphocytes, low acid, high gastrin and pernicious anemia (Osmosis GI, p. 41).

## Ulcer drugs
| Drug | Mechanism | Key point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole) | Inhibit the parietal cell H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (proton pump) | Most potent acid blockers; part of *H. pylori* therapy |
| H2 blockers (cimetidine, famotidine) | Block histamine at parietal cell H2 receptors | Cimetidine inhibits cytochrome P450 (raises warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline levels) and has antiandrogen effects (gynecomastia). Your notes: famotidine is the most potent H2 blocker; given at bedtime for nocturnal acid; renally cleared (Pharmacology High Yield, PDF p. 160). **Recent change:** your notes also list ranitidine, but in April 2020 the FDA asked makers to remove all prescription and over-the-counter ranitidine from the US market because of contamination with NDMA, a probable carcinogen. [Pharmacy Times: FDA requests removal of all ranitidine products, 1 Apr 2020](https://pharmacytimes.com/view/fda-requests-removal-of-all-ranitidine-products-from-us-market) |
| Sucralfate | Breaks down in acid and forms a physical barrier over the ulcer base; does not lower acid | Needs acid to work ("acts only in acid medium", Pharmacology High Yield, PDF p. 161; adheres to the mucosa, Osmosis GI, p. 30); binds other drugs and reduces their absorption |
| Antacids | Neutralize acid | Magnesium: diarrhea; aluminum: constipation; reduce absorption of tetracycline, digoxin and iron |
| Misoprostol | Prostaglandin E1 analog: less acid, more mucus and bicarbonate | Used to **prevent** NSAID-induced ulcers in high-risk patients; cramping and diarrhea (about 30%); contraindicated in pregnancy (abortifacient). Your notes list its other use: first-trimester termination of pregnancy (Pharmacology High Yield, PDF p. 165) |
| Bismuth | Coats the ulcer and is toxic to *H. pylori* (standard teaching) | Part of first-line quadruple therapy; turns stools and tongue black; avoid in renal failure (Pharmacology High Yield, PDF p. 161) |
| *H. pylori* first line (ACG 2024) | **Bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days**: PPI + bismuth subsalicylate + metronidazole + tetracycline | MSD makes bismuth quadruple therapy the first choice where clarithromycin resistance is above 15% or unknown. Clarithromycin triple therapy (PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin) is used only where resistance is low; ACG 2024 says "bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT) for 14 days is the preferred regimen when antibiotic susceptibility is unknown"; for patients already treated once, "Salvage regimens containing clarithromycin or levofloxacin should only be used if antibiotic susceptibility is confirmed" [Chey et al., ACG Clinical Guideline: Treatment of H. pylori Infection, Am J Gastroenterol 2024;119:1730-1753](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39626064/); the no-prior-macrolide rule is standard teaching here. Confirm cure with a urea breath or stool antigen test at least 4 weeks after antibiotics and 1 week off the PPI. **Recent change:** your notes give triple therapy (PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin) for 2 weeks and quadruple therapy for 1 week (Osmosis GI, p. 40); ACG 2024 makes 14-day bismuth quadruple therapy the preferred first choice. |

[StatPearls: PUD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534792/), [StatPearls: Misoprostol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539873/), [MSD Manual: Medications for Gastric Acidity](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/medications-for-the-treatment-of-gastric-acidity), [MSD Manual: H. pylori Infection](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/helicobacter-pylori-infection)

## Gastric vs duodenal ulcer
| Feature | Gastric ulcer | Duodenal ulcer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain and food | No consistent pattern; eating sometimes worsens it | Relieved by eating (returns 2-3 hours after) |
| Weight | Loss | Gain |

[StatPearls: PUD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534792/), [MSD Manual: Peptic Ulcer Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/peptic-ulcer-disease). Weight loss vs gain is standard teaching.

## Fecal occult blood tests
| Feature | Guaiac test (gFOBT) | Fecal immunochemical test (FIT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Detects | Peroxidase-like activity of heme (turns guaiac blue) | Human globin, with antibodies |
| Bleeding site picked up | Anywhere, including upper GI | Mainly lower GI (globin is digested higher up) |
| Diet rules | Red meat and raw peroxidase-rich vegetables (turnips, broccoli) can cause false positives; high-dose vitamin C can cause false negatives (StatPearls gives 1-2 g/day; some sources warn above 250 mg/day); NSAIDs/aspirin can cause real GI bleeding | None |
| Samples | 3 separate bowel movements, yearly | One sample, yearly; no diet or medication restrictions |

[StatPearls: Fecal Occult Blood Test](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537138/), [USPSTF: Colorectal Cancer Screening, 2021](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening)

- Your notes list a positive stool guaiac test and CEA among colorectal cancer lab findings, and lack of screening colonoscopy as a risk factor (Osmosis GI, pp. 49-50).
- **Recent change:** USPSTF (2021) now screens average-risk adults aged 45-75 (changed from 50); your notes do not give an age. [USPSTF: Colorectal Cancer Screening, 2021](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening)
- A positive test needs further evaluation, such as colonoscopy. [StatPearls: FOBT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537138/)
- FOBT is a screening test for average-risk people; it is **not** for high-risk or symptomatic patients, who need prompt GI referral. [StatPearls: FOBT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537138/)

### Worked example: what a positive FIT means
Use made-up but realistic numbers to practice the formulas (illustrative, not from a study). Screen 10,000 average-risk adults; 50 have colorectal cancer (prevalence 0.5%). Say FIT sensitivity is 80% and specificity is 95%.
- True positives = 0.80 × 50 = 40. False negatives = 10.
- True negatives = 0.95 × 9,950 = 9,452.5, about 9,453. False positives = 9,950 − 9,453 = 497.
- Positive predictive value (PPV) = 40 / (40 + 497) = 0.074, about 7%. Negative predictive value = 9,453 / (9,453 + 10) = 99.9%.
- **Lesson:** when the disease is rare, most positive screens are false positives, which is why every positive FIT goes to colonoscopy. PPV rises with prevalence; sensitivity and specificity do not change (standard teaching).
- **Lead-time bias:** screening finds cancer earlier, so survival measured from diagnosis looks longer even if death comes at the same time. **Length-time bias:** screening picks up slow-growing tumors more often, which have better outcomes anyway (standard teaching). See [11-biostats-risk-and-survival.md](11-biostats-risk-and-survival.md).

## Clinical vignette
A 62-year-old with knee osteoarthritis takes ibuprofen daily and now has epigastric pain that is worse after meals. Which drug both protects the mucosa and specifically replaces what NSAIDs remove?

**Answer:** misoprostol.
**Why:** NSAIDs block COX-1, lowering prostaglandins; misoprostol is a prostaglandin E1 analog that restores mucus and bicarbonate [StatPearls: Misoprostol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539873/); MSD adds that it inhibits acid secretion, enhances mucosal defense and is given with the NSAID to high-risk patients [MSD Manual: Medications for Gastric Acidity](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/medications-for-the-treatment-of-gastric-acidity).
**Trap:** misoprostol is contraindicated in pregnancy because it causes uterine contractions.

## Common traps
- Guaiac detects heme activity, so red meat can make it falsely positive; FIT detects human globin and needs no diet change.
- Vitamin C makes guaiac falsely **negative**.
- A positive FOBT is not a diagnosis; it leads to colonoscopy.
- Duodenal ulcer pain improves with food; gastric ulcer pain has no consistent pattern, and eating sometimes worsens it.

## Practice questions
1. A patient's guaiac test is positive after a weekend of steak dinners; colonoscopy is normal. Why?
   - **Answer:** dietary false positive from heme in red meat.
   - **Why:** guaiac reacts to the peroxidase activity of any heme, including from meat. [StatPearls: FOBT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537138/)
   - **Trap:** FIT would not react, since it detects human globin.
2. Which stool test is more specific for colonic (lower GI) bleeding?
   - **Answer:** the fecal immunochemical test (FIT).
   - **Why:** it detects human globin, which is mostly digested if the bleeding is higher up. [StatPearls: FOBT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537138/)
   - **Trap:** guaiac picks up upper GI blood too.
3. How does *H. pylori* survive gastric acid?
   - **Answer:** urease converts urea to ammonia, which buffers acid around the organism.
   - **Why:** this is the basis of the urea breath test too. [StatPearls: PUD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534792/), [MSD Manual: H. pylori Infection](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/helicobacter-pylori-infection)
   - **Trap:** assuming *H. pylori* always raises acid. Antral infection raises gastrin and acid (duodenal ulcer), but body infection causes atrophy and low acid (gastric ulcer, cancer). [MSD Manual: H. pylori Infection](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/gastritis-and-peptic-ulcer-disease/helicobacter-pylori-infection)
4. A 55-year-old man has 3 months of fatigue and looser stools. Hb is 9.8 g/dL with a low ferritin. He asks for a home stool blood test. What is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** colonoscopy (diagnostic evaluation), not a stool test.
   - **Why:** FOBT is not indicated for symptomatic or high-risk patients. [StatPearls: FOBT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537138/)
   - **Trap:** a negative FOBT would falsely reassure.
