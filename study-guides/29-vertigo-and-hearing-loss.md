# Vertigo and hearing loss

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (hearing pp. 61-67, vestibular system and vertigo pp. 67-72). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where a different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
For vertigo, ask **how long** each spell lasts and whether **hearing** is involved: seconds with head movement and normal hearing = benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV); 20 minutes to 12 hours with low-pitch hearing loss, ringing and ear fullness = Meniere disease; days of constant vertigo with normal hearing = vestibular neuronitis. Any brain signs, a normal head impulse test, or nystagmus that changes direction = think **stroke** (central). For hearing loss, the tuning forks sort it: in **conductive** loss the Weber sound goes to the **bad** ear and bone beats air on Rinne; in **sensorineural** loss the Weber sound goes to the **good** ear and air still beats bone.

## Core mechanism
1. **Vertigo** is "a sensation of movement of the self or the environment when there is no actual movement". **Peripheral** vertigo comes from the inner ear or vestibular nerve; **central** vertigo usually from "dysfunction within the brain stem or cerebellum". [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo)
2. **Ear symptoms point peripheral.** "Ear symptoms (eg, tinnitus, fullness, hearing loss) usually indicate a peripheral disorder." In central causes, "nystagmus is not inhibited by visual fixation", and the HINTS bedside exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) points to a central cause when there is "a normal horizontal head impulse test, direction changing nystagmus in eccentric gaze, or vertical skew deviation". [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo) Why a **normal** head impulse test is the worrying result: with one-sided inner ear damage, the eyes slip off the target with a fast head turn "and then quickly and voluntarily return back to the target (called delayed catch-up saccades)", which is an abnormal test. [MSD Manual: Meniere Disease](https://msdmanuals.com/en-pt/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/meniere-disease) So a normal test in a patient with constant vertigo means the vestibular system works and the problem is in the brain (standard teaching).
3. **Hearing loss has two kinds.** Conductive loss comes from "lesions in the external auditory canal, tympanic membrane (TM), or middle ear"; sensorineural loss from "lesions of either the inner ear (sensory) or the auditory (8th) cranial nerve (neural)". [MSD Manual: Hearing Loss](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/hearing-loss/hearing-loss)
4. **Tuning forks.** Weber (fork on the forehead): "In unilateral conductive hearing loss, the tone is louder in the ear with hearing loss"; "in unilateral sensorineural hearing loss, the tone is louder in the normal ear". Rinne (fork on the mastoid, then by the ear): "With conductive hearing loss over 25 dB, the relationship is reversed; bone conduction is louder than air conduction." [MSD Manual: Hearing Loss](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/hearing-loss/hearing-loss) Why Weber goes to the bad ear in conductive loss (standard teaching): the blocked ear hears less room noise, so the bone-carried tone seems louder there.

## Compare: causes of vertigo

| Cause | How long | Hearing | Key clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BPPV** | "A few seconds to minutes" | Normal | Set off by moving the head (rolling over in bed, bending down); positive Dix-Hallpike |
| **Meniere disease** | "20 minutes to 12 hours" | Fluctuating **low-frequency** sensorineural loss | Tinnitus and ear fullness on the same side; endolymphatic hydrops |
| **Vestibular neuronitis** | Up to about 1 week, slowly easing | Normal | "Sudden, incapacitating, severe vertigo with no hearing loss or other findings" |
| **Labyrinthitis** | Several days (El-Husseiny p. 72) | Hearing loss and tinnitus | Like neuronitis but with hearing loss; often follows an upper respiratory infection (p. 72) |
| **Vestibular migraine** | Episodes | Sometimes ear symptoms | Migraine history; probably the most common cause of dizziness overall |
| **Cerebellar or brainstem stroke** | Sudden, continuous | Hearing loss if the cochlear artery is involved | Ataxia, headache, other neurologic signs; image immediately |
| **Vestibular schwannoma** | Slowly progressive | One-sided hearing loss, tinnitus | Imbalance; see guide 28 |

Sources for the table: [MSD Manual: Selected Causes of Dizziness and Vertigo table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-causes-of-dizziness-and-vertigo); [MSD Manual: BPPV](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo); [MSD Manual: Meniere Disease](https://msdmanuals.com/en-pt/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/meniere-disease); [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo)

- **BPPV:** "displacement of otoconial crystals" into the semicircular canals, "most commonly in the posterior semicircular canal". On Dix-Hallpike, vertigo and nystagmus "can take about 5 to 10 seconds" to start, last 10 to 30 seconds and then fade, and the "nystagmus is torsional and occurs when the head is turned to the affected ear". Treatment is canalith repositioning, "most commonly the Epley maneuver". [MSD Manual: BPPV](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo) The MSD vertigo table describes the nystagmus as having "a latency of 0 to 30 seconds", being "fatigable", and "torsional, beating toward the undermost ear". [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo) For posterior canal BPPV it is also upbeating (standard teaching). It is "the most common cause of relapsing otogenic vertigo". [MSD Manual: BPPV](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo)
- **Your notes on peripheral vertigo** (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 71-72): **BPPV** comes from crystal deposits (canaliths) in a semicircular canal; Dix-Hallpike (lying back quickly with the head turned 45 degrees) brings on vertigo and nystagmus; it usually resolves alone but can recur, and the **Epley** maneuver relieves it. **Meniere disease** is too much endolymph because it is **not absorbed** properly; the swelling damages both the balance and hearing parts, giving the triad of **tinnitus** (often with fullness), **vertigo** and **sensorineural hearing loss**. **Labyrinthitis** is a viral or post-viral inflammation with sudden severe vertigo for several days plus hearing loss and tinnitus. **Other source (BPPV):** your notes call BPPV the most common cause of vertigo; MSD calls it the most common cause of relapsing vertigo from the ear and names vestibular migraine as probably the most common cause of dizziness overall. **Other source (labyrinthitis):** your notes describe it as inflammation of the vestibular nerve; the hearing loss shows the cochlea is involved too, which is what separates it from vestibular neuronitis (standard teaching).
- **Central vertigo in your notes:** from any brainstem or cerebellar tumor, bleed or ischemia; get an urgent non-contrast head CT when there are stroke risk factors, a new headache or neurologic signs (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 71).
- **Caloric test (your notes, pp. 68-70):** **cool** water in one ear acts like a lesion on that side, so the eyes drift slowly **toward** the cool ear and the fast phase beats **away** from it; warm water does the opposite (mnemonic COWS: cold opposite, warm same, standard teaching). No fast phase means a cortical problem.
- **Meniere disease:** endolymphatic hydrops "is a pathologic process observed in all patients with Meniere disease". The diagnosis rests on "fluctuating low-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, episodic vertigo", with fullness and tinnitus in the same ear. "Diuretics and a low-salt diet" are first-line. [MSD Manual: Meniere Disease](https://msdmanuals.com/en-pt/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/meniere-disease)
- **Red flags for a central cause:** head or neck pain, ataxia, loss of consciousness, a focal neurologic deficit, and "severe, continuous symptoms for > 1 hour". "Loss of consciousness is not associated with dizziness due to peripheral vestibular pathology." [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo)
- **Inner ear blood supply:** the internal auditory artery, the inner ear's only supply, usually comes from the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA), so an AICA stroke can cause sudden hearing loss with vertigo (guide 27). [Lee H et al. Arch Neurol 2001;58:1287-1289](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/780050)

## Compare: hearing loss

| | Conductive | Sensorineural |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Ear canal, eardrum, middle ear | Cochlea or cranial nerve VIII |
| Weber | Louder in the **bad** ear | Louder in the **good** ear |
| Rinne (bad ear) | Bone louder than air (loss over 25 dB) | Air louder than bone (normal pattern) |
| Common causes | Earwax ("the most common cause of treatable conductive hearing loss"), otitis media, otosclerosis | Aging (high pitches first), noise (4 kHz first), ototoxic drugs, Meniere, vestibular schwannoma |

Source for the table: [MSD Manual: Hearing Loss](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/hearing-loss/hearing-loss)

- **Age-related (presbycusis):** "Higher frequencies are more affected than lower frequencies." **Noise:** loss "typically occurs first at 4 kHz". **Ototoxic drugs:** aspirin, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, cisplatin, furosemide, ethacrynic acid and quinine; many "also have vestibular toxicity". [MSD Manual: Hearing Loss](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/hearing-loss/hearing-loss) Why high pitches go first: high tones are sensed at the **base** of the cochlea near the oval window, and low tones at the apex near the helicotrema; presbycusis loses hair cells at the base first (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 62, 66). Your notes add that loud noise breaks the hair-cell stereocilia against the tectorial membrane, and high-frequency hearing goes first whatever the noise (think of a rock musician); a sudden very loud blast can rupture the eardrum (pp. 62-63).
- **Otosclerosis:** "abnormal accumulation of new bone, most commonly within the oval window", which stops the stapes from moving and causes conductive loss. "Half of all cases are inherited", and "most cases do not become evident until the late adolescent or early adult years". Treatment is a hearing aid or stapedectomy. [MSD Manual: Otosclerosis](https://msdmanuals.com/en-au/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/middle-ear-and-tympanic-membrane-disorders/otosclerosis) That it can get worse in pregnancy is standard teaching (not on the MSD page).
- **Cholesteatoma:** a build-up of shed keratin in the middle ear that can erode the ossicles and mastoid, causing **conductive** loss, often with **painless ear discharge** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 66).
- **Hyperacusis:** sounds seem too loud when the **facial nerve** is damaged, because the **stapedius** no longer steadies the stapes (p. 66).
- **Where in the brain? (your notes, pp. 64-65):** a lesion of cranial nerve VIII or the cochlear nucleus, **before** the fibers cross in the trapezoid body, causes **profound one-sided** deafness on that side. A lesion **after** the crossing (superior olive, lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculus, medial geniculate body, auditory cortex) causes only **slight loss in both ears** plus trouble **locating** sounds. The superior olive is the first place both ears' input meets.

## Clinical vignette
A 68-year-old woman gets 20 seconds of violent spinning every morning when she rolls onto her right side in bed. Her hearing is normal. When her head is turned right and lowered back over the table edge, torsional nystagmus starts after a few seconds and fades within half a minute. What is the treatment?

**Answer:** BPPV of the right posterior canal; treat with the Epley canalith repositioning maneuver.
**Why:** brief vertigo set off by head position, normal hearing, and delayed, fading torsional nystagmus toward the affected ear on Dix-Hallpike are BPPV, from loose otoconia in the posterior canal. [MSD Manual: BPPV](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo)
**Trap:** Meniere disease, which lasts 20 minutes to hours and brings hearing loss and ear fullness.

## Common traps
- Constant vertigo for a day with a **normal** head impulse test = stroke until proven otherwise, not neuronitis. [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo)
- Neuronitis has **no** hearing loss; labyrinthitis **has** hearing loss. [MSD Manual: Selected Causes of Dizziness and Vertigo table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-causes-of-dizziness-and-vertigo)
- Meniere hearing loss is **low**-frequency; age-related loss is **high**-frequency; noise hits **4 kHz** first.
- Weber goes **toward** a conductive loss but **away from** a sensorineural loss. Always pair it with Rinne. Your notes: a **negative** Rinne (bone louder than air) means conductive loss (El-Husseiny p. 65).
- Deaf in one ear from a brain lesion = cochlear nerve or nucleus; a cortical lesion never causes one-sided deafness (pp. 64-65).
- Painless ear discharge with conductive loss = cholesteatoma (p. 66).
- An aminoglycoside plus a loop diuretic (furosemide) stacks two ototoxic drugs. [MSD Manual: Hearing Loss](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/hearing-loss/hearing-loss)

## Practice questions
1. A 30-year-old has hearing loss in the left ear. On Weber, the tone is louder in the left ear. On Rinne in the left ear, bone conduction is louder than air conduction. What type of hearing loss is it, and what is one cause?
   - **Answer:** left conductive hearing loss, for example earwax, otitis media or otosclerosis.
   - **Why:** in conductive loss "the tone is louder in the ear with hearing loss" on Weber, and bone beats air on Rinne. [MSD Manual: Hearing Loss](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/hearing-loss/hearing-loss)
   - **Trap:** right sensorineural loss. That would also send the Weber tone left, but the left Rinne would be normal (air louder than bone).
2. A 45-year-old has attacks of spinning lasting about 3 hours, with roaring tinnitus, fullness and muffled low-pitched hearing in the right ear. Between attacks his hearing partly recovers. What is the underlying problem, and what is first-line treatment?
   - **Answer:** Meniere disease from endolymphatic hydrops; a low-salt diet and diuretics.
   - **Why:** episodic vertigo lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours, with fluctuating low-frequency sensorineural loss, ear fullness and tinnitus on one side, is Meniere disease, and "diuretics and a low-salt diet" are first-line. [MSD Manual: Meniere Disease](https://msdmanuals.com/en-pt/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/inner-ear-disorders/meniere-disease)
   - **Trap:** vestibular schwannoma, which causes slowly progressive one-sided loss, not attacks with recovery.
3. A 28-year-old woman has slowly worsening hearing in both ears. Her mother wore hearing aids from age 35. Her eardrums look normal, and Rinne shows bone louder than air on both sides. What is the lesion?
   - **Answer:** otosclerosis: new bone at the oval window stops the stapes from moving.
   - **Why:** otosclerosis runs in families ("half of all cases are inherited"), shows up in early adulthood and causes conductive loss behind a normal eardrum. [MSD Manual: Otosclerosis](https://msdmanuals.com/en-au/professional/ear,-nose,-and-throat-disorders/middle-ear-and-tympanic-membrane-disorders/otosclerosis)
   - **Trap:** presbycusis, which is sensorineural (air still beats bone) and starts later in life.
4. A 65-year-old with hypertension has had constant vertigo and vomiting for 6 hours. He cannot walk without falling. The head impulse test is normal, and the nystagmus changes direction with gaze. What should happen next?
   - **Answer:** immediate brain imaging for a cerebellar or brainstem stroke.
   - **Why:** a normal head impulse test and direction-changing nystagmus point to a central cause, and ataxia with severe continuous symptoms for more than 1 hour is a red flag. [MSD Manual: Dizziness and Vertigo](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-ear-problems/dizziness-and-vertigo)
   - **Trap:** vestibular neuronitis. Neuronitis gives an abnormal head impulse test and one-direction nystagmus, and patients can usually walk with help (standard teaching).
5. After a small stroke in the right temporal lobe, a 70-year-old notices that hearing is a little worse in both ears and he cannot tell where sounds come from. Why are both ears affected?
   - **Answer:** above the cochlear nuclei, the auditory pathway carries input from both ears, so a lesion there (here the auditory cortex) gives slight loss in both ears and poor sound localization, not one-sided deafness.
   - **Why:** fibers cross at the trapezoid body to the superior olive, the first site of input from both ears; lesions after this point cause mild bilateral loss and localization trouble. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 64-65)
   - **Trap:** a right cochlear nerve lesion, which would cause profound deafness in the right ear only.
