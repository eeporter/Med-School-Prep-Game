## In one line
**Small fetus: symmetric = a fetal problem early; asymmetric (head spared) = a placental or maternal problem later.** **Fetal testing looks for hypoxia: a reactive NST and a BPP of 8-10 are reassuring.** **Labor problems come from the passenger, the pelvis or the powers.** **On the monitor, early = head, variable = cord, late = placenta** (El-Husseiny OB pp. 88-125).

## Fetal growth (El-Husseiny OB pp. 88-90)
- **Growth restriction:** EFW **under the 10th percentile** for gestational age; accurate early dating (ultrasound before 20 weeks) is essential (p. 88). MSD: SGA = under the 10th percentile.
- **Correction (birth weight under 2,500 g is a different term):** your notes give "<2,500 grams" as another definition of growth restriction (p. 88). The WHO defines **low birth weight** as under 2,500 g "**regardless of gestational age**", so a preterm baby can be low birth weight but normally grown. Growth restriction is judged against the percentile for gestational age (MSD).

| | **Symmetric** | **Asymmetric** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pattern | Everything small | **Head spared**, small abdomen |
| Cause | **Fetal**, early: aneuploidy (trisomy 21, 18, 13), early TORCH infection, major anomalies | **Placental or maternal**, later: infarction, abruption, TTTS, velamentous cord; hypertension, preeclampsia, lupus, long-standing type 1 diabetes, malnutrition, tobacco, alcohol, drugs |
| Work-up | Detailed ultrasound, **karyotype**, infection screen | Serial ultrasound, NST, AFI, BPP, **umbilical artery Doppler** |
| Fluid | Antepartum tests usually normal | AFI often **low** |

Sources: El-Husseiny OB pp. 88-90. MSD agrees: symmetric "usually results from a fetal problem that begins early in gestation"; asymmetric "usually results from placental or maternal problems" in the late second or third trimester.
- **Other source (MSD):** SGA newborns risk **hypoglycemia**, **polycythemia** (chronic mild hypoxia), hypothermia, and perinatal asphyxia ("the most serious potential complication"), with meconium aspiration.
- **Macrosomia** (p. 90): EFW over the 90th-95th percentile, or birth weight over 4,000-4,500 g. Risks: **diabetes (gestational or overt)**, prolonged pregnancy, obesity, high weight gain, multiparity, male fetus.

## Antepartum fetal testing (El-Husseiny OB pp. 91-97)
The tests are good at confirming the fetus is well, but poor at predicting harm (p. 91). They are used when the risk of fetal death is high: hypertension, diabetes, decreased fetal movement, post-term pregnancy, growth restriction (p. 91).

| Test | Reassuring | Worrying | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NST** | **Reactive**: 2 accelerations in 20 minutes (p. 92) | Nonreactive: often sleep, prematurity, drugs, CNS anomaly (p. 92) | **Vibroacoustic stimulation**, then BPP or CST (p. 92) |
| **BPP** (5 parts × 2 points: NST, fluid, body movement, tone, breathing) | **8-10/10** (p. 93) | **0-4/10** = hypoxia from placental insufficiency (p. 93) | 0-4: **prompt delivery**; 6/10: repeat in 24 hours (p. 93) |
| **Modified BPP** | NST + amniotic fluid; almost as predictive (p. 93) | | |
| **AFI** (4 quadrants) | | **Oligohydramnios**: AFI ≤5 cm or deepest pocket under 2 cm; **polyhydramnios**: ≥24 cm (p. 94) | |
| **CST** (3 contractions in 10 minutes) | **Negative**: no late decelerations (p. 95) | **Positive**: late decelerations with ≥50% of contractions (p. 96) | Positive: **prompt delivery** (p. 96) |
| **Umbilical artery Doppler** | Good diastolic flow (low resistance) | **Absent or reversed diastolic flow** (p. 97) | May need delivery; useful only in growth-restricted fetuses (p. 97) |

- **FHR** (p. 92): normal **110-160/min**; bradycardia under 110; tachycardia over 160.
- **CST contraindications** (p. 96): anything where contractions are dangerous: prior **classical** uterine incision, prior myomectomy, **placenta previa**, incompetent cervix, preterm rupture of membranes, preterm labor.
- **Other source (MSD):** an acceleration is a rise of **≥15/min for ≥15 seconds** at 32 weeks or later (≥10/min for ≥10 seconds before 32 weeks).

## Fetal orientation and breech (El-Husseiny OB pp. 99-105)
- **Lie:** longitudinal in 99% at term; **transverse** lie is common early and usually corrects; at term, ECV or cesarean (p. 99). MSD: transverse lie with shoulder presentation "requires cesarean delivery".
- **Presentation:** cephalic in 96% at term (p. 99). **Breech types** (p. 99): **frank** (hips flexed, knees extended; the only breech that could be delivered vaginally), **complete** (hips and knees flexed), **footling** (legs extended). The main risk of vaginal breech birth is **trapping of the after-coming head** (p. 99).
- **Position:** **occiput anterior** is the most common at delivery (p. 101). MSD: **occiput posterior** is the most common abnormal position and can stall the second stage.
- **Attitude:** vertex (fully flexed; most common), military, brow, face (fully extended) (p. 101). **Station:** centimeters above or below the **ischial spines** (p. 102).
- **Breech** (p. 103): about 25% before 28 weeks, only 4% by 37 weeks. Risks: prematurity, multiparity, twins, uterine anomalies, fibroids, fetal anomalies, abnormal placentation. Confirm with **ultrasound**.
  - **ECV** at **37 weeks or later** if there is no contraindication to vaginal birth (previa, active herpes, prior classical cesarean) or to ECV (ruptured membranes, abnormal FHR tracing, oligohydramnios). It lowers cesarean rates. **A prior low transverse cesarean is not a contraindication** and does not lower success (p. 103).
  - With contraindications: cesarean at 37 weeks (p. 103).
  - **Other source (MSD):** ECV is usually done at 37 or 38 weeks and succeeds in about **50-75%**.

## Normal labor (El-Husseiny OB pp. 106-111)
- **Labor** = regular contractions (at least every 5 minutes, lasting 30 seconds) that cause progressive effacement and dilation (p. 106). The **upper segment** contracts and thickens; the **lower segment** thins passively (p. 106).
- **Cardinal movements** (pp. 106-107): **engagement, descent, flexion** (together), then **internal rotation**, **extension**, **external rotation**, **expulsion**.

| Stage | From → to | Your notes' limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1, latent** | Regular contractions → about **6 cm** | Up to 20 h (first baby) or 14 h (later babies); never by itself a reason for cesarean (p. 109) |
| **1, active** | **6 cm → 10 cm** | Expected ≥1 cm every 2 hours (p. 109) |
| **2** | Full dilation → baby | Up to 3 h first baby (4 h with epidural); 2 h later babies (3 h with epidural) (p. 110) |
| **3** | Baby → placenta | Up to **30 minutes** (p. 110) |
| **"4"** | First 2 hours after birth | Watch vital signs and bleeding for preeclampsia and hemorrhage (p. 110) |

- **False labor (Braxton Hicks):** mild, irregular contractions **without cervical change**; return to routine care (p. 110).
- **Oxytocin** (p. 111): posterior pituitary hormone used for induction, augmentation and postpartum hemorrhage.
  - Because it resembles ADH, long high-dose use causes **water retention, hyponatremia and seizures** (p. 111). Pharm HY (PDF 147): high doses cause an "SIADH action"; overstimulation can rupture a scarred uterus after cesarean.
  - **Tachysystole** = more than 5 contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over 30 minutes; it reduces placental blood flow between contractions and can cause FHR abnormalities (p. 111).
  - Osmosis Physiology (p. 561): during pregnancy oxytocin is held back by placental oxytocinase and progesterone; estrogen raises myometrial oxytocin receptors at term, and head pressure on the cervix → more oxytocin is a positive feedback loop (the name **Ferguson reflex** is standard teaching).
- **Other source (MSD):** latent phase mean 7.3-8.6 h in first labors (95th percentile 17-21 h); second stage mean 36-57 minutes in first labors (95th percentile 122-197 minutes); third stage "may last up to 30 minutes".

## Abnormal labor (El-Husseiny OB pp. 112-115)
- **Three Ps:** **Passenger** (big baby, malposition such as OP, anomaly), **Pelvis** (small bony pelvis), **Powers** (weak or uncoordinated contractions) (p. 113).

| Problem | Definition (your notes) | Usual cause | Management |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Prolonged latent phase** | Under 6 cm for over 20 h (first baby) or 14 h (p. 112) | **Too much analgesia** (most common), weak or uncoordinated contractions | **Therapeutic rest and sedation** (p. 112) |
| **Protracted active phase** | Over 6 cm, dilating under 1 cm per 2 hours (p. 113) | Passenger, pelvis, powers | **IV oxytocin**, not cesarean (p. 113) |
| **Arrest of active phase** | ≥6 cm, membranes ruptured, **no change for ≥4 h with adequate contractions** or ≥6 h on oxytocin with inadequate contractions (pp. 109, 113) | Passenger, pelvis, powers | Cesarean (standard teaching) |
| **Prolonged second stage** | Not enough descent after ≥3 h pushing (first baby) or ≥2 h (later); add 1 h with an epidural (p. 115) | **Fetal malposition** (OT, OP) is most common (p. 115) | Check contractions and pushing (oxytocin, coaching). Head **not engaged → cesarean**; **engaged → forceps or vacuum** (p. 115) |
| **Prolonged third stage** | Placenta not delivered in 30 minutes (p. 115) | Weak contractions, or **accreta, increta, percreta** (guide 37) | **Manual removal**, rarely hysterectomy (p. 115) |

- **Other source (MSD, protracted labor):** active-phase acceleration starts at "≥ 4 to 6 cm", and MSD describes arrest as no change for **2 to 4 hours**; second-stage arrest is ≥3 h pushing (first baby) or ≥2 h (later babies), plus 1 hour with an epidural (matches your notes). Oxytocin is used for weak contractions if the fetus is under 5,000 g (under 4,500 g with diabetes).

## Delivery emergencies and injuries (El-Husseiny OB pp. 116-121)
- **Umbilical cord prolapse** (p. 116): occult, partial or complete; risk = ruptured membranes with the presenting part not filling the cervix, malpresentation.
  - **Do not hold or push back the cord.** **Knee-chest position**, **lift the presenting part off the cord**, **immediate cesarean** (p. 116).
  - **Other source (MSD):** overt prolapse is more common with breech or transverse lie; severe bradycardia or severe variable decelerations show cord compression; keep holding the presenting part up while preparing cesarean.
- **Shoulder dystocia** (p. 117): the shoulders are delayed after the head delivers, with the **anterior shoulder stuck behind the pubic symphysis**. Risks: **diabetes, obesity, post-term pregnancy** (all → macrosomia).
  - First steps: **McRoberts** (flex the hips sharply onto the abdomen; flattens the sacral promontory) plus **suprapubic pressure** (dislodges the anterior shoulder); together they relieve almost half (p. 117).
  - **Other source (MSD):** the "**turtle sign**" (the head delivers and pulls back against the perineum) makes the diagnosis; further maneuvers include Wood screw, Rubin and delivery of the posterior arm; Zavanelli is a last resort; "**Fundal pressure is avoided**". Baby: brachial plexus injury, clavicle or humerus fracture. Mother: hemorrhage, lacerations, sphincter injury.
  - **Brachial plexus birth injuries** (El-Husseiny MSK pp. 24-25):
    - **Erb palsy** (upper trunk, **C5-C6**) from **lateral traction on the neck** during delivery: arm hangs at the side (deltoid, supraspinatus), **internally rotated** (infraspinatus), **extended and pronated** (biceps): "**waiter's tip**";
    - **Klumpke palsy** (lower trunk, **C8-T1**) from an **upward pull on the arm**.
- **Perineal lacerations** (p. 119):

| Degree | Tissue torn | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First** | Vaginal mucosa only | Often none |
| **Second** | + perineal body muscles (not the sphincter) | Sutures |
| **Third** | + **anal sphincter** (not rectal mucosa) | Sutures to prevent anal incontinence |
| **Fourth** | Through to the **rectal mucosa** | Faulty healing → **rectovaginal fistula** |

- Uncomplicated lacerations: NSAIDs and sitz baths (p. 118).
- **Rectovaginal fistula** (p. 120): obstetric injury is the most common cause, mostly after third- or fourth-degree tears, poor repair or infection; in low-resource settings, from **prolonged second stage pressure necrosis**. Symptoms: **gas or stool through the vagina**, foul brown discharge; dark red velvety rectal mucosa seen on the back vaginal wall; surgical repair.
- **Episiotomy** (p. 121): no longer routine in the US; more pain, slower return to sex and **more extension into the sphincter and rectum** than natural tears. Possible uses: shoulder dystocia, nonreassuring FHR, forceps or vacuum, vaginal breech, narrow birth canal.

## Intrapartum fetal heart rate monitoring (El-Husseiny OB pp. 122-125)
- A well-oxygenated fetal brain gives a baseline of **110-160/min** with **moderate variability (6-25/min)** (p. 122). MSD agrees.

| Deceleration | Shape and timing | Cause | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Early** | Gradual, mirrors the contraction | **Head compression** → vagal response | **None**: benign (p. 123) |
| **Variable** | **Abrupt** drop and quick return; not always tied to contractions | **Cord compression** (often after membranes rupture) | Depends on the overall tracing (p. 123) |
| **Late** | Gradual, starts **after the contraction peak** | **Uteroplacental insufficiency** → hypoxemia and acidemia | Treat as a warning sign (p. 123) |

Sources: El-Husseiny OB p. 123 for causes; MSD for the timing definitions ("begins near the start" for early, "abrupt decrease of ≥ 15 beats/minute" for variable, "begins after the peak" for late).

- **Categories** (p. 125):
  - **I** (normal): low risk; continue routine care;
  - **II** (indeterminate): keep watching and re-evaluate;
  - **III** (abnormal): high risk of severe hypoxia → **intrauterine resuscitation**: reposition the mother, oxygen, IV fluids, **stop oxytocin**; if not fully dilated and no improvement → **immediate cesarean**.
  - **Other source (MSD):** a category III pattern "usually indicates fetal metabolic acidosis"; a **sinusoidal pattern** is abnormal; corrective steps include supplemental oxygen, repositioning, treating maternal hypotension and stopping oxytocin; if it does not improve and birth is not imminent, "urgent delivery by cesarean is needed".

## Labor analgesia and anesthesia (El-Husseiny OB pp. 126-128)
- **Pain pathways** (p. 126): **stage 1** (contractions and cervical dilation) → **T10-T12**; **stage 2** (perineal stretch) → **S2-S4**.
- **Other source (Labor and Maguire 2008):** first-stage pain "is mediated by **T10 to L1** spinal segments"; second-stage pain "is carried by **T12 to L1, and S2 to S4**". If an answer choice says T10-L1 for stage 1, that is the same pathway.
- **IV opioids and sedatives** (p. 127): easy and cheap, but can **depress the newborn's breathing** if given close to birth → **naloxone** for the baby. MSD: naloxone 0.01 mg/kg; recheck the newborn 1-2 hours later.
- **Paracervical block** (p. 127): blocks Frankenhauser's ganglion beside the cervix; can cause **transient fetal bradycardia**. MSD: fetal bradycardia in over 10%, so it is rarely appropriate.
- **Pudendal block** (p. 127; El-Husseiny MSK p. 55): local anesthetic around the **pudendal nerve (S2-S4)** where it passes the **ischial spine** (the landmark), in **stage 2**, for perineal anesthesia. MSD: it numbs the lower vagina, perineum and back of the vulva, but not the front of the vulva (lumbar dermatomes).
- **Epidural** (p. 128): local anesthetic in the epidural space blocks lumbosacral roots for both stages and can be extended for cesarean. MSD: it does not raise the cesarean rate.
  - **Hypotension** (up to 10%): sympathetic block → venodilation → less venous return → lower cardiac output → less placental blood flow. Treat with **left uterine displacement**, an **IV fluid bolus** or a **vasopressor** (p. 128).
  - **Post-dural puncture headache**: accidental dural puncture → CSF leak → **headache worse sitting or standing, better lying down**, within 72 hours, sometimes with nausea and neck stiffness; most resolve; severe cases → **epidural blood patch** (p. 128).

## Clinical vignette
A 30-year-old woman in her first labor at 40 weeks is 8 cm dilated with an epidural and an oxytocin infusion. She has had 6 contractions in each of the last three 10-minute windows. The FHR baseline is 150/min with minimal variability, and each contraction is followed by a gradual drop in heart rate that begins after the peak of the contraction and recovers slowly.

- **Tracing:** **late decelerations** with reduced variability from **tachysystole** (more than 5 contractions in 10 minutes) cutting placental blood flow (El-Husseiny OB pp. 111, 123; MSD timing definition).
- **First steps:** **stop the oxytocin**, turn her onto her **left side**, give an **IV fluid bolus** and oxygen, and check for **epidural hypotension** (pp. 125, 128; MSD).
- **If no improvement:** she is not fully dilated, so **immediate cesarean** (p. 125).
- **Why the epidural matters:** sympathetic block can drop her blood pressure and placental perfusion (p. 128).

## Common traps
- **Under 2,500 g = low birth weight, not growth restriction** (WHO; MSD).
- **Asymmetric (head-sparing) growth restriction points to the placenta**; symmetric to the fetus (pp. 89-90; MSD).
- **Nonreactive NST is often sleep**: try vibroacoustic stimulation before assuming hypoxia (p. 92).
- **BPP 0-4 → deliver; 6 → repeat in 24 h** (p. 93).
- **CST is contraindicated with previa or a prior classical incision** (p. 96).
- **A prior low transverse cesarean does not rule out ECV** (p. 103).
- **Protracted active phase → oxytocin; arrest → cesarean** (p. 113).
- **Prolonged latent phase → rest, not cesarean** (p. 112).
- **Early = head, variable = cord, late = placenta** (p. 123).
- **Cord prolapse: lift the presenting part, never push the cord back** (p. 116; MSD).
- **Shoulder dystocia: McRoberts + suprapubic pressure; no fundal pressure** (p. 117; MSD).
- **Erb = C5-C6, waiter's tip; Klumpke = C8-T1** (MSK pp. 24-25).
- **Pudendal block landmark = ischial spine** (p. 127; MSK p. 55).
- **Positional headache after an epidural = dural puncture → blood patch if severe** (p. 128).
- **High-dose oxytocin → hyponatremia** (p. 111; Pharm HY PDF 147).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 32-year-old woman with preeclampsia at 33 weeks has a fundal height of 28 cm. Ultrasound shows an estimated fetal weight at the 6th percentile; the head circumference is normal for dates, but the abdominal circumference is small. The amniotic fluid index is 4 cm. Which mechanism best explains the growth pattern?
   - **Answer:** **reduced uteroplacental blood flow, with blood redirected to the fetal brain.**
   - **Why:** head-sparing (asymmetric) growth restriction comes from placental insufficiency due to maternal disease such as preeclampsia, and the AFI is often low (El-Husseiny OB pp. 89-90; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **a fetal chromosome abnormality**, which causes **symmetric** growth restriction starting early (p. 89).

2. A 28-year-old woman at 22 weeks has an ultrasound showing that the head, abdomen and femur all measure below the 3rd percentile for dates, which were set by a first-trimester ultrasound. A large ventricular septal defect and clenched hands with overlapping fingers are seen. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **fetal karyotype (amniocentesis with chromosome analysis).**
   - **Why:** symmetric growth restriction starting early, with structural anomalies, suggests a fetal cause such as **aneuploidy** (trisomy 18); the work-up includes a detailed ultrasound, karyotype and infection screening (El-Husseiny OB pp. 88-89).
   - **Trap:** **umbilical artery Doppler studies**, which help with placental (asymmetric) growth restriction (p. 97).

3. A 26-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes at 35 weeks has a nonstress test. In 20 minutes there are no accelerations; the baseline is 140/min with moderate variability and no decelerations. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **vibroacoustic stimulation and continued monitoring.**
   - **Why:** a nonreactive NST is often due to fetal sleep; vibroacoustic stimulation is the next step, then a BPP or CST if still nonreactive (El-Husseiny OB p. 92).
   - **Trap:** **immediate delivery**. A nonreactive NST has a high false-positive rate and cannot by itself diagnose acidemia (p. 92).

4. A 35-year-old woman at 38 weeks with chronic hypertension reports decreased fetal movement. A biophysical profile scores 4/10: the NST is nonreactive, there are no breathing movements, and the deepest fluid pocket is 1.5 cm; tone and body movements are present. Which is the most appropriate management?
   - **Answer:** **prompt delivery.**
   - **Why:** a BPP of 0-4/10 indicates fetal hypoxia from placental insufficiency and a high risk of fetal death (El-Husseiny OB p. 93). The deepest pocket under 2 cm also meets the oligohydramnios cutoff (p. 94).
   - **Trap:** **repeat the BPP in 24 hours**, which is the plan for an equivocal 6/10 (p. 93).

5. A 24-year-old woman in her first labor at 39 weeks is 6 cm dilated. Each contraction is matched by a gradual, shallow fall in fetal heart rate that starts as the contraction starts and returns to baseline as it ends. The baseline is 135/min with moderate variability. What causes these decelerations?
   - **Answer:** **compression of the fetal head** (a vagal response).
   - **Why:** these are **early decelerations**, a benign finding that needs no action (El-Husseiny OB p. 123; MSD timing).
   - **Trap:** **uteroplacental insufficiency**, which causes **late** decelerations starting after the contraction peak (p. 123).

6. A 29-year-old woman in labor at 40 weeks has her membranes ruptured artificially at 5 cm. Over the next 30 minutes the fetal heart rate shows sudden, sharp drops of 30-40/min lasting less than a minute, with a quick return to baseline; they do not consistently line up with contractions. What is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** **umbilical cord compression.**
   - **Why:** abrupt drops that are not always timed with contractions are **variable decelerations**; loss of amniotic fluid after rupture can lead to cord compression (El-Husseiny OB p. 123; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **head compression**, which gives gradual early decelerations that mirror contractions.

7. A 31-year-old woman at 37 weeks has a fetus in frank breech presentation confirmed by ultrasound. Her only previous birth was by low transverse cesarean for a nonreassuring tracing. Membranes are intact, the fluid volume is normal, the placenta is fundal, and the fetal tracing is reactive. Which is the most appropriate recommendation?
   - **Answer:** **offer external cephalic version.**
   - **Why:** ECV is offered at 37 weeks or later if there is no contraindication; a prior **low transverse** cesarean is not a contraindication and does not lower success (El-Husseiny OB p. 103). MSD: success about 50-75%.
   - **Trap:** **scheduled cesarean at 37 weeks because of the prior cesarean**. That plan is for women with contraindications such as a prior **classical** incision or previa (p. 103).

8. A 27-year-old woman in her first labor is 7 cm dilated with ruptured membranes. An intrauterine pressure catheter shows adequate contractions. Her cervix has not changed in 4.5 hours. The fetal tracing is category I. The estimated fetal weight is 4,100 g. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **cesarean delivery.**
   - **Why:** at ≥6 cm with ruptured membranes, no change for ≥4 hours despite adequate contractions is **arrest of the active phase** (El-Husseiny OB pp. 109, 113). Arrest, unlike slow progress, is not fixed by more oxytocin (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **start oxytocin**, which is for a protracted active phase or weak contractions (p. 113).

9. A 22-year-old woman in her first labor has had painful regular contractions for 22 hours. Her cervix is 4 cm dilated and has changed only slowly. She received several doses of IV opioid. Membranes are intact, and the fetal tracing is category I. Which is the most appropriate management?
   - **Answer:** **therapeutic rest with sedation.**
   - **Why:** a latent phase over 20 hours in a first labor is a **prolonged latent phase**, most often from too much analgesia, and is managed with rest and sedation; it is never by itself a reason for cesarean (El-Husseiny OB pp. 109, 112).
   - **Trap:** **cesarean delivery for failure to progress**. She is not yet in the active phase (6 cm).

10. A 30-year-old woman in her first labor has an epidural and has pushed for 4.5 hours with good effort and adequate contractions. The head is engaged at +3 station, in the occiput posterior position. The fetal tracing is category I. Which is the most appropriate next step?
    - **Answer:** **operative vaginal delivery (vacuum or forceps).**
    - **Why:** over 4 hours of pushing with an epidural in a first labor is a **prolonged second stage**; the most common cause is malposition such as OP. With adequate powers and an **engaged** head, forceps or vacuum is the next step (El-Husseiny OB p. 115; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **emergency cesarean**, which your notes reserve for a head that is **not engaged** (p. 115).

11. A 33-year-old woman in labor with a fetus in footling breech presentation has spontaneous rupture of membranes at 5 cm. The fetal heart rate falls to 70/min. On exam, a loop of pulsating umbilical cord is in the vagina. Which is the most appropriate immediate action while preparing for delivery?
    - **Answer:** **push the presenting part up off the cord with a hand in the vagina, put her in knee-chest position, and perform an immediate cesarean.**
    - **Why:** this is overt **cord prolapse**, more common with breech presentation once membranes rupture (El-Husseiny OB p. 116; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **pushing the cord back into the uterus**, which your notes say not to do (p. 116).

12. A 36-year-old woman with gestational diabetes and a BMI of 38 delivers the head of a 4,600-g baby, which then retracts tightly against the perineum. The shoulders do not deliver with gentle downward traction. Which is the most appropriate first step?
    - **Answer:** **McRoberts maneuver with suprapubic pressure.**
    - **Why:** the "turtle sign" means **shoulder dystocia** with the anterior shoulder stuck behind the pubic symphysis. McRoberts flattens the sacral promontory and suprapubic pressure dislodges the shoulder; together they relieve almost half (El-Husseiny OB p. 117; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **fundal pressure**, which MSD says to avoid; pushing from above drives the shoulder harder against the pubic bone (standard teaching).

13. A newborn delivered after a difficult shoulder dystocia holds his right arm at his side, internally rotated, with the elbow extended and the forearm pronated. His grasp is normal. Which nerve roots are most likely injured?
    - **Answer:** **C5-C6 (upper trunk of the brachial plexus).**
    - **Why:** this is **Erb palsy** ("waiter's tip") from lateral traction on the neck during delivery: loss of the deltoid and supraspinatus (arm at the side), infraspinatus (internal rotation) and biceps (extended, pronated) (El-Husseiny MSK p. 24).
    - **Trap:** **C8-T1** (Klumpke palsy), which affects the hand muscles after an upward pull on the arm (MSK p. 25).

14. A 25-year-old woman had a vaginal delivery with a fourth-degree laceration 12 days ago. She now passes gas and small amounts of stool through her vagina and has a foul brown discharge. Exam shows dark red, velvety tissue on the back wall of the vagina. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **rectovaginal fistula.**
    - **Why:** obstetric injury, especially breakdown of a third- or fourth-degree tear repair, is the most common cause; the rectal mucosa is seen on the posterior vaginal wall; treatment is surgical repair (El-Husseiny OB pp. 119-120).
    - **Trap:** **a third-degree laceration that healed normally**. Third-degree tears do not reach the rectal mucosa, and normal healing does not leak stool (p. 119).

15. A 29-year-old woman in labor receives an epidural. Fifteen minutes later her blood pressure is 84/48 mm Hg (it was 122/76), she feels nauseated, and the fetal heart rate shows late decelerations. Which is the most likely cause of the fetal heart rate change?
    - **Answer:** **sympathetic blockade causing venodilation, less venous return and lower placental blood flow.**
    - **Why:** epidural hypotension reduces cardiac output and placental perfusion, which can cause fetal acidosis; treat with left uterine displacement, an IV fluid bolus or a vasopressor (El-Husseiny OB p. 128).
    - **Trap:** **an allergic reaction to the local anesthetic**, which would bring urticaria or wheeze, not this timing after the block (standard teaching).

16. A 27-year-old woman who had an epidural during labor 2 days ago has a severe headache that starts within minutes of sitting up and goes away when she lies flat. She has mild neck stiffness but no fever, and her neurologic exam is normal. Bed rest and analgesics have not helped, and she cannot care for her baby. Which treatment is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **an epidural blood patch.**
    - **Why:** a **positional headache** after neuraxial anesthesia comes from an accidental dural puncture with CSF leak and low pressure; most resolve, but severe cases get a blood patch (El-Husseiny OB p. 128).
    - **Trap:** **lumbar puncture for suspected meningitis**. She has no fever, and the headache is clearly positional.

17. During the second stage of labor, an obstetrician plans to numb the perineum by injecting local anesthetic through the lateral vaginal wall. Which bony landmark guides the injection?
    - **Answer:** **the ischial spine.**
    - **Why:** a **pudendal block** targets the pudendal nerve (S2-S4) as it passes the ischial spine (El-Husseiny OB p. 127; El-Husseiny MSK p. 55; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **the sacral promontory**, a landmark for pelvic measurements, not for the pudendal nerve (standard teaching).

18. A 23-year-old woman has had labor induced with a high-dose IV oxytocin infusion and large volumes of dextrose in water for 18 hours. She becomes confused and then has a generalized seizure. Her blood pressure is 118/74 mm Hg, and her urine has no protein. Which laboratory finding is most likely?
    - **Answer:** **low serum sodium (hyponatremia).**
    - **Why:** oxytocin is structurally similar to ADH, so long high-dose infusions cause **water retention and hyponatremia**, which can cause seizures (El-Husseiny OB p. 111; Pharm HY PDF 147: "SIADH action").
    - **Trap:** **eclampsia**, which is unlikely with a normal blood pressure and no proteinuria (guide 37).
