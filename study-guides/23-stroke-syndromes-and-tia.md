# Stroke syndromes by artery, aphasias and TIA

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (stroke pp. 172-177; lobes, aphasias and blood supply pp. 115-130) and the Osmosis High-Yield Neurology notes (TIA, aphasias, lacunar stroke). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where a newer or different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Match the deficit to the artery. **Leg** worse than arm = anterior cerebral. **Face and arm** worse than leg, plus aphasia or neglect = middle cerebral. **Vision** loss on one side of both eyes = posterior cerebral. Pure motor or pure sensory with no cortical signs = lacunar. **Crossed** findings (face on one side, body on the other) = brainstem.

## Stroke basics
About **85%** of strokes are ischemic (a blocked artery) and **15%** are hemorrhagic (a bleed). Ischemic strokes are **thrombotic** (a clot on a plaque at the site, often the middle cerebral artery), **embolic** (a clot from elsewhere, such as atrial fibrillation, or a leg DVT crossing a patent foramen ovale; can hit several territories at once), or **global hypoxic** (whole-brain low flow, as in cardiac arrest). (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 172) Your notes call stroke the third most common cause of death in the US (p. 172).
- **Recent change:** in the CDC's latest data (2024), stroke is the **4th** leading cause of death in the US, after heart disease, cancer and accidents. [CDC FastStats: Leading Causes of Death (2024 data, reviewed 29 Sep 2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/fastats/leading-causes-of-death.htm)

## Core mechanism
1. Ischemic stroke is usually from a clot forming on a large-artery plaque (atherothrombosis), an **embolus** (for example from atrial fibrillation), or a **lacunar** infarct in a small deep artery. MSD gives the usual cause of lacunes as **lipohyalinosis**. [MSD Manual: Ischemic Stroke](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/ischemic-stroke)
2. Each artery feeds a fixed part of the brain, and the motor and sensory cortex is mapped like a body (homunculus). The **leg** area sits on the medial surface (anterior cerebral territory), and the **face and arm** areas on the lateral surface (middle cerebral territory). That is why the pattern of weakness points to the artery (homunculus layout is standard teaching). [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes)
3. Language sits in the **left** hemisphere in right-handed people and in about two-thirds of left-handed people. So a left middle cerebral stroke causes aphasia, and a right one causes neglect. [MSD Manual: Aphasia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/function-and-dysfunction-of-the-cerebral-lobes/aphasia), [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes)
4. In the **brainstem**, cranial nerve nuclei serve the same side, while the long motor and sensory tracts have not yet crossed (or have just crossed). So one lesion gives **same-side face** findings with **opposite-side body** findings. MSD: "Ipsilateral facial sensory loss or motor weakness with contralateral body hemianesthesia or hemiparesis indicates a lesion at the pons or medulla." [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes)

## Global hypoxic injury (watershed)
- If whole-brain ischemia lasts **longer than about 5 minutes**, neurons die. The most vulnerable cells are the **hippocampus** (damaged first), the neocortex, cerebellar **Purkinje** cells and the **watershed** zones between the anterior, middle and posterior cerebral territories. Mnemonic from your notes: "vulnerable hippos need pure water". Watershed infarcts are wedge-shaped strips on both sides near the top of the brain. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 172-173)

## Stroke syndromes by artery
| Artery | Deficits |
| --- | --- |
| Anterior cerebral (uncommon) | Opposite-side weakness and numbness, **worst in the leg**; urinary incontinence (your notes add: usually only when both sides are damaged, El-Husseiny p. 126); apathy, confusion, poor judgment, mutism; grasp reflex; gait apraxia |
| Middle cerebral (common) | Opposite-side weakness and numbness, **face and arm worse than leg**; dysarthria; opposite-side homonymous hemianopia, or an opposite-side **upper (superior) quadrantanopia** if the temporal-lobe branches to **Meyer's loop** are hit; **aphasia** if the dominant (usually left) side; **neglect and apraxia** if the nondominant side. Supplies Broca and Wernicke areas and, through its **lenticulostriate** branches, the genu and posterior limb of the internal capsule (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 125) |
| Posterior cerebral | Opposite-side **homonymous hemianopia**, often with **macular sparing** (the occipital pole gets blood from both the posterior and middle cerebral arteries; El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 122, 126); **alexia without agraphia** if the left posterior cerebral artery also hits the splenium of the corpus callosum (p. 122); cortical blindness if both sides are hit (Osmosis HY Neurology, stroke); memory loss; third nerve palsy; hemiballismus |
| Ophthalmic (first major branch of the internal carotid; El-Husseiny p. 176) | **Monocular** vision loss (amaurosis) |
| Vertebrobasilar | Cranial nerve signs (vertigo, nystagmus, double vision, trouble swallowing, slurred speech), ataxia, **crossed** sensory and motor deficits, coma and death if the basilar artery is fully blocked |
| Lacunar (small deep penetrating arteries such as the **lenticulostriate** branches; damaged by **lipohyalinosis** and microatheroma, mostly from chronic **hypertension**) | **No cortical signs**, plus one of: **pure motor** hemiparesis (posterior limb of the **internal capsule**), **pure sensory** loss (**thalamus**), ataxic hemiparesis, or dysarthria-clumsy hand. Healed lacunes are small fluid-filled cavities ("lacune" means lake). (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 129; Osmosis HY Neurology, lacunar stroke) |

Source: [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes), plus your notes where cited.

**Internal capsule blood supply** (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 127):

| Part | Artery | Main tracts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anterior limb | Medial striate branch of the anterior cerebral | Thalamocortical |
| Genu | Lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral | Corticobulbar (face) |
| Posterior limb | Lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral | Corticospinal and sensory thalamocortical |

### Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome
Most often from blockage of the **posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA)**; vertebral artery dissection can also be the cause (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 82). Each finding maps to a structure in the lateral medulla:

| Structure hit | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Spinal trigeminal nucleus and tract | **Same-side face**: loss of pain and temperature |
| Lateral spinothalamic tract | **Opposite-side body**: loss of pain and temperature |
| Nucleus ambiguus | Trouble swallowing, hoarseness, slurred speech |
| Descending sympathetic (oculosympathetic) fibers | **Horner syndrome** on the same side: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis |
| Vestibular nuclei | Vertigo, nausea, vomiting, nystagmus |
| Inferior cerebellar peduncle | Same-side limb ataxia |

Sources: El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 82 (every row except the vestibular nuclei, including the same-side Horner and ataxia; nucleus ambiguus signs are specific to PICA); Osmosis HY Neurology, p. 17 (dizziness and nystagmus, the vestibular nuclei row). Limb weakness is not a feature, because the pyramids are medial (standard teaching).

**Read more on EyeWiki:** [Wallenberg Syndrome](https://eyewiki.org/Wallenberg_Syndrome) (link for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

## Aphasias
| Type | Lesion (left hemisphere) | Speech | Comprehension | Repetition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broca (expressive) | Frontal or frontoparietal, including Broca area | **Nonfluent**; frustrated | Good | Impaired |
| Wernicke (receptive) | Temporoparietal, including Wernicke area | **Fluent but meaningless**; unaware | **Poor** (spoken and written) | Impaired |
| Conduction | Subcortical, under the superior temporal gyrus or inferior parietal lobe | Fluent, many word errors | Good | **Impaired** (the key finding) |
| Global | Large frontotemporoparietal lesion | Nonfluent, sparse | Poor | Impaired |
| Transcortical motor | Frontal lobe **around** Broca area (Broca and arcuate spared) | Nonfluent | Good | **Intact** |
| Transcortical sensory | Temporal lobe **around** Wernicke area | Fluent | Poor | **Intact** |
| Mixed transcortical | Watershed areas around both (both areas and arcuate spared) | Nonfluent | Poor | **Intact** |

Sources: [MSD Manual: Aphasia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/function-and-dysfunction-of-the-cerebral-lobes/aphasia) for the first four rows; El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 120 for the transcortical rows. Conduction aphasia is damage to the **arcuate fasciculus**, the tract linking Wernicke and Broca areas, which is why repetition fails while comprehension and fluency are fine. Every transcortical aphasia spares the arcuate fasciculus, so **repetition is intact**. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 119-120)

**Other lobe syndromes in your notes:**
- **Gerstmann syndrome:** lesion of the **angular gyrus** in the dominant (left) parietal lobe. Four signs: **agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia, left-right confusion**, often with alexia. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 118)
- **Alexia without agraphia:** the person can write but cannot read. Cause: left posterior cerebral artery stroke hitting the left visual cortex and the splenium, so the intact right visual cortex cannot send words to the left language areas. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 122)

## TIA and acute care
- **TIA in your notes:** a brief, reversible focal deficit with **no infarct on MRI**; most resolve in **under 15 minutes**, and by the classic rule symptoms clear within 24 hours. TIAs are always from clots (thrombus or embolus), never from bleeding. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 176) Osmosis gives a duration of a few minutes to one hour (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Transient ischemic attack").
  - **Recent change:** the definition is now **tissue-based**, not time-based. MSD says deficits that clear between 1 and 24 hours often show infarction on MRI and "are thus no longer considered TIAs". [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack)
- **TIA (MSD):** focal brain ischemia that causes sudden, temporary deficits without permanent infarction. Symptoms usually last under 1 hour, most under 5 minutes. Stroke risk is high, starting in the first 24 hours. The ABCD2 score estimates 2-day stroke risk: about 1% (score 0-3), 4% (4-5) and 8% (6-7). [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack)
- **Amaurosis fugax** is brief one-eye blindness (usually under 5 minutes) from ophthalmic artery ischemia. [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack) Your notes add the usual cause: an embolus from a carotid plaque lodging in the retinal artery (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 176).
- **Subclavian steal:** a narrowed subclavian artery before the vertebral artery's origin. When the arm exercises, blood is pulled backward from the vertebral artery, giving vertebrobasilar symptoms. [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack) Your notes add: the arm gets **claudication** (cold, tingling, aching) with exercise, along with dizziness or visual symptoms; **duplex ultrasound** showing reversed vertebral flow makes the diagnosis; bypass surgery cures it (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 177).
- **Imaging in your notes:** non-contrast head CT is the best first test, to rule out a bleed before giving tPA; MRI is the most accurate test. Bleeds show at once as white (hyperdense) areas; many ischemic strokes stay invisible on CT for **6-24 hours**, while **diffusion-weighted MRI** shows ischemia within **3-30 minutes**. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 173)
- **Imaging (MSD):** CT or MRI is done first to **exclude hemorrhage** before clot-busting drugs. Medium to large infarcts start to show as dark (hypodense) areas on CT 6-12 hours after ischemia begins, so an early CT can look normal; diffusion-weighted MRI picks up early ischemia. [MSD Manual: Ischemic Stroke](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/ischemic-stroke) **Other source:** MSD gives 6-12 hours for CT changes; your notes give 6-24 hours. Either way, an early CT can be normal.
- **What the dead tissue looks like over time:** brain infarcts undergo **liquefactive** necrosis, not coagulative necrosis like the heart (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 174). Timeline from your notes (p. 175):

  | Time | Microscope |
  | --- | --- |
  | 12-24 hours | **Red neurons** (pink cytoplasm, shrunken nucleus, lost Nissl substance) |
  | 24-72 hours | Necrosis and **neutrophils** |
  | 3-5 days | **Macrophages** (microglia) eat the debris and fill with lipid |
  | 1-2 weeks | Reactive gliosis; the area liquefies |
  | Over 2 weeks | Glial scar around a **cyst** |

- **Treatment in your notes:** tPA within **3-4.5 hours** of onset if there is no bleed, and/or thrombectomy for a large-artery blockage; aspirin or clopidogrel; control of blood pressure, sugar and lipids; treat atrial fibrillation and carotid stenosis. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 174)
- **Recent change, thrombolysis (2026 AHA/ASA guideline):** IV **tenecteplase or alteplase within 4.5 hours**, started as fast as possible without waiting for extra imaging such as CT angiography or perfusion scans. Selected patients at 4.5-9 hours (or who woke up with symptoms) may be treated if perfusion imaging shows salvageable brain. **Thrombectomy:** for a large-vessel anterior circulation blockage within 6 hours, and in selected patients up to 24 hours; for **basilar artery** blockage within 24 hours. [2026 AHA/ASA Guideline for the Early Management of Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke (Prabhakaran et al., Stroke 2026, doi:10.1161/STR.0000000000000513), as summarized by emDocs](https://www.emdocs.net/2026-guideline-update-early-management-of-acute-ischemic-stroke/) MSD still describes the older (2019) windows. [MSD Manual: Ischemic Stroke](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/ischemic-stroke)
- **Prevention:** antiplatelet therapy such as aspirin for noncardioembolic stroke; a statin for atherosclerotic stroke (LDL cut by 50% or below 70 mg/dL). Carotid endarterectomy or stenting after a recent nondisabling stroke with 70-99% same-side carotid narrowing; MSD's stroke page cites 60% or more for other symptomatic patients such as TIA [MSD Manual: Ischemic Stroke](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/ischemic-stroke), while its TIA page says endarterectomy helps with more than 70% stenosis [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack). For exams, 70% or more on the symptomatic side is the safe answer (standard teaching).

## Clinical vignette
A 70-year-old right-handed man with atrial fibrillation suddenly cannot speak in sentences. He understands commands and is clearly frustrated. His right face and arm are weak; his leg is only mildly weak. Which artery is blocked?

**Answer:** the left middle cerebral artery (superior division, per Osmosis HY Neurology, "Broca's aphasia"), causing Broca aphasia.
**Why:** face and arm worse than leg means middle cerebral territory. Nonfluent speech with good comprehension and frustration is Broca aphasia from a left frontal lesion. Atrial fibrillation is an embolic source. [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes), [MSD Manual: Aphasia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/function-and-dysfunction-of-the-cerebral-lobes/aphasia), [MSD Manual: Ischemic Stroke](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/ischemic-stroke)
**Trap:** anterior cerebral artery, which spares language and hits the leg most.

## Common traps
- Leg > arm = anterior cerebral; face and arm > leg = middle cerebral.
- Broca: Broken speech, understands. Wernicke: Wordy nonsense, doesn't understand. Conduction: can't repeat (mnemonics are standard teaching).
- Neglect of the left side = right (nondominant) parietal lesion in the middle cerebral territory.
- Crossed signs (face one side, body the other) = brainstem, not cortex.
- Wallenberg: same-side face and opposite-side body pain and temperature loss, hoarseness and Horner, with **no** limb weakness.
- An early normal CT does not rule out ischemic stroke; its job is to exclude bleeding.
- A TIA is defined by **no infarction**, not by a 24-hour clock. Most last under 5 minutes, and symptoms with infarction on imaging are a stroke even if they resolve (the older 24-hour time-based definition is in your notes, El-Husseiny p. 176). [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack)
- One-eye vision loss = ophthalmic artery (carotid circulation), not posterior cerebral.
- Poor repetition = Broca, Wernicke, conduction or global. **Intact** repetition = a transcortical aphasia (arcuate fasciculus spared). (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 120)
- Pure motor stroke = posterior limb of the internal capsule; pure sensory stroke = thalamus (lacunes). (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 129)
- Global hypoxia hits the **hippocampus** first. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 173)
- Brain infarct = **liquefactive** necrosis; red neurons appear at 12-24 hours. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 174-175)

## Practice questions
1. A 64-year-old has sudden weakness of the left leg, with the arm only mildly affected, and new urinary incontinence. Which artery?
   - **Answer:** right anterior cerebral artery.
   - **Why:** the anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial cortex with the leg area; MSD lists leg-dominant weakness and urinary incontinence. [MSD Manual: Selected Stroke Syndromes table](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/selected-stroke-syndromes)
   - **Trap:** middle cerebral, which hits the face and arm hardest.
2. A 58-year-old has vertigo, hoarseness and trouble swallowing. He has a right ptosis and small right pupil, lost pain and temperature on the right face and the left body, and right-sided ataxia. Strength is normal. Which artery?
   - **Answer:** right posterior inferior cerebellar artery (lateral medullary syndrome).
   - **Why:** spinal trigeminal (same-side face), spinothalamic (opposite body), nucleus ambiguus (hoarseness, swallowing), sympathetic fibers (Horner), vestibular nuclei (vertigo) and inferior cerebellar peduncle (ataxia) all sit in the lateral medulla; Horner and ataxia are on the same side as the lesion (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 82; Osmosis HY Neurology, p. 17).
   - **Trap:** a hemispheric stroke; crossed face and body findings put the lesion in the brainstem.
3. A 67-year-old speaks fluently but makes no sense. He cannot follow commands and seems unaware of the problem. Where is the lesion?
   - **Answer:** left temporoparietal area, including Wernicke area.
   - **Why:** fluent, meaningless speech with poor comprehension and no awareness is Wernicke aphasia. [MSD Manual: Aphasia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/function-and-dysfunction-of-the-cerebral-lobes/aphasia)
   - **Trap:** conduction aphasia, where comprehension is good and repetition is the problem.
4. A man has 3 minutes of painless vision loss in the right eye, "like a curtain", which fully resolves. Carotid imaging shows 80% narrowing of the right internal carotid. What was the event, and what procedure is considered?
   - **Answer:** amaurosis fugax (a TIA in the ophthalmic artery territory); carotid endarterectomy on the right.
   - **Why:** brief one-eye blindness comes from ophthalmic artery ischemia. Endarterectomy is useful with more than 70% same-side stenosis. [MSD Manual: Transient Ischemic Attack](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/stroke/transient-ischemic-attack)
   - **Trap:** posterior cerebral artery stroke, which affects the same half of the visual field in both eyes. The "curtain" description is standard teaching.
5. After a stroke, a 72-year-old speaks haltingly in short phrases but understands well and can **repeat** long sentences perfectly. Where is the lesion?
   - **Answer:** left frontal lobe **around** Broca area, sparing Broca area and the arcuate fasciculus (transcortical motor aphasia).
   - **Why:** nonfluent speech with good comprehension looks like Broca aphasia, but intact repetition means the arcuate fasciculus loop still works. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 120)
   - **Trap:** Broca aphasia, where repetition is impaired.
6. A 60-year-old is resuscitated after 10 minutes of cardiac arrest. Weeks later he cannot form new memories. Which region was damaged first, and what does a brain biopsy show at 12-24 hours?
   - **Answer:** the **hippocampus**; **red neurons** (pink, shrunken neurons with dark nuclei).
   - **Why:** global ischemia over about 5 minutes kills the most vulnerable neurons first, and the hippocampus is first; red neurons are the first microscopic change at 12-24 hours. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 172-175)
   - **Trap:** coagulative necrosis, which is what the heart shows; the brain undergoes liquefactive necrosis.
