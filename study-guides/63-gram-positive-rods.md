## In one line
**Sort a gram-positive rod by spores and oxygen:**
1. **Spore-formers** (survive boiling; killed by autoclaving at 121°C for 15 minutes, El-Husseiny Micro p. 63):
   - **aerobic = *Bacillus*** (*B. anthracis*, *B. cereus*);
   - **anaerobic = *Clostridium*** (*C. tetani*, *C. botulinum*, *C. perfringens*, *C. difficile*).
2. **No spores:**
   - ***Listeria*** (tumbling motility, grows in the fridge, intracellular);
   - ***Corynebacterium diphtheriae*** (club-shaped, phage-encoded toxin).
3. **Branching filaments:**
   - ***Actinomyces*** = anaerobic, **not** acid-fast, sulfur granules, **penicillin**;
   - ***Nocardia*** = aerobic, **partially** acid-fast, **TMP-SMX**. "Treatment is a SNAP": Sulfonamides for *Nocardia*, *Actinomyces* gets Penicillin (p. 87).

## Core mechanism: four toxin patterns
- **Two-part (A-B) toxins:** a B part binds the cell, an A part does the damage inside (pp. 64, 82).
  - **Anthrax:** PA is the B part. It carries EF and LF into the cell. **EF is a calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase** that raises cAMP, causing edema and weak neutrophils and macrophages (pp. 64-65), like the pertussis adenylate cyclase toxin.
  - **Diphtheria:** the B part binds the **HB-EGF receptor** on heart and nerve cells. The A part **ADP-ribosylates EF-2** and stops protein synthesis (p. 82).
- **Proteases that cut SNARE proteins** (p. 68):
  - **tetanus toxin** travels **backward up the motor axon** to the spinal cord and blocks release of **glycine and GABA** from inhibitory interneurons (Renshaw cells). Result: **spastic** paralysis (p. 68);
  - **botulinum toxin** stays at the **neuromuscular junction** and blocks **ACh** release. Result: **flaccid** paralysis (p. 71).
- **Membrane-splitting enzyme:** *C. perfringens* **alpha toxin = phospholipase C (lecithinase)**. It splits membrane lecithin and damages red cells, platelets, white cells and endothelial cells, causing hemolysis and tissue death (p. 74).
- **Cytoskeleton toxins:** *C. difficile* **toxin A (enterotoxin)** and **toxin B (cytotoxin)** disrupt actin and tight junctions in colon cells (pp. 75-76).

## Bacillus anthracis (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 63-66)
- **Lab:** aerobic spore-forming rods in long chains ("medusa head"). The capsule is **protein (poly-D-glutamate)**, not sugar, and blocks phagocytosis (pp. 63-64).
- **Spread:** contact with livestock, hides, wool or hair. **No person-to-person spread.** A case without animal exposure should raise concern for **bioterrorism** and be reported (pp. 64-65).
- **Forms** (pp. 65-66):
  - **cutaneous** (most common): papule → vesicle → **painless black eschar** with marked **edema** around it;
  - **inhalational** ("woolsorters' disease", about 5% of cases): spores are carried to **mediastinal lymph nodes**, causing **hemorrhagic mediastinitis**. Chest X-ray shows a **widened mediastinum**, with bloody pleural effusions and shock;
  - **gastrointestinal:** from infected meat (rare).
- **Mortality:**
  - Your notes say pulmonary anthrax has "near 100% mortality" (p. 64).
  - **Other source:** MSD gives **90%** for untreated inhalation anthrax and **20%** for untreated cutaneous anthrax; the CDC's 2023 anthrax guideline gives mortality with adequate treatment of **under 2% for cutaneous**, about **45% for inhalation** and **over 90% for anthrax meningitis**.
- **Treatment:**
  - Pharm HY lists anthrax among the "first line" uses of penicillin (PDF 120).
  - **Other source (CDC 2023 guideline) for systemic anthrax (inhalation and other non-cutaneous forms):**
    - **meningitis excluded or unlikely:** **1 bactericidal drug plus 1 protein synthesis inhibitor**. Bactericidal drugs: a **fluoroquinolone** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), a carbapenem, or penicillin G/ampicillin if the strain is susceptible. Protein synthesis inhibitors named by MSD: clindamycin, doxycycline and minocycline;
    - **meningitis possible or confirmed:** **2 bactericidal drugs from different classes plus a protein synthesis inhibitor or rifampin** (an RNA synthesis inhibitor). MSD prefers **meropenem** when meningitis is suspected;
    - MSD's own summary starts every inhalation or systemic case on this **3-drug** regimen;
    - IV therapy for at least 2 weeks, then oral therapy to complete **60 days** from illness onset.
  - **Antitoxin** is added for all non-cutaneous systemic anthrax (CDC 2023). The monoclonal antitoxins (**raxibacumab, obiltoxaximab**) **block PA binding** (MSD).
  - **PEP** after inhaled exposure: oral **doxycycline, ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin for 60 days**, plus the vaccine series (MSD).

## Bacillus cereus (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 66-67)
- Spores survive cooking. Classic food: **reheated fried rice** (p. 66).
- **Two forms** (pp. 66-67):
  - **emetic:** a **preformed, heat-stable** toxin. **Vomiting and cramps 1-6 hours** after eating, much like staph food poisoning. Linked to **rice**;
  - **diarrheal:** a **heat-labile** toxin made in the gut that raises cAMP. **Cramps and watery diarrhea 8-16 hours** after eating, much like *C. perfringens*. Linked to **meat dishes**. (Your notes also say "18 hours" on p. 66.)
- Treatment is supportive (p. 67).

## Clostridium tetani: tetanus (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 67-70)
- **Spread:** spores enter **puncture wounds and dirty lacerations**. Dead tissue gives the low-oxygen setting the spores need. In poorer countries, tetanus also follows **unclean umbilical cord care** (newborns) and retained placenta (mothers). **Swallowed toxin does no harm** (p. 67).
- **Mechanism** (p. 68): toxin binds the motor nerve ending, travels **backward up the axon** to the spinal cord and brainstem, and blocks **glycine and GABA** release from inhibitory interneurons. Once bound, it **cannot be neutralized** by antitoxin.
- **Signs** (p. 68):
  - **trismus** (lockjaw);
  - **risus sardonicus** (fixed grin);
  - **opisthotonos** (arched back);
  - painful spasms set off by noise or touch;
  - death from respiratory muscle spasm.
- **Your notes' treatment** (p. 69): antitoxin ± vaccine booster, antibiotics, **diazepam** for spasms and **wound debridement**.
- **Other source (treatment):**
  - CDC: **TIG 500 units once**, wound debridement, sedation and muscle relaxants; vaccinate once stable.
  - MSD: "**Metronidazole** is the recommended antibiotic". **Benzodiazepines** are the standard of care for spasms. Watch for **autonomic instability** (high blood pressure, fast heart rate).
  - **Tetanus disease does not give immunity** (CDC, MSD). Every survivor needs vaccination.
- **Vaccine:** a **toxoid** (formalin-inactivated toxin). Antibodies from an immunized mother cross the placenta and protect the newborn (p. 70).
- **Correction (vaccine schedule):** your notes list the series as 2, 4, 6 and 15-18 months and **10-12 years**, then every 10 years (p. 70). The CDC schedule is:
  - **DTaP at 2, 4 and 6 months, 15-18 months and 4-6 years** (5 doses);
  - **Tdap once at 11-12 years**;
  - then **Td or Tdap every 10 years**.
- **Other source (wounds)**, CDC wound management:
  - **clean, minor wound:** vaccine if the last dose was **10 or more years** ago. **TIG is never needed** for clean, minor wounds;
  - **dirty or major wound:** vaccine if the last dose was **5 or more years** ago. Add **TIG** only if the primary series is **unknown or incomplete**, or the patient has HIV or severe immunodeficiency;
  - unknown or incomplete history: vaccinate for any wound.

## Clostridium botulinum: botulism (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 70-73)
Guide 26 compares botulism with myasthenia gravis and Guillain-Barré and covers infant botulism; this section adds microbiology and treatment.
- **Adults swallow preformed toxin;** infants swallow **spores** (honey) that grow and make toxin in the gut (p. 70).
- **Food:** home-canned vegetables (**green beans, mushrooms**), smoked or salted fish (p. 71).
- **Mechanism:** blocks **ACh release** at the neuromuscular junction (p. 71). Types **A, B and E** cause human disease; type A is the most potent poison known (p. 70).
- **Signs:**
  - cranial nerves first ("**4 D's**": diplopia, dysphagia, dysphonia, dyspnea), then **descending, symmetric, flaccid** paralysis. Little vomiting, **no fever**; death from respiratory failure (p. 72);
  - MSD adds: **no sensory loss**, clear mind, and **weak or absent pupil light reflex**.
- **Wound botulism (MSD):** people who inject **black tar heroin** under the skin or into muscle ("skin popping"). Treated with debridement and penicillin or metronidazole, plus antitoxin.
- **Correction (antitoxin timing):** your notes say antitoxin "is only effective ... within 12 hours after ingestion" (p. 72).
  - The CDC says to request antitoxin **immediately** and **not wait** for lab confirmation. Antitoxin does **not reverse** existing paralysis but **stops its progression** by neutralizing toxin still in the blood.
  - MSD: give it "as soon as possible after clinical diagnosis". It is "less likely to be of benefit if given **> 72 hours after symptom onset**".
  - So a patient seen on day 2 still gets antitoxin.
- **Infant botulism** (p. 72): constipation, weak suck, "floppy baby"; almost all recover.
  - **Other source:** MSD gives ages **2 weeks to 12 months** (most under 6 months; your notes say up to 6 months). Treatment is **BabyBIG** (human botulinum immune globulin). **Antibiotics are avoided**, because killing bacteria in the gut may release more toxin.
- **Medical botulinum toxin** (p. 73): dystonia (for example torticollis), achalasia, muscle spasms in multiple sclerosis and Parkinson disease, and wrinkles. The effect lasts about 3 months while the nerve ending regrows. Osmosis lists botulinum toxin for diffuse esophageal spasm too (p. 221).

## Clostridium perfringens and C. septicum (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 73-75)
- **Alpha toxin = phospholipase C = lecithinase.** It splits membrane phospholipids, causing hemolysis, tissue death and edema (p. 74).
- **Gas gangrene (myonecrosis)** (p. 74):
  - about 95% of cases are *C. perfringens*;
  - follows a **penetrating wound contaminated with soil**, where spores germinate in dead, low-oxygen tissue;
  - **sudden severe pain**, fever, **gas in tissue (crepitus, gas on X-ray)**, hemorrhagic bullae, dusky skin.
- **Other source (gas gangrene, MSD):**
  - skin turns **bronze**, then blackish green; crepitation is felt in about 80%;
  - treatment = **penicillin plus clindamycin** with **urgent surgical debridement**; hyperbaric oxygen may help in extensive disease;
  - mortality is **40% or more with treatment** and 100% without.
- **Food poisoning** (p. 75): **large numbers of spores** are eaten. They germinate in the gut and release an enterotoxin, so **watery diarrhea starts late**. It resolves in under 24 hours (p. 74). Compare the early onset of staph and *B. cereus* emetic toxin, which are preformed.
  - **Other source (MSD):** outbreaks follow **cooked meat held warm** (room temperature up to 60°C, as on a warming table), usually in commercial establishments and rarely at home. Symptoms start **6-24 hours** after eating; vomiting and fever are unusual. MSD calls it the most frequent clostridial infection, typically from type A.
- ***C. septicum*** (p. 75):
  - gut commensal that enters blood through a **break in the colon** and causes **spontaneous (non-traumatic) gas gangrene**;
  - most cases have an underlying **colon cancer**, so look for it (your notes). MSD agrees: spontaneous cases usually come from occult colon perforation in **colon cancer**, and **neutropenia** raises the risk.

## Clostridioides (Clostridium) difficile (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 75-77)
- **Name:** MSD and the IDSA now use ***Clostridioides difficile***. Your notes use *Clostridium*. Both names refer to the same organism.
- **Cause:** antibiotics wipe out normal colon flora. *C. difficile* overgrows and releases **toxin A** (enterotoxin, fluid) and **toxin B** (cytotoxin, necrosis) (pp. 75-76).
- **Antibiotics most often blamed:**
  - your notes: **clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, penicillin and broad-spectrum cephalosporins** (p. 76);
  - Pharm HY: "most commonly" **3rd-generation cephalosporins and clindamycin** (PDF 122); a past question there gives **aminopenicillins** (PDF 123);
  - **Other source:** MSD says most antibiotics can cause it. It lists the highest risk as **cephalosporins (especially 3rd generation), penicillins (especially ampicillin and amoxicillin), clindamycin and fluoroquinolones**. On exams, **clindamycin** is the classic answer.
- **Pathology:** yellow-white **pseudomembranes** of neutrophils, fibrin and dead epithelium on colonoscopy (p. 76). Osmosis lists *C. difficile* colitis as a cause of protein-losing enteropathy (p. 259).
- **Diagnosis:** your notes call **PCR for the toxin A and B genes** the best test (p. 76).
  - **Other source:** MSD adds that the toxin-gene test "often remains positive after successful treatment", so it is not a test of cure.
- **Complication:** **toxic megacolon** with risk of perforation (p. 76).
- **Recent change (treatment):** your notes say "metronidazole or oral vancomycin, or fidaxomicin" (p. 76). The IDSA/SHEA 2021 update (via AFP 2022):
  - **first episode:** **oral fidaxomicin 200 mg twice daily for 10 days is preferred**; **oral vancomycin 125 mg four times daily for 10 days** is the alternative;
  - **metronidazole** only for **nonsevere** disease when neither drug is available;
  - **fulminant disease** (MSD): **oral (or nasogastric) vancomycin 500 mg four times daily plus IV metronidazole**;
  - **recurrence:** fidaxomicin; **FMT** after multiple recurrences. **Bezlotoxumab** was added for recurrence in the 2021 update, but MSD now says it "is no longer manufactured". MSD also lists newer licensed **fecal microbiota products** (oral capsules and a rectal suspension).
- **Infection control** (p. 77): **gown and gloves**, dedicated equipment, and **soap and water**, because alcohol hand gel does not kill spores. MSD adds that rooms need **spore-killing (sporicidal) disinfectants**.
- **Correction (Pharm HY, PDF 122):**
  - the page heading "Pseudomonas Colitis" should read **pseudomembranous colitis**. Its cause, as the same page says, is *C. difficile*;
  - the drug list "Metronidazole, Vancomycin, **Rifampicin**, Fidaxomicin" and the "Rifampicin uses" (IBS, traveller's diarrhoea, hepatic encephalopathy) describe **rifaximin**, not rifampicin. The rifaximin label lists **travelers' diarrhea from noninvasive *E. coli***, **lowering the risk of hepatic encephalopathy coming back**, and **IBS with diarrhea**. *C. difficile* is **not** a labeled use; it appears only as a warning.
- Pharm HY: oral vancomycin is taken by mouth for pseudomembranous colitis; IV vancomycin is the drug of choice for MRSA (PDF 121).

## Listeria monocytogenes (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 78-80)
- **Lab** (p. 78): gram-positive rod, **no spores**, **beta-hemolytic**. **Tumbling motility at 22°C** (not at 37°C). Grows at **4°C** (**cold enrichment**).
- **Food** (p. 78): **soft cheeses, deli meats, hot dogs, unpasteurized milk**, coleslaw and raw vegetables. It grows in the fridge.
- **How it survives** (pp. 78-79):
  - **listeriolysin O** lets it **escape the phagosome into the cytoplasm**;
  - it then builds **actin "rocket tails"** and pushes directly into the next cell, so it never meets antibody or complement;
  - so clearance depends on **cell-mediated immunity** (activated macrophages, interferon-gamma).
- **Who gets sick** (pp. 79-80):
  - **pregnant women:** flu-like illness, amnionitis, miscarriage; crosses the placenta;
  - **newborns:** sepsis and **meningitis**. In utero infection can cause **granulomatosis infantiseptica**: widespread small granulomas with central necrosis and high mortality;
  - **older and immunocompromised adults:** bacteremia and meningitis. Your notes call it the most common cause of meningitis in kidney transplant patients and adults with cancer (p. 80). Osmosis also lists it as a bacterial cause of encephalitis (p. 505);
  - healthy adults: mild gastroenteritis or none.
- **Other source:** the CDC names **pregnant women, newborns, adults 65 and older and people with weak immune systems** as higher risk. It says **1 in 4** pregnant women who get listeriosis **lose the pregnancy or the baby** shortly after birth.
- **Treatment:** **ampicillin**. **Cephalosporins do not work** (p. 80). MSD agrees ("should not be used") and adds that **gentamicin** is commonly given with ampicillin. Meningitis is treated for at least 21 days.

## Corynebacterium diphtheriae: diphtheria (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 81-84)
- **Lab** (p. 81):
  - club-shaped rods in **V or L shapes**;
  - **gray-black colonies on cysteine-tellurite agar**;
  - **metachromatic granules** on **Loeffler** medium;
  - **Elek test** shows whether a strain makes toxin.
- **Toxin needs two things** (pp. 81-82):
  1. a **lysogenic beta-prophage** carrying the *tox* gene (**lysogenic conversion**). A harmless strain can become toxigenic after phage infection;
  2. **low iron**: an iron-activated repressor turns the toxin gene off.
- **Mechanism:** B part binds **HB-EGF receptor** (heart and nerve cells); A part **ADP-ribosylates EF-2** and stops protein synthesis (p. 82).
- **Disease** (pp. 82-83):
  - **gray pseudomembrane** on tonsils, pharynx or larynx that **bleeds when scraped off** and can block the airway;
  - **bull neck** from swollen cervical nodes;
  - toxin in the blood causes **myocarditis and arrhythmias** and **nerve palsies**;
  - think of it in an **unvaccinated** patient back from an area where diphtheria is still common.
- Osmosis lists *C. diphtheriae* as a historical cause of croup (p. 883).
- **Vaccine:** the toxoid makes antibodies against the **B part**, blocking binding (p. 83).
  - **Other source:** your notes say "DPT". US children now get **DTaP** (acellular pertussis), then Tdap and Td boosters (CDC).
- **Treatment, in order of importance** (p. 84):
  1. **antitoxin** (neutralizes toxin not yet inside cells; give it fast);
  2. **penicillin or erythromycin** (stops toxin release and spread);
  3. vaccine.
- **Other source:** the CDC agrees: **DAT for respiratory diphtheria** (from the CDC in the US), and "only erythromycin or penicillin" as antibiotics. It notes penicillin resistance in several regions. Diphtheria "might not confer immunity", so survivors are vaccinated. Close contacts get cultures, prophylaxis (erythromycin or penicillin, or one IM dose of benzathine penicillin) and vaccination if not up to date.

## Actinomyces israelii (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 85-86)
- **Lab:** **anaerobic**, gram-positive **branching** rods, **not acid-fast**. **Sulfur granules** are yellow clumps of bacteria (no actual sulfur) (p. 85).
- **Where:** normal flora of the **gums** and female genital tract (p. 86).
- **Disease** (p. 86):
  - **cervicofacial** ("lumpy jaw"): a slow jaw or neck mass after **dental work or oral trauma**, with **sinus tracts draining yellow granules**;
  - **lung** disease after aspiration (mimics abscess, cancer or TB);
  - **pelvic disease with an IUD**.
- **Treatment:** **long course of penicillin** plus surgical drainage (p. 86).
  - **Other source:** MSD: **high-dose IV penicillin G**, then oral penicillin or amoxicillin, for **at least 8 weeks** (sometimes a year or more). Remove the IUD in pelvic disease.

## Nocardia (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 86-87; NBME TC p. 299)
- **Lab:** **obligate aerobe**, gram-positive **branching** rods, **partially (weakly) acid-fast** (p. 86). **Urease-positive** (NBME TC p. 299).
- **Disease** (p. 87):
  - ***N. asteroides*:** **lung** infection with cavities in **immunocompromised** patients (steroids, transplant, cancer). It **mimics TB, but the PPD is negative**. Spreads to the **brain** (abscess, seizures);
  - ***N. brasiliensis*:** skin infection after trauma; **mycetoma** with draining granules.
- **Treatment:** **TMP-SMX** (high-dose sulfonamides) (p. 87).
  - **Other source:** MSD agrees (TMP-SMX first-line). In severe or disseminated disease it adds **amikacin, imipenem or meropenem**. Brain abscess occurs in about 37% of cases, and **up to 40%** of patients have no known immune problem.

## Clinical vignette
At a family dinner, six guests eat fried rice that was cooked the day before, left on the counter overnight and briefly reheated. Within 2 hours, four of them have nausea, repeated vomiting and cramps. None has a fever. Two other guests skipped the rice but ate a beef dish; they develop cramps and watery diarrhea about 12 hours later.

- **Diagnosis:** ***Bacillus cereus*** food poisoning, both forms (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 66-67).
- **Rice group (emetic form):** spores survive cooking, germinate as the rice cools, and make a **preformed, heat-stable** toxin. Reheating does not destroy it, so vomiting starts **1-6 hours** after eating (pp. 66-67).
- **Beef group (diarrheal form):** bacteria grow in the gut and make a **heat-labile** toxin that raises cAMP. Diarrhea starts **8-16 hours** after eating (p. 67).
- **Treatment:** supportive; antibiotics do not act on the toxins (p. 67).
- **Look-alikes:** staph food poisoning (also fast vomiting, but mayonnaise salads and pastries, guide 62) and *C. perfringens* (also late diarrhea, from spores in cooked meat held warm; p. 75; MSD).

## Common traps
- **Spores: autoclave, not boiling** (p. 63). Alcohol gel does not kill *C. difficile* spores; use soap and water (p. 77).
- **Tetanus = spastic** (inhibitory neurons blocked); **botulism = flaccid** (ACh blocked) (pp. 68, 71).
- **Botulism antitoxin is not limited to 12 hours.** Give it as soon as botulism is suspected (CDC; MSD).
- **Tetanus does not make you immune.** Vaccinate after recovery (CDC).
- **TIG only for dirty or major wounds in patients with an unknown or incomplete series** (or HIV or severe immunodeficiency) (CDC).
- **The childhood DTaP series has a 4-6 year dose;** Tdap comes at 11-12 years (CDC).
- **Metronidazole is no longer first-line for *C. difficile*.** Fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin (IDSA 2021). Oral, not IV, vancomycin.
- **Rifaximin, not rifampicin,** is the gut-only drug for travelers' diarrhea, hepatic encephalopathy and IBS with diarrhea (label).
- ***Listeria* = ampicillin;** cephalosporins miss it (p. 80; MSD).
- **Diphtheria: antitoxin first,** then antibiotics; don't wait for the culture or Elek test (p. 84; CDC).
- **Spontaneous gas gangrene with no wound → *C. septicum* → look for colon cancer** (p. 75; MSD).
- **Actinomyces vs Nocardia:** anaerobe, not acid-fast, penicillin vs aerobe, partially acid-fast, TMP-SMX ("SNAP", p. 87).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 46-year-old man who makes drums from imported goat hides has a painless lesion on his forearm. It began as an itchy bump 5 days ago and is now a black, depressed eschar with marked surrounding swelling. Gram stain of fluid from the edge shows large gram-positive rods in chains. Which toxin component is required for the other toxin components to enter host cells?
   - **Answer:** **protective antigen.**
   - **Why:** this is cutaneous anthrax. PA is the binding (B) part; it carries edema factor and lethal factor into the cell, and neither works without it (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 64-65). The monoclonal antitoxins work by blocking PA binding (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **edema factor**. It is the calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase that causes the swelling, but it needs PA to get in (p. 65).

2. A 52-year-old mail sorter has 2 days of fever, muscle aches and dry cough, followed by severe shortness of breath and hypotension. Chest X-ray shows a widened mediastinum and bilateral pleural effusions. He is alert, with no headache or neck stiffness, and CSF is normal. Blood cultures grow gram-positive rods in long chains with a protein capsule. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **IV ciprofloxacin plus clindamycin (a protein synthesis inhibitor), with an anthrax antitoxin.**
   - **Why:** this is inhalation anthrax with hemorrhagic mediastinitis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 66). With meningitis excluded, the CDC's 2023 guideline uses 1 bactericidal drug plus 1 protein synthesis inhibitor, with antitoxin for all non-cutaneous systemic anthrax. If meningitis were possible, the regimen would add a second bactericidal drug (CDC 2023; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **oral doxycycline alone for 60 days**. That is post-exposure prophylaxis for people who are exposed but well, not treatment of a sick patient (MSD).

3. Twenty people develop crampy abdominal pain and watery diarrhea 10-14 hours after a banquet. None vomits, and all recover within a day. The main dish was a large pot of beef gravy that was cooked the day before and kept warm on a steam table. Which mechanism best explains the delay between eating and symptoms?
   - **Answer:** **ingested spores germinate in the intestine and then release enterotoxin.**
   - **Why:** *C. perfringens* food poisoning needs large numbers of spores. They germinate in the gut, so onset is late (El-Husseiny Micro p. 75). It is common and self-limited (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **a preformed heat-stable toxin in the food**. That causes vomiting within hours (staph, *B. cereus* emetic form) (p. 75).

4. A 64-year-old gardener who has not had a vaccine since childhood stepped on a rusty nail 9 days ago. He now cannot open his jaw, has a fixed grimace and has painful back-arching spasms when a door slams. Which effect of the responsible toxin best explains these findings?
   - **Answer:** **blocked release of glycine and GABA from inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord.**
   - **Why:** tetanus toxin travels backward up the motor axon and cleaves SNARE proteins in inhibitory neurons (Renshaw cells). Motor neurons fire unchecked, causing spastic paralysis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 68).
   - **Trap:** **blocked release of ACh at the neuromuscular junction**. That is botulinum toxin, which causes flaccid paralysis (p. 71).

5. A 34-year-old woman falls from her bike and gets a deep thigh laceration contaminated with soil. She completed her childhood vaccines and received Tdap 7 years ago. She has no immune disorder. Which tetanus prevention is most appropriate after wound cleaning?
   - **Answer:** **a tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine (Td or Tdap) without TIG.**
   - **Why:** for a dirty wound, the CDC advises vaccine if the last dose was 5 or more years ago. TIG is only for an unknown or incomplete series, HIV or severe immunodeficiency (CDC wound management).
   - **Trap:** **vaccine plus TIG**. She has a complete series, so passive antibody is not needed.

6. A 47-year-old woman has double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech and difficulty swallowing that began 36 hours ago and are now spreading to her arms. Pupils react sluggishly, sensation is normal and she has no fever. Three days ago she ate home-canned green beans. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **give botulism antitoxin now, without waiting for toxin test results.**
   - **Why:** antitoxin neutralizes toxin still in the blood and stops progression; it does not reverse paralysis already present. The CDC says not to wait for laboratory confirmation. MSD says antitoxin is "less likely to be of benefit" only when given **more than 72 hours** after symptoms begin.
   - **Trap:** **supportive care only because more than 12 hours have passed since eating**. That is the 12-hour cutoff in your notes (p. 72), which current CDC and MSD advice does not use.

7. A 25-year-old man has an open tibial fracture after a farm machinery accident. Thirty hours later he has sudden severe leg pain, fever and hypotension. The skin over the calf is bronze with hemorrhagic bullae, and gas is felt under the skin. Which toxin activity is mainly responsible for the tissue destruction?
   - **Answer:** **phospholipase C (lecithinase) that splits membrane phospholipids.**
   - **Why:** *C. perfringens* alpha toxin damages the membranes of red cells, platelets, white cells and endothelial cells, causing hemolysis, tissue death and loss of blood supply (El-Husseiny Micro p. 74). Gas gangrene needs urgent debridement plus penicillin and clindamycin (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **a superantigen that activates many T cells at once**. That drives toxic shock from staph or group A strep, not clostridial myonecrosis (guide 62).

8. A 71-year-old man has sudden severe pain and swelling of his left thigh, with crepitus, but no wound or injury. He has had 3 months of fatigue, and his hemoglobin is 8.9 g/dL with a low MCV. Blood cultures grow an anaerobic, spore-forming gram-positive rod. After surgery and antibiotics, which test is most important?
   - **Answer:** **colonoscopy.**
   - **Why:** spontaneous gas gangrene is usually from *C. septicum*, which enters the blood through a colon lesion, most often cancer (El-Husseiny Micro p. 75; MSD). Iron deficiency anemia in an older man also points to colon cancer.
   - **Trap:** **echocardiography for endocarditis**. Nothing here suggests a valve infection; the source is the colon.

9. A 76-year-old woman develops 7 watery stools a day, cramping and a temperature of 38.0°C, 1 week after finishing clindamycin for a dental abscess. White cell count is 13,000/mm³ and creatinine is normal. Stool tests confirm toxin-producing *C. difficile*. Which treatment is preferred?
   - **Answer:** **oral fidaxomicin for 10 days.**
   - **Why:** the IDSA/SHEA 2021 update prefers fidaxomicin for a first episode, with oral vancomycin 125 mg four times daily as the alternative (AFP 2022). Clindamycin is a classic trigger (El-Husseiny Micro p. 76).
   - **Trap:** **oral metronidazole**. It is now used only for nonsevere disease when fidaxomicin and vancomycin are unavailable (IDSA 2021). Vancomycin for this infection is given by mouth (Pharm HY PDF 121); IV vancomycin does not reach the colon lumen (standard teaching).

10. A 30-year-old woman at 29 weeks' gestation has fever and muscle aches a week after eating unpasteurized soft cheese. Blood cultures grow beta-hemolytic gram-positive rods that tumble when viewed at room temperature. Inside cultured macrophages, the organism leaves the phagosome and moves from cell to cell on actin tails. Which bacterial product allows escape from the phagosome?
   - **Answer:** **listeriolysin O.**
   - **Why:** this pore-forming hemolysin frees *Listeria* into the cytoplasm, where it uses actin polymerization to spread without meeting antibody (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 78-79). Treatment is ampicillin, since cephalosporins do not work (p. 80; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **blocking phagosome-lysosome fusion**. That is how *M. tuberculosis* and *Salmonella* survive; *Listeria* escapes instead (p. 79).

11. A 7-year-old unvaccinated boy who arrived from a refugee camp 5 days ago has fever, sore throat and a swollen "bull" neck. A gray membrane covers his tonsils and bleeds when the edge is lifted. He has mild stridor. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **give diphtheria antitoxin promptly, with penicillin or erythromycin, before culture results.**
   - **Why:** antitoxin neutralizes toxin that has not yet entered heart or nerve cells, so speed matters most (El-Husseiny Micro p. 84). The CDC recommends starting presumptive treatment before confirmation.
   - **Trap:** **wait for an Elek test to confirm toxin production**. The delay lets toxin reach the heart and nerves.

12. In a laboratory, a nontoxigenic *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* strain is exposed to a filtrate from a toxigenic strain. Some colonies of the formerly harmless strain now produce diphtheria toxin, but only in low-iron medium. Which process best explains how they gained the toxin gene?
   - **Answer:** **lysogenic conversion by a bacteriophage carrying the *tox* gene.**
   - **Why:** the beta-corynephage integrates as a prophage. Toxin is expressed only when iron is low, because an iron-activated repressor blocks the gene (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 81-83).
   - **Trap:** **transformation by free DNA**. The filtrate passes phage, and the gene stays on the prophage.

13. A 38-year-old man has a firm, slowly growing swelling at the angle of his jaw 2 months after a tooth extraction. The mass has opened onto the skin and drains pus containing yellow granules. Gram stain shows branching gram-positive filaments; an acid-fast stain is negative, and the organism grows only without oxygen. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **a long course of high-dose penicillin, with drainage.**
   - **Why:** this is cervicofacial actinomycosis (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 85-86). MSD gives IV penicillin G, then oral penicillin or amoxicillin, for at least 8 weeks.
   - **Trap:** **TMP-SMX**. That treats *Nocardia*, which is aerobic and partially acid-fast (p. 87).

14. A 58-year-old man who received a kidney transplant 1 year ago takes prednisone and tacrolimus. He has a 3-week cough and fever, a cavitary lung lesion and new right-arm weakness. MRI shows a brain abscess. Sputum shows branching gram-positive rods that are partially acid-fast. A tuberculin skin test is negative. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **TMP-SMX, combined with another agent such as imipenem or amikacin for brain involvement.**
   - **Why:** *Nocardia* causes cavitary lung disease in immunocompromised patients and spreads to the brain (El-Husseiny Micro p. 87). MSD gives TMP-SMX as first-line, combined with amikacin, imipenem or meropenem in severe or disseminated disease.
   - **Trap:** **rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol**. TB is also a cavitary, acid-fast lung disease, but the PPD is negative and the rods branch (p. 87).

15. A 44-year-old man recovers from generalized tetanus after 3 weeks in intensive care. He never completed childhood vaccination. Before discharge, he asks if the illness has made him immune. Which is the most appropriate recommendation?
   - **Answer:** **begin a full tetanus toxoid vaccine series.**
   - **Why:** tetanus disease does not give immunity, so survivors are vaccinated (CDC; MSD). The usual explanation is that the amount of toxin that causes disease is too small to trigger protective antibody (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **no vaccine, because natural infection gives lasting immunity**. That is true for measles, not tetanus.
