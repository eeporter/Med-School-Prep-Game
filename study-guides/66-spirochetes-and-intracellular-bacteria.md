## In one line
**Spirochetes are corkscrews that are too thin to Gram stain** (*Treponema*, *Leptospira*) **or big enough for Giemsa** (*Borrelia*). **Chlamydia and Rickettsia are obligate intracellular** and are treated with doxycycline or a macrolide. **Mycoplasma has no cell wall**, so beta-lactams never work (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 152, 162, 171).

## Core mechanism: why these bugs behave differently
| Group | Cell wall | Lives where | Can you see it? | Key drug |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Spirochetes** | Gram-negative envelope, endoflagella under the outer sheath | Not obligate intracellular (*Leptospira* can be cultured, p. 158) | Darkfield (*Treponema*, *Leptospira*); Giemsa or Wright (*Borrelia*) | Penicillin (syphilis); doxycycline (Lyme) |
| **Chlamydiaceae** | Peptidoglycan lacks muramic acid | **Obligate intracellular; make no ATP** | Not on Gram stain; inclusions on Giemsa | Doxycycline, azithromycin |
| **Rickettsiaceae** | Normal peptidoglycan | **Obligate intracellular; limited ATP** | Too small to Gram stain well | Doxycycline |
| **Mycoplasma** | **None** (sterols in the membrane) | Outside cells | Not on Gram stain; Giemsa | Macrolide, doxycycline, fluoroquinolone |

Sources: El-Husseiny Micro pp. 152-153 (spirochetes), p. 162 (comparison table), p. 169 and p. 171 (*Mycoplasma*).

- **Why beta-lactams fail:** beta-lactams block cell wall building. *Mycoplasma* has no wall, and the chlamydial wall is unusual, so penicillins and cephalosporins do not work (pp. 162, 169, 171). CDC: all mycoplasmas "are inherently resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics".
- **Chlamydial life cycle** (pp. 162-163):
  - the **elementary body (EB)** is the small, inert, **infectious** form ("**E**nfectious"); it enters the cell by phagocytosis;
  - inside, it becomes a **reticulate body (RB)**, which **replicates** by binary fission ("**R**eplicates");
  - RBs turn back into EBs, the cell bursts, and EBs infect new cells. The growing colony is the **inclusion body** seen on a stained smear.
- **Correction (*Borrelia* is not obligate intracellular):** NBME TC p. 249 calls *Borrelia burgdorferi* an "obligate intracellular spirochete". Obligate intracellular bacteria cannot grow outside host cells. *Borrelia* grows in cell-free laboratory media (BSK-H and MKP), including from erythema migrans skin biopsies (Ružić-Sabljić et al., *PLOS ONE* 2017). Your El-Husseiny notes list only the Chlamydiaceae and Rickettsiaceae as obligate intracellular (p. 162) and call *Borrelia* "difficult to culture" (p. 159).

## Treponema pallidum: syphilis (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 153-157)
- **Spread** (p. 153): sex; across the placenta; fresh blood. It dies in stored blood within 3-5 days at 4°C.
- **Damage comes from endarteritis** (inflamed small arteries) and the disease tends to become chronic (p. 153).
- **Primary** (pp. 153-154): a single **hard, painless ulcer (chancre)** with enlarged regional nodes; it heals in 4-6 weeks even without treatment. Darkfield or fluorescent microscopy of chancre fluid shows the organism.
  - **Other source (MSD):** the chancre appears after about **3-4 weeks** (range 1-13) and heals in 3-12 weeks.
- **Secondary = systemic** (p. 154): fever, fatigue, **generalized nontender lymphadenopathy**, a **maculopapular rash including the palms and soles**, and **condylomata lata** (smooth, moist, wart-like genital lesions).
  - **Other source (MSD):** secondary syphilis starts **6-12 weeks** after the chancre appears.
- **Latent** (p. 155): no symptoms, positive serology. MSD: **early latent** is under 1 year from infection, **late latent** 1 year or more.
- **Tertiary** (p. 155):
  - **gummas** (chronic granulomas) in organs and bone;
  - **aortitis** from destruction of the **vasa vasorum**, causing a thoracic aneurysm (Osmosis p. 15: "endarteritis obliterans" of the vasa vasorum);
  - **neurosyphilis**: **tabes dorsalis** (broad-based ataxia, positive Romberg, Charcot joints), general paresis, and **Argyll Robertson pupils** (constrict to near focus, not to light; guide 27);
  - **Other source (MSD):** about **one-third** of untreated people develop late syphilis (your notes say about 40%). Cardiovascular disease usually appears 10-25 years after infection, tabes dorsalis 20-30 years.
- **Congenital** (p. 155): stillbirth or death soon after birth, or later signs: **rhagades** (scars at the mouth corners), **snuffles**, **saddle nose**, **Hutchinson teeth**, **mulberry molars**, **saber shins**, **CN VIII deafness**. Treat the mother early in pregnancy.
  - **Other source (MSD congenital syphilis, Apr 2026):** untreated primary or secondary syphilis in the mother "usually is transmitted"; latent or tertiary syphilis less often (7-40%); risk rises in the second half of pregnancy. The **Hutchinson triad** (interstitial keratitis, Hutchinson incisors, eighth-nerve deafness) "is diagnostic". Treating the mother **less than 4 weeks before delivery** may not cure the fetus. CDC: test at the **first prenatal visit**, and retest some people early in the third trimester and at delivery.

### Tests (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 156-157)
| | **Nontreponemal** (RPR, VDRL) | **Treponemal** (FTA-ABS, MHA-TP) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Detects | Antibodies to **cardiolipin**, a host lipid released by damaged cells | Antibodies to *T. pallidum* itself |
| Use | **Screening**; titers **follow treatment** | **Confirm** a positive screen |
| After cure | Falls | **Stays positive for life** (your notes) |
| False positives | **Pregnancy, viral infection (mono, hepatitis), drugs, rheumatic fever, lupus and leprosy** | Rare |

- Both can be **negative early**, because antibodies take about 4 weeks; treponemal tests turn positive sooner (p. 157).
- NBME TC p. 389: **antiphospholipid antibodies** in lupus can give a **false-positive syphilis test**.
- **Neurosyphilis:** test the CSF with VDRL, FTA-ABS and PCR (p. 155).
- **Other source (MSD):** many labs now **reverse the order**, screening with a treponemal immunoassay first. Treponemal tests "usually remain positive for decades" and cannot show treatment response. Other false-positive RPR causes include tuberculosis, endocarditis and antiphospholipid syndrome.

### Treatment
- **Your notes** (p. 157): **benzathine penicillin** (long-acting) for primary and secondary syphilis, with no penicillin resistance. Pharm HY lists penicillin as first choice for syphilis (PDF 120).
- **Correction (wording):** your notes say "penicillin G for congenital and late syphilis" (p. 157). The form and schedule depend on the stage (MSD, following the CDC):
  - **primary, secondary, early latent:** benzathine penicillin G **2.4 million units IM once**;
  - **late latent, unknown duration, or tertiary without nerve involvement:** benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM **weekly for 3 doses**;
  - **neurosyphilis (and eye or ear syphilis):** **IV aqueous penicillin G** every 4 hours for **10-14 days**;
  - **congenital syphilis:** aqueous penicillin G IV or procaine penicillin G IM for **10 days** (MSD congenital syphilis, citing CDC 2021).
- **Pregnancy:** penicillin is the only recommended drug; a pregnant patient with penicillin allergy is **desensitized** (MSD; guide 36).
- **Penicillin allergy, not pregnant (MSD):** doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 14 days (28 days for late latent). Azithromycin has increasingly high failure rates.
- **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** (p. 157): fever, chills, headache and myalgia in the first day of treatment, from killed spirochetes; usually self-limited. MSD: starts **6-12 hours** after the first dose, mostly in secondary syphilis, and resolves within 24 hours. It is **not** a penicillin allergy.
- **Partners (MSD):** treat partners from the **past 90 days** of a primary, secondary or early latent case even if their tests are negative.
- **Recent change (doxycycline PEP, CDC 2024):** for MSM and transgender women who had syphilis, chlamydia or gonorrhea in the past 12 months, clinicians can offer **doxycycline 200 mg once within 72 hours after sex** (no more than 200 mg every 24 hours). In trials it cut syphilis and chlamydia by more than 70% and gonorrhea by about 50%. The CDC makes no recommendation for cisgender women (not enough evidence).

## Leptospira interrogans: leptospirosis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 158)
- Thin spirochete with **hooked ends**, seen on darkfield; can be cultured; diagnosed mostly by serology (p. 158).
- **Spread:** **animal urine in water** (dogs, livestock, **rats**) through mucous membranes or skin breaks; recreational water (jet skiers) and jobs such as **sewer work** (p. 158).
- **Disease** (p. 158):
  - most cases are a mild self-limited fever;
  - **conjunctival suffusion** (red eyes without pus or inflammation) is a key clue;
  - **Weil disease**: **jaundice and azotemia** (liver and kidney failure), fever, bleeding and anemia.
- **Treatment:** Pharm HY lists penicillin as first choice (PDF 120).
- **Other source (MSD):** illness is **biphasic**; conjunctival suffusion appears on day 3 or 4; outbreaks follow heavy rain and flooding. With jaundice the death rate is 5-15%. Mild disease: **oral doxycycline** (or amoxicillin) for 5-7 days. Severe disease: **IV penicillin G**, ceftriaxone or ampicillin for 7 days. Prevention during exposure: doxycycline 200 mg once a week.

## Borrelia burgdorferi: Lyme disease (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 159-160)
- **Tick:** ***Ixodes*** (deer tick), which also carries *Anaplasma* and *Babesia* (guide 61); hikers and campers in **New England** (p. 159).
- **Reservoirs:** white-footed mice (nymphs) and white-tailed deer (adult ticks) (p. 159). MSD adds that deer host adult ticks but do not carry *Borrelia*; more than 90% of US cases are from Maine to Virginia and in Wisconsin, Minnesota and Michigan.
- **Spread in the body:** skin → blood → **heart, joints and nervous system** (p. 159).
- **Stages** (pp. 159-160; NBME TC p. 249):
  1. **Early localized:** **erythema migrans**, an expanding red ring with central clearing ("bull's-eye") at the bite, plus flu-like symptoms.
  2. **Early disseminated:** more EM lesions, **carditis with AV block**, **facial nerve palsy**, migratory joint and muscle pain.
  3. **Late:** chronic **arthritis** and encephalopathy. NBME TC p. 249 puts aseptic meningitis here too.
- Mnemonic: "**A Key Lyme pie to the FACE**": **F**acial palsy, **A**rthritis, **C**ardiac block, **E**rythema migrans (p. 160).
- **Other source (MSD, updated Sep 2026):**
  - EM occurs in **at least 75%**; it appears **3-32 days** after the bite and is diagnosed clinically, because serology is often still negative;
  - facial (Bell) palsy "can be bilateral" (your notes say "typically bilateral"; MSD does not say it is usually bilateral). **Lymphocytic meningitis** and radiculopathy are early disseminated findings (weeks to months), not only late ones;
  - carditis in about 8% (fluctuating AV block, sometimes complete); Osmosis also lists Lyme disease as a cause of myocarditis (p. 90);
  - arthritis in about 60% of untreated patients, mostly the **knee**, more swollen than painful;
  - serology for later stages: an EIA confirmed by a second EIA or a Western blot.
- **Treatment (your notes, p. 160):** **doxycycline** first (it also treats anaplasmosis from the same tick); **amoxicillin**; **ceftriaxone** for severe illness, CNS signs or heart block.
- **Other source (nerve involvement can be treated by mouth):** your notes give ceftriaxone for CNS signs (p. 160). MSD's adult table gives **oral doxycycline for 14-21 days for facial palsy** and lists oral doxycycline as an option for early neurologic disease, with IV ceftriaxone for meningitis as the other choice. **Carditis** still starts with IV ceftriaxone in most cases (oral drugs only for mild first-degree block with PR under 300 ms); complete heart block may need a temporary pacemaker.
- **Other source (MSD) regimens:** EM: doxycycline 10 days, or amoxicillin or cefuroxime 14 days. Arthritis: oral drug for 28 days; IV ceftriaxone if it does not respond. Pregnancy: amoxicillin. MSD avoids doxycycline in children under 8 for Lyme disease.
- **Prophylaxis after a tick bite (MSD, citing IDSA 2020):** one dose of **doxycycline 200 mg** only if all apply: an adult or nymphal ***I. scapularis*** tick, attached **≥36 hours**, started **within 72 hours** of removal, in an area where ≥20% of ticks carry *Borrelia*, and doxycycline is not contraindicated.

## Borrelia recurrentis: relapsing fever (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 160-161)
- **Spread:** the **human body louse** carries *B. recurrentis*; ticks carry the other *Borrelia* species (p. 160).
- **Pattern:** sudden fever for 3-7 days, then days to weeks without fever, then relapse (p. 160).
- **Why it relapses:** **antigenic variation**. Programmed DNA rearrangement changes the surface proteins, so antibodies against the first strain miss the new one (p. 161).
- **Other source (MSD):** in the US, tick-borne relapsing fever comes from *Ornithodoros* soft ticks in the **western states**, especially in rodent-infested **mountain cabins**. Diagnose by seeing spirochetes on a **Wright- or Giemsa-stained blood smear during fever**. Doxycycline is the treatment. A Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction can start within 2 hours of treatment.

## Chlamydia (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 162-166)
- **Species:** *C. trachomatis*; *Chlamydophila pneumoniae* and *C. psittaci*, which cause **atypical pneumonia** by aerosol (pp. 162, 166). Psittacosis comes from **parrots and other birds** (p. 175).
- **Target:** nonciliated columnar or cuboidal epithelium of mucosal surfaces (p. 163).

| Serotypes | Disease | Mnemonic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A, B, C** | **Trachoma**: chronic follicular conjunctivitis → scarring → inturned lashes → corneal scarring and blindness; spread by hands and flies | **ABC** = **A**frica, **B**lindness, **C**hronic |
| **D-K** | **Urethritis, cervicitis, PID**, ectopic pregnancy, infertility, epididymitis, proctitis; **neonatal conjunctivitis** and **neonatal pneumonia** (staccato cough, eosinophilia) | D-K = everything else |
| **L1, L2, L3** | **Lymphogranuloma venereum**: small **painless** genital ulcer, then **painful inguinal nodes that ulcerate (buboes)**; untreated, strictures and fistulas | L = lymph nodes |

Source: El-Husseiny Micro pp. 164-165.

- Your notes: *C. trachomatis* D-K is **the most common bacterial STD in the US** (herpes and HPV are more common overall) (p. 164).
- **Diagnosis:** **NAAT** (also cytoplasmic inclusions on Giemsa or fluorescent stain) (p. 166).
- **Trachoma (WHO, Sep 2026):** the "leading infectious cause of blindness" worldwide (about 1.9 million blind or visually impaired). The **SAFE** strategy: **S**urgery for trichiasis, **A**ntibiotics (mass **azithromycin**), **F**acial cleanliness, **E**nvironmental improvement.
- **Recent change (first-choice drug):** your notes say "doxycycline or azithromycin (favored because onetime treatment)" (p. 166). The **CDC 2021** guideline makes **doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days** the recommended regimen and azithromycin 1 g once an **alternative**. Reason: in a trial of rectal chlamydia in MSM, cure was **100% with doxycycline vs 74% with azithromycin**. Add ceftriaxone if gonorrhea is possible (p. 166; guide 64).
- **Recent change (pregnancy):** your notes say "Erythromycin is effective in infected mothers to prevent neonatal disease" (p. 166). CDC 2021: in pregnancy give **azithromycin 1 g once** (amoxicillin 7 days is the alternative); erythromycin "is no longer recommended" because of stomach side effects. Retest about **4 weeks** after treatment and again at 3 months.
- **Newborns (CDC 2021):** erythromycin eye ointment at birth does **not** prevent chlamydial conjunctivitis. Treat chlamydial conjunctivitis or pneumonia with **oral erythromycin for 14 days** (your notes p. 166: systemic treatment also prevents pneumonia); azithromycin for 3 days is an alternative for pneumonia.
- **LGV (CDC 2021):** **doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 21 days**. After rectal exposure, LGV most often causes **proctocolitis**, which can mimic inflammatory bowel disease. Your notes also give doxycycline (p. 165). Pharm HY lists tetracyclines first for LGV, chlamydia and rickettsial infection (PDF 125).

## Rickettsiae and related bacteria (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 166-168)
| Disease | Organism | Vector | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rocky Mountain spotted fever** | *Rickettsia rickettsii* | ***Dermacentor*** (dog tick) | Fever, headache, rash that starts at the **wrists and ankles** and spreads to the trunk, **palms and soles** |
| **Epidemic typhus** | *R. prowazekii* | **Human body louse** | Rash starts on the **trunk**, spares palms and soles |
| **Endemic (murine) typhus** | *R. typhi* | **Fleas** | Same trunk rash |
| **Ehrlichiosis** | *Ehrlichia chaffeensis* | *Amblyomma* (**lone star tick**) | **Morulae in monocytes**; rash uncommon |
| **Anaplasmosis** | *Anaplasma* | ***Ixodes*** | **Morulae in granulocytes** |
| **Q fever** | *Coxiella burnetii* | **None**: inhaled from cattle or sheep birth fluids | No rash; pneumonia; culture-negative endocarditis (guide 14) |

Sources: El-Husseiny Micro pp. 166-168 and the zoonoses table on p. 175.

- **RMSF** (p. 166): despite the name, most cases are in the **South Atlantic states** (especially North Carolina). Rickettsiae infect **capillary endothelial cells**, causing vasculitis of the brain, liver, skin, lungs, kidneys and gut.
- **Palms and soles rash** = **CARS**: **C**oxsackie A (hand-foot-mouth), **R**MSF, **S**econdary **s**yphilis (p. 167). Mnemonics: "Rickettsii on the wRists, Typhus on the Trunk"; "**MEGA** berry": **M**onocytes = **E**hrlichiosis, **G**ranulocytes = **A**naplasmosis (p. 167).
- **Treatment:** **doxycycline** (p. 168 for Q fever; Pharm HY PDF 125 for rickettsial infections).
- **Other source (CDC):**
  - doxycycline is the drug of choice for **all tickborne rickettsial diseases in patients of all ages**, **including children under 8**; short courses did not stain permanent teeth. "Treatment decisions ... should never be delayed while awaiting laboratory confirmation" (MMWR 2016; RMSF clinical care, Mar 2025);
  - RMSF rash appears **2-4 days** after fever starts; fewer than half have a rash in the first 3 days; about 90% eventually get one. Labs: **thrombocytopenia, hyponatremia**, high transaminases. Doxycycline works best if started in the **first 5 days**. Death rate is 5-10% overall and 40-50% when treatment starts on day 8 or 9;
  - RMSF vectors also include the **brown dog tick** (*Rhipicephalus*) in Arizona and along the US-Mexico border;
  - ehrlichiosis: rash in up to 60% of children but under 30% of adults; leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, high transaminases.

## Mycoplasma (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 169-172)
- **Smallest free-living bacteria**, **no cell wall**, **sterols (cholesterol) in the membrane**; grown on cholesterol-rich media (**Eaton agar**); not seen on Gram stain (p. 169).
- **Spread:** respiratory droplets in close groups: families, **military recruits**, medical school classes (p. 169).
- **Damage:** attaches by the **P1 protein**, stops cilia, and releases hydrogen peroxide and superoxide (pp. 169-170).
- **"Walking pneumonia"** (p. 170): young adults; dry hacking cough, low fever, malaise; **the chest X-ray looks worse than the patient**.
- **Beyond the lungs** (p. 170): **cold agglutinin (IgM) hemolysis** (guide 40; NBME TC p. 117), Stevens-Johnson syndrome (guide 45), joint pain.
- **Treatment:** a **macrolide**, **doxycycline** or a **fluoroquinolone**; cell wall drugs (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, vancomycin) do not work (p. 171).
  - **Other source (CDC, May 2026):** macrolides for children and adults; fluoroquinolones for adults; doxycycline if not improving on a macrolide. Macrolide resistance "has remained under 10% in United States". Most infections are self-limited.
- ***Ureaplasma urealyticum*** (p. 172): **urease-positive**; urethritis, prostatitis, kidney stones.

## Bartonella henselae (El-Husseiny Micro p. 172)
- Lives in cats' mouths; spread by **cat scratches and bites** (p. 172). MSD: cats, particularly **kittens**, are a major reservoir, and cat fleas spread it among cats.
- **Cat-scratch disease:** low fever and swollen nodes, self-limited (p. 172).
  - **Other source (MSD):** a papule at the scratch in 3-10 days, then regional nodes within 2 weeks; nodes can suppurate; **Parinaud oculoglandular syndrome** (conjunctivitis with a preauricular node); neuroretinitis. Usually no antibiotics in healthy people; azithromycin may shorten symptoms. Nodes shrink in 2-5 months.
- **Bacillary angiomatosis:** red-purple papules in **immunocompromised** people (p. 172), which "may resemble Kaposi sarcoma" (MSD). MSD: caused by *B. henselae* or *B. quintana*; can cause peliosis hepatis; treat with **erythromycin or doxycycline for at least 3 months**.
- Also a cause of **culture-negative endocarditis** (p. 172).

## Clinical vignette
A 23-year-old man has had a non-itchy rash for 1 week. Six weeks ago he noticed a painless sore on his penis that healed by itself. He has fever, painless swollen nodes in his neck and groin, and a macular rash on his trunk, **palms and soles**, and moist gray plaques near the anus. The RPR is reactive at 1:64, and the treponemal antibody test is positive. He gets one dose of benzathine penicillin G. Eight hours later he has fever to 39°C, chills and muscle aches, and his rash looks redder.

- **Diagnosis:** **secondary syphilis**: rash including the palms and soles, generalized lymphadenopathy, **condylomata lata** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 154).
- **Tests:** the RPR screens and its titer will be used to track treatment; the treponemal test confirms and will stay positive (pp. 156-157).
- **Treatment:** benzathine penicillin G **2.4 million units IM once** (p. 157; MSD).
- **The reaction:** **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** from killed spirochetes (p. 157). It starts within 6-12 hours and settles within 24 hours (MSD). Give supportive care (antipyretics) (standard teaching); it is **not** a penicillin allergy.
- **Partners:** treat anyone exposed in the past 90 days even if their tests are negative (MSD).

## Common traps
- **Syphilis chancre is painless; chancroid is painful** (guide 64). **LGV ulcer is painless but its nodes are painful** (p. 165).
- **Screening (RPR/VDRL) is nonspecific; the treponemal test confirms and stays positive** (pp. 156-157). Follow cure with the RPR titer.
- **Late latent syphilis = 3 weekly benzathine doses; neurosyphilis = IV aqueous penicillin** (MSD).
- **Jarisch-Herxheimer ≠ penicillin allergy** (p. 157; MSD).
- **Palms and soles rash: CARS** (p. 167). **RMSF starts at the wrists and ankles; typhus starts on the trunk** (p. 167).
- **Doxycycline for suspected RMSF at any age**, without waiting for lab results (CDC).
- **Monocyte morulae = *Ehrlichia*; neutrophil morulae = *Anaplasma*** (p. 167).
- **Erythema migrans needs no blood test** and is treated on sight (MSD).
- **Facial palsy alone in Lyme disease can be treated with oral doxycycline** (MSD).
- **Chlamydia first choice is now doxycycline 7 days**, except in pregnancy, where it is azithromycin (CDC 2021).
- **Erythromycin eye ointment does not prevent chlamydial conjunctivitis** (CDC 2021).
- ***Borrelia* is not obligate intracellular** (NBME TC p. 249 is wrong; see Correction).
- **Mycoplasma: no cell wall → beta-lactams useless** (p. 171).
- **Q fever: no vector, no rash** (p. 168; guide 14).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 25-year-old man has a 1-week-old sore on the shaft of his penis. It is a single, firm, clean-based, painless ulcer, and he has a firm, nontender lymph node in the right groin. Darkfield microscopy of fluid from the ulcer shows motile corkscrew-shaped organisms. He has no drug allergies. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM, one dose.**
   - **Why:** a painless indurated chancre with motile spirochetes on darkfield is **primary syphilis** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 153-154). One dose of long-acting benzathine penicillin treats primary, secondary and early latent syphilis (p. 157; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **benzathine penicillin weekly for 3 weeks**, which is for late latent syphilis or syphilis of unknown duration (MSD).

2. A 34-year-old woman was treated for secondary syphilis 2 years ago with one dose of benzathine penicillin G. At that time her RPR titer was 1:128. Today she has no symptoms, her RPR is nonreactive, and her FTA-ABS is reactive. She has had no new partners. Which is the best explanation for these results?
   - **Answer:** **treponemal antibodies usually stay positive after cure; the nonreactive RPR shows successful treatment.**
   - **Why:** nontreponemal titers fall with treatment and are used to follow it; treponemal tests stay positive (El-Husseiny Micro p. 157; MSD: "usually remain positive for decades").
   - **Trap:** **treatment failure needing a second course**. A reactive FTA-ABS alone does not mean active infection.

3. A 26-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus has had two miscarriages. A routine screening RPR is reactive at a titer of 1:2. An FTA-ABS test is nonreactive. She has no genital lesions or rash. Her partial thromboplastin time is prolonged. What is the most likely cause of the reactive RPR?
   - **Answer:** **antiphospholipid (anticardiolipin) antibodies.**
   - **Why:** the RPR detects antibodies to **cardiolipin**, a phospholipid (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 156-157). Lupus and antiphospholipid antibodies cause false-positive tests (p. 157; NBME TC p. 389; MSD). A negative treponemal test rules out syphilis.
   - **Trap:** **latent syphilis**. Latent syphilis would have a **positive** treponemal test.

4. A 61-year-old man has had sharp, stabbing pains in his legs for months and now walks with a wide-based, stamping gait. He falls when he closes his eyes while standing. Vibration and position sense are lost in both feet. His pupils constrict when he looks at a near object but not to light. Serum and CSF VDRL are reactive. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **IV aqueous penicillin G for 10-14 days.**
   - **Why:** this is **tabes dorsalis** with Argyll Robertson pupils, a form of neurosyphilis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 155; guide 27). Neurosyphilis needs IV aqueous penicillin G every 4 hours for 10-14 days (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **benzathine penicillin G weekly for 3 weeks**, which treats late latent syphilis but does not reach treponemicidal levels in the CSF (standard teaching).

5. A 58-year-old man has a diastolic murmur at the right upper sternal border. Imaging shows a dilated ascending aorta. His treponemal antibody test is positive. Which process damaged the aortic wall?
   - **Answer:** **endarteritis obliterans of the vasa vasorum**, starving the media of blood.
   - **Why:** tertiary syphilis causes aortitis by destroying the vasa vasorum, leading to a thoracic aneurysm and aortic regurgitation (El-Husseiny Micro p. 155; Osmosis p. 15; MSD: 10-25 years after infection).
   - **Trap:** **cystic medial necrosis**, a noninfectious cause of aneurysm (Osmosis p. 15) seen with Marfan syndrome (standard teaching).

6. A 3-week-old infant has a blood-tinged nasal discharge, a copper-colored macular rash on the palms and soles, and an enlarged liver and spleen. X-rays show periostitis of the long bones. His mother had no prenatal care. Which measure would most likely have prevented this disease?
   - **Answer:** **syphilis testing at the first prenatal visit and penicillin treatment of the mother, at least 4 weeks before delivery.**
   - **Why:** this is **early congenital syphilis** (snuffles, palm and sole rash, hepatosplenomegaly, periostitis) (El-Husseiny Micro p. 155; MSD). Your notes say to treat the mother early in pregnancy (p. 155); MSD: treatment less than 4 weeks before delivery may not cure the fetus; CDC: test at the first prenatal visit.
   - **Trap:** **erythromycin eye ointment at birth**, which prevents gonococcal eye infection, not syphilis.

7. A 31-year-old man develops fever, severe muscle pain, headache and red eyes without discharge 10 days after a freshwater triathlon held after heavy rains. Three days later he is jaundiced, his creatinine is 3.1 mg/dL, and he has nosebleeds. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **IV penicillin G** (ceftriaxone is an equal choice).
   - **Why:** fever with **conjunctival suffusion** after water contaminated by animal urine, then **jaundice and kidney failure**, is **Weil disease** (severe leptospirosis) (El-Husseiny Micro p. 158; MSD). Severe disease is treated with IV penicillin G, ceftriaxone or ampicillin (MSD); Pharm HY lists penicillin first (PDF 120).
   - **Trap:** **oral doxycycline**, which is for mild leptospirosis (MSD).

8. A 40-year-old woman from Connecticut has a red, expanding, ring-shaped rash on her thigh with central clearing, now 10 cm across, 9 days after a camping trip. She has mild fatigue and headache. She does not remember a tick bite. She is not pregnant. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **start oral doxycycline (10 days) now, without serologic testing.**
   - **Why:** this is **erythema migrans**, early localized Lyme disease (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 159-160). MSD: EM is diagnosed clinically because antibodies are often not yet positive; a single EM lesion needs 10 days of doxycycline.
   - **Trap:** **order Lyme serology and treat only if positive**. Early serology is often negative and would delay treatment (MSD).

9. A 22-year-old man from Wisconsin has light-headedness and fatigue. Five weeks ago he had a large red ring-shaped rash on his back that faded. His pulse is 38/min. An ECG shows complete heart block. Which treatment is most appropriate in addition to cardiac monitoring?
   - **Answer:** **IV ceftriaxone** (with a temporary pacemaker if needed).
   - **Why:** this is **Lyme carditis**, an early disseminated finding (El-Husseiny Micro p. 160; NBME TC p. 249). Your notes give ceftriaxone for heart block (p. 160); MSD gives IV ceftriaxone for carditis, with oral drugs only for mild first-degree block, and a temporary pacemaker for complete block.
   - **Trap:** **a permanent pacemaker**. Lyme AV block fluctuates and usually resolves with treatment (MSD: complete block "may require a temporary pacemaker").

10. A 35-year-old woman in Massachusetts removes an engorged tick from her thigh. A nurse identifies it as an adult *Ixodes scapularis* that had been attached for about 2 days. It was removed 30 hours ago. She feels well, has no rash, is not pregnant or breastfeeding, and has no drug allergies. More than 20% of local ticks carry *Borrelia burgdorferi*. Which is the most appropriate management?
    - **Answer:** **a single oral dose of doxycycline 200 mg.**
    - **Why:** she meets every IDSA criterion: an *I. scapularis* tick, attached ≥36 hours, prophylaxis within 72 hours of removal, a high-prevalence area, and no contraindication (MSD, citing IDSA 2020).
    - **Trap:** **a 10-day course of doxycycline**, which treats established Lyme disease, not a bite.

11. A 29-year-old man has his third episode of fever in 5 weeks after a vacation in a rodent-infested mountain cabin in Colorado. Each episode lasts about 4 days and ends suddenly, followed by a week without fever. During a fever, a Wright-stained blood smear shows spirochetes between the red cells. Which mechanism best explains the repeated episodes?
    - **Answer:** **antigenic variation of the organism's surface proteins.**
    - **Why:** this is **relapsing fever** (*Borrelia*; tick-borne in the western US). Programmed DNA rearrangement changes the surface proteins, so antibodies against the first population miss the new one (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 160-161; MSD: soft ticks, mountain cabins, spirochetes on a smear during fever).
    - **Trap:** **survival inside macrophages**, which explains relapse in diseases such as brucellosis and typhoid, not relapsing fever (standard teaching).

12. A 6-year-old girl in North Carolina has had fever, severe headache and muscle aches for 4 days. Pink macules began on her wrists and ankles and have spread to her trunk, palms and soles. Platelets are 88,000/mm³ and serum sodium is 129 mEq/L. Her mother removed a tick from her scalp last week. Which is the most appropriate next step?
    - **Answer:** **start doxycycline now.**
    - **Why:** this is **Rocky Mountain spotted fever** (*R. rickettsii*, *Dermacentor* tick, rash from the wrists and ankles inward, thrombocytopenia, hyponatremia) (El-Husseiny Micro p. 166; CDC). The CDC: doxycycline for patients of **all ages**, including children under 8; never wait for lab confirmation; it works best in the first 5 days.
    - **Trap:** **wait for serology, or avoid doxycycline because she is under 8**. Delayed treatment raises the death rate sharply (CDC: 40-50% when treatment starts on day 8 or 9).

13. A 67-year-old man in Minnesota has 5 days of fever, chills, headache and muscle aches. He has no rash. WBC is 2,600/mm³, platelets 70,000/mm³, and liver enzymes are raised. A peripheral smear shows clusters of small inclusions inside neutrophils. Which organism is most likely?
    - **Answer:** ***Anaplasma phagocytophilum*.**
    - **Why:** morulae in **granulocytes** after an *Ixodes* tick exposure in the upper Midwest is **anaplasmosis** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 167, "MEGA berry"; CDC MMWR 2016: *I. scapularis* in the northeastern and Midwestern US). Treat with doxycycline (CDC).
    - **Trap:** ***Ehrlichia chaffeensis***, whose morulae are in **monocytes** and whose vector is the lone star tick (p. 167; MMWR 2016).

14. A 24-year-old man who has sex with men has rectal discomfort. A rectal NAAT is positive for *Chlamydia trachomatis*; tests for gonorrhea, syphilis and HIV are negative. He has no inguinal nodes and no bloody discharge. Which treatment is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days.**
    - **Why:** the CDC 2021 guideline prefers doxycycline for chlamydia. In a trial of rectal chlamydia in MSM, cure was 100% with doxycycline vs 74% with azithromycin (CDC).
    - **Trap:** **azithromycin 1 g once**, which your notes favor (p. 166) but is now only an alternative (CDC 2021).

15. A 27-year-old woman at 14 weeks' gestation has a positive cervical NAAT for *Chlamydia trachomatis* at her first prenatal visit. Her gonorrhea test is negative. She has no allergies. Which treatment is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **azithromycin 1 g orally once**, with a repeat NAAT about 4 weeks later.
    - **Why:** the CDC 2021 regimen in pregnancy is azithromycin (amoxicillin is the alternative); test of cure at about 4 weeks is recommended in pregnancy (CDC). Treating her protects the newborn from conjunctivitis and pneumonia (El-Husseiny Micro p. 164).
    - **Trap:** **doxycycline**, which is avoided in pregnancy, or **erythromycin**, which your notes list (p. 166) but the CDC no longer recommends because of stomach side effects.

16. A 19-year-old college student has had a dry cough, sore throat, low fever and fatigue for 10 days. He still attends classes. A chest X-ray shows patchy infiltrates in both lungs that look worse than he does. He took amoxicillin for 5 days without improvement. Which property of the most likely organism explains why amoxicillin failed?
    - **Answer:** **it has no cell wall.**
    - **Why:** this is "walking pneumonia" from ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae*** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 170). It lacks peptidoglycan, so cell wall drugs cannot work; macrolides, doxycycline or fluoroquinolones are used (p. 171; CDC: mycoplasmas "are inherently resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics").
    - **Trap:** **beta-lactamase production**, which explains amoxicillin failure against *H. influenzae* or *Moraxella*, not *Mycoplasma* (standard teaching).

17. A 38-year-old man with untreated HIV infection and a CD4 count of 40/mm³ has several bright red, raised, berry-like skin nodules that bleed easily. He has two kittens. A skin biopsy shows a proliferation of small blood vessels; special stains show bacilli. Which treatment is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **erythromycin (or doxycycline) for at least 3 months.**
    - **Why:** this is **bacillary angiomatosis** from *Bartonella* in an immunocompromised patient (El-Husseiny Micro p. 172; MSD). MSD: it "may resemble Kaposi sarcoma"; treat with erythromycin or doxycycline for at least 3 months.
    - **Trap:** **chemotherapy for Kaposi sarcoma**. Kaposi sarcoma (HHV-8) is a tumor, not an infection, and does not contain bacteria (standard teaching).

18. A 45-year-old woman in a rural area of Ethiopia has painful eyes. Her upper eyelids are scarred and her eyelashes turn inward and rub the cornea, which is cloudy. Several children in her village have chronic follicular conjunctivitis, and flies cluster on their faces. Which measure would best prevent new cases in this village?
    - **Answer:** **mass azithromycin treatment with face washing and better water and sanitation** (the WHO SAFE strategy).
    - **Why:** this is **trachoma** (*C. trachomatis* serotypes A-C), spread by hands and flies; chronic infection scars the lids and turns the lashes in (El-Husseiny Micro p. 164). WHO: SAFE = surgery, antibiotics (mass azithromycin), facial cleanliness, environmental improvement.
    - **Trap:** **topical corticosteroid drops**, which do not treat the infection or stop its spread (standard teaching).
