## In one line
Every drug hits one target; every resistance mechanism either destroys the drug, changes the target, keeps the drug out, or pumps it out. Learn the drug-target pair, and the resistance follows.

## Core mechanism: four ways bacteria resist
The four mechanisms and the three ways genes spread are also summarized on [MSD Manual: Overview of Antibacterial Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/bacteria-and-antibacterial-medications/overview-of-antibacterial-medications).
1. **Destroy the drug:** enzymes such as beta-lactamases and carbapenemases break the beta-lactam ring. [StatPearls: Antibiotic Resistance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)
2. **Change the target:** altered penicillin-binding proteins (MRSA), altered 23S rRNA, altered DNA gyrase. [StatPearls: Antibiotic Resistance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)
3. **Keep the drug out:** lose porin channels in the outer membrane (gram-negatives). [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)
4. **Pump the drug out:** efflux transporters. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)

Genes spread between bacteria by **transformation** (taking up free DNA), **transduction** (carried by a phage) and **conjugation** (direct transfer, often of plasmids). [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)

## MRSA and VRE: the two classic target changes
| Feature | MRSA | VRE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug normally works by | Beta-lactams acylate the transpeptidase (penicillin-binding protein, PBP) and block peptidoglycan cross-linking | Vancomycin binds the D-Ala-D-Ala end of peptidoglycan precursors |
| What changes | *mecA* gene makes PBP2a, a PBP that beta-lactams bind poorly | The precursor end changes to D-Ala-D-**lactate**, which vancomycin binds weakly |
| Gene location | *mecA* on the staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (mobile element) | *vanA* (on transposon Tn1546, usually carried on plasmids) is most common; *vanB* is often chromosomal ([Miller 2020, PMC7640809](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7640809/)) |
| Usual organism | *Staphylococcus aureus* | Mostly *Enterococcus faecium* |
| Treatment options | Vancomycin or linezolid; ceftaroline (the anti-MRSA cephalosporin) | Linezolid or daptomycin |

Your notes (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 327, 333-334): the anti-staphylococcal penicillins (methicillin, nafcillin, oxacillin, cloxacillin, dicloxacillin) resist beta-lactamase because a bulky side group blocks the enzyme from the beta-lactam ring, so MRSA resists them by an **altered PBP target**, not an enzyme. Vancomycin binds the D-Ala-D-Ala end of cell-wall precursors; VRE (and the emerging VRSA) swap in **D-lactate**, so vancomycin cannot bind. Vancomycin side effects: nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, thrombophlebitis and **red man syndrome** (histamine-driven flushing with fast IV infusion; slow the infusion and pretreat with antihistamines). Daptomycin, for MRSA skin infection and bacteremia, punches channels in the bacterial membrane so ions leak out and the cell depolarizes.

Sources: [StatPearls: Beta-Lactam Antibiotics](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545311/), [StatPearls: Staphylococcal Pneumonia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559152/), [StatPearls: Antibiotic Resistance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/), [StatPearls: VRE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513233/)

## Beta-lactamase inhibitors
Clavulanic acid, sulbactam and tazobactam protect a partner penicillin from beta-lactamase; newer avibactam and vaborbactam also cover KPC (*Klebsiella pneumoniae* carbapenemase) isolates [MSD Manual: Klebsiella, Enterobacter, and Serratia Infections](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/gram-negative-bacilli/klebsiella-enterobacter-and-serratia-infections), but not metallo-beta-lactamases such as NDM (standard teaching). [StatPearls: Beta-Lactams](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545311/), [StatPearls: Antibiotic Resistance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)

## Antifungals: target map
| Class (example) | Target | Effect | Key adverse effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Polyenes (amphotericin B, nystatin) | Bind ergosterol | Make pores in the membrane | Nephrotoxicity (less with liposomal form) |
| Azoles (fluconazole, ketoconazole) | Inhibit lanosterol 14α-demethylase | Block ergosterol synthesis | Drug interactions with all azoles, fewest with fluconazole (MSD); they inhibit human cytochrome P450, so all raise levels of P450-cleared drugs, and ketoconazole also blocks steroid synthesis (low cortisol and testosterone: gynecomastia, low libido, irregular periods); liver dysfunction (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 356) |
| Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin) | Inhibit β-(1,3)-D-glucan synthase | Weaken the cell **wall** | Hepatitis, rash (MSD); GI upset and flushing from histamine release (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 357) |
| Terbinafine | Inhibits squalene epoxidase | Blocks an early ergosterol step; used for dermatophytes, especially nail infection | GI upset, headache, liver toxicity, taste change (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 356; StatPearls names headache as the most common) |
| Flucytosine | Converted by fungal cytosine deaminase to 5-fluorouracil | Blocks DNA synthesis (inhibits thymidylate synthase) and disrupts RNA | **Bone marrow suppression** (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia); used with amphotericin B for cryptococcosis |
| Griseofulvin | Binds fungal microtubules | Stops cell division; deposits in new keratin, so it works orally (not topically) on dermatophytes | Teratogenic, carcinogenic, confusion, headache; **induces** cytochrome P450, so warfarin is broken down faster (El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 357) |

Your notes add: only fluconazole among the azoles is said to reach the CSF, so it is used for long-term suppression of cryptococcal meningitis in AIDS; itraconazole for Blastomyces, Coccidioides and Histoplasma; voriconazole for Aspergillus; flucytosine is given **with amphotericin B** for cryptococcal meningitis and causes bone marrow suppression (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 355-356).

**Other source (azoles kill or stop fungi?):** your notes call the azoles "fungicidal" (p. 355). Azoles are usually described as fungistatic against yeasts such as Candida (standard teaching); StatPearls does not use either word. If a question asks which class is fungistatic, the usual answer is the azoles.

[StatPearls: Antifungal Agents](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538168/), [MSD Manual: Antifungal Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/fungal-infections/antifungal-medications), [FDA label: Ancobon (flucytosine), 2022](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2022/017001s034lbl.pdf) (flucytosine mechanism and marrow toxicity)

## Antiviral example: acyclovir
- Acyclovir is phosphorylated first by **viral thymidine kinase**, so it is activated only in infected cells; its triphosphate inhibits viral DNA polymerase and is an obligate chain terminator. [Bacon et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC145299/)
- **Your notes (El-Husseiny Microbiology, pp. 360-362):** acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir and ganciclovir need a **viral kinase** for the first phosphate; host kinases add the rest, and the triphosphate stops viral DNA polymerase by chain termination. Acyclovir covers HSV and VZV, has weak EBV activity, no CMV activity and no effect on latent virus; its main toxicity is **crystal nephropathy**, prevented by hydration and slower infusion. Ganciclovir (and the oral prodrug valganciclovir) is for CMV, including retinitis, and causes dose-limiting **leukopenia and thrombocytopenia**. **Foscarnet** is a pyrophosphate analog that binds the polymerase directly, needs no viral kinase, and is used for ganciclovir-resistant CMV retinitis and acyclovir-resistant HSV; it causes nephrotoxicity and low magnesium and calcium (it chelates calcium), which can trigger seizures.
- **Correction (which kinase):** your notes say ganciclovir needs "viral thymidine kinase" (p. 360). That is true in HSV-infected cells, but in **CMV** the first phosphate is added by a different viral kinase, the **UL97** phosphotransferase; mutations in UL97 or in the DNA polymerase cause ganciclovir-resistant CMV (standard teaching).
- About 95% of acyclovir-resistant herpes simplex virus (HSV) isolates lack functional thymidine kinase. Foscarnet does not need thymidine kinase, so it is the substitute of choice. [Bacon et al. 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC145299/)

## Clinical vignette
A 70-year-old on a ventilator develops pneumonia. Sputum grows gram-positive cocci in clusters that are resistant to oxacillin. Which gene product explains the resistance, and which drug is a standard choice?

**Answer:** penicillin-binding protein 2a from *mecA*; vancomycin or linezolid.
**Why:** PBP2a has low affinity for beta-lactams, so cross-linking continues despite the drug. [StatPearls: Staphylococcal Pneumonia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559152/)
**Trap:** choosing beta-lactamase production. Oxacillin is built to resist staphylococcal beta-lactamase (its bulky side group blocks the enzyme; El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 327), so oxacillin resistance means the target (the PBP) has changed.

## Common traps
- MRSA resistance is a **target change** (PBP2a), not an enzyme; adding clavulanate does not help.
- Vancomycin binds the **substrate** (D-Ala-D-Ala), not an enzyme.
- Amphotericin binds ergosterol; azoles block ergosterol **synthesis**; echinocandins hit the **wall**, not the membrane.
- Acyclovir-resistant HSV usually lacks thymidine kinase, so ganciclovir (also TK-dependent) will not rescue it; foscarnet will. (Your notes list ganciclovir among the drugs that need viral thymidine kinase; El-Husseiny Microbiology, p. 360.)

## Practice questions
1. A patient's *Enterococcus faecium* bacteremia does not respond to vancomycin. What molecular change explains this?
   - **Answer:** the peptidoglycan precursor ends in D-Ala-D-lactate instead of D-Ala-D-Ala.
   - **Why:** vancomycin binds the altered terminus weakly; vanA (usually plasmid-borne) and vanB are the common resistance genes. [StatPearls: VRE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513233/)
   - **Trap:** choosing a beta-lactamase or efflux pump.
2. A drug that inhibits β-(1,3)-D-glucan synthase is used for invasive candidiasis. Which class?
   - **Answer:** echinocandin (for example caspofungin).
   - **Why:** glucan synthase builds the fungal cell wall. [StatPearls: Antifungal Agents](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538168/), [MSD Manual: Antifungal Medications](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/fungal-infections/antifungal-medications)
   - **Trap:** azoles act on ergosterol synthesis in the membrane.
3. HSV lesions in an immunocompromised patient keep growing despite acyclovir. What is the most likely mechanism, and which drug works?
   - **Answer:** thymidine kinase-deficient virus; foscarnet.
   - **Why:** about 95% of resistant isolates lack TK, and foscarnet does not need TK activation. [Bacon et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC145299/)
   - **Trap:** increasing the acyclovir dose.
4. A *Klebsiella* isolate becomes resistant to many drugs after acquiring a plasmid from another species. Which transfer mechanism?
   - **Answer:** conjugation.
   - **Why:** conjugation moves plasmids directly between bacteria; resistance genes often ride on mobile elements. [StatPearls: Antibiotic Resistance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513277/)
   - **Trap:** transduction uses a bacteriophage; transformation is uptake of free DNA.
