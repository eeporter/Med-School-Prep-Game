# Anemia part 2: normal-size anemias, hemolysis, sickle cell and marrow failure

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials for USMLE Step 1, Hematology & Oncology (normocytic anemia and reticulocytes p. 43, extravascular hemolysis pp. 43-48, intravascular hemolysis pp. 49-53, marrow failure pp. 54-56, hemoglobin electrophoresis p. 56). Small-cell and big-cell anemias and anemia of chronic disease are in guide 39. Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
A normal-size (normocytic) anemia is either **destruction** (hemolysis: the marrow is working, so reticulocytes are **high**) or **underproduction** (the marrow is failing, so reticulocytes are **low**). For hemolysis, ask **where** the cells die: in the **spleen** (extravascular: big spleen, jaundice) or **inside vessels** (intravascular: hemoglobin in the urine, haptoglobin gone).

## Step 1: the reticulocyte count (p. 43)
- Red cells live **120 days**, so about **1-2%** are replaced daily; normal reticulocyte count is **1-2%**.
- A healthy marrow facing anemia raises it **above 3%**.
- **The percent is falsely high in anemia**, because it is a percent of fewer red cells. **Correct it: reticulocyte % × (patient's Hct ÷ 45).**
  - Corrected **over 3%** → good marrow response → **destruction** (or bleeding).
  - Corrected **under 3%** → poor response → **underproduction**.
- **Worked example:** reticulocytes 6%, Hct 22%. 6 × 22/45 = **2.9%** → just under 3%, so the marrow response is **not** adequate despite the "high" raw number.

## Step 2: where are the cells destroyed? (pp. 43-44, 49)
| | **Extravascular** (spleen, liver macrophages) | **Intravascular** (inside vessels) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | Macrophages eat red cells; heme → iron (recycled) + protoporphyrin → **unconjugated bilirubin** | Cells burst in the blood; free hemoglobin |
| Findings | **Splenomegaly**, **jaundice**, **pigment gallstones**, high LDH | **Hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria**, **hemosiderinuria** (days later), **very low haptoglobin**, high LDH |
| Urine | **No** hemoglobin | **Hemoglobin** (red-brown urine) |
| Examples | Hereditary spherocytosis, sickle cell, HbC, warm (IgG) AIHA | PNH, G6PD deficiency, cold (IgM) AIHA (your notes; MSD says mostly extravascular), MAHA, prosthetic valves, malaria |

LDH = lactate dehydrogenase. AIHA = autoimmune hemolytic anemia. MAHA = microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. **Haptoglobin** binds free hemoglobin and protects the kidney tubules from it (p. 49).

## Extravascular hemolysis: problems inside the red cell

### Hereditary spherocytosis (p. 44)
- Inherited defect in the proteins that tie the membrane to the cytoskeleton: **spectrin, ankyrin or band 3**.
- The cell loses bits of membrane over time and becomes a **sphere**: small, dense, **no central pallor**, stains darker red.
- Spheres cannot squeeze through the spleen and are eaten there.
- **Findings:** spherocytes, **high MCHC** (mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration) and high RDW, **splenomegaly**, jaundice, **pigment gallstones**, **aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19**.
- **Test:** **osmotic fragility**: spheres burst early in dilute salt water.
- **Treatment: splenectomy**. The anemia goes away but **spherocytes remain**, and **Howell-Jolly bodies** (nuclear remnants the spleen used to remove) appear.

**Other source (spherocytosis):** MSD: **autosomal dominant**; protein 4.2 is also involved; the **direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test is negative**, which separates it from immune hemolysis; reduced **EMA (eosin-5-maleimide) binding** is another test; splenectomy "after appropriate vaccination" is for symptomatic hemolysis, gallstone pain or repeated aplastic crises ([MSD: Hereditary Spherocytosis, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/hereditary-spherocytosis-and-hereditary-elliptocytosis)).

### Sickle cell disease (pp. 45-47)
**The mutation:** autosomal recessive; in the **beta chain**, **glutamic acid (charged, water-loving) → valine (water-avoiding)**. Disease = two abnormal genes, **over 90% HbS** (p. 45).

**Why cells sickle (p. 46):** **deoxygenated HbS polymerizes** into needles.
- **Triggers:** low oxygen, **dehydration**, **acidosis**.
- **Protection:** **HbF**; babies are protected for the first months. **Hydroxyurea raises HbF.**

**Hemolysis (p. 46):** both kinds. Extravascular (anemia, jaundice, pigment stones) and intravascular (low haptoglobin, target cells). The marrow expands: **crew-cut skull, chipmunk face**, extramedullary hematopoiesis, **aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19**.

**Vaso-occlusion (pp. 46-47):**
| Complication | What happens | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dactylitis** | Bone infarcts in hands and feet | Swollen hands and feet in an **infant**; often the first sign |
| **Autosplenectomy** | Spleen scars and shrinks | **Encapsulated infections** (*S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *N. meningitidis*, *Salmonella*); **Salmonella osteomyelitis**; **Howell-Jolly bodies** |
| **Acute chest syndrome** | Clots in lung microvessels, often after **pneumonia** | Chest pain, shortness of breath, new lung infiltrate; your notes: **most common cause of death in adults** |
| **Pain crisis** | Tissue ischemia | Bone, chest, belly pain |
| **Renal papillary necrosis** | Infarcts of the papillae | Gross hematuria, proteinuria |

Your notes say infection with encapsulated bacteria is the most common cause of death in **children**, and that children should be vaccinated by age 5 (p. 46).

**Sickle cell trait (p. 47):** one normal and one sickle gene, **under 50% HbS** (HbA is made a bit more efficiently). Usually **no anemia and no symptoms**. Cells sickle only in the **renal medulla** (very low oxygen and very salty): **microscopic hematuria** and, over time, **trouble concentrating urine**.

**Lab tests (p. 47):**
| | Disease (HbSS) | Trait (HbAS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sickle and target cells on smear | **Yes** | No |
| Metabisulfite screen (makes any HbS sickle) | Positive | **Positive** |
| Electrophoresis | **90% HbS, 8% HbF, 2% HbA2, no HbA** | **55% HbA, 43% HbS, 2% HbA2** |

**Other source (sickle cell numbers and prevention):** MSD: trait in about **8%** of people of African ancestry in the US (your notes: 10%); **oral penicillin from birth to at least 5 years**, plus pneumococcal, *H. influenzae* and meningococcal vaccines; "acute chest syndrome is the leading cause of death in sickle cell disease" overall; **transcranial Doppler** screening yearly from age 2 to 16 to catch stroke risk; trait can rarely cause exertional rhabdomyolysis and is linked to the very rare **renal medullary carcinoma** ([MSD: Sickle Cell Disease, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/sickle-cell-disease)).

**Recent change (sickle cell treatment):** besides hydroxyurea, MSD now lists **L-glutamine** (lowers oxidative stress), **crizanlizumab** (blocks P-selectin, so sickle cells stick less to vessel walls), and two gene therapies: **lovotibeglogene autotemcel** (adds a working beta gene) and **exagamglogene autotemcel** (CRISPR editing to switch HbF back on). Stem cell transplant "remains the only curative treatment" (same MSD page).

### Hemoglobin C (pp. 47-48)
- Autosomal recessive beta-chain change: **glutamic acid → lysine**. HbC is less soluble and forms **crystals**, making cells stiff → **mild** extravascular hemolysis.
- **HbC crystals** on the smear.

### Hemoglobin electrophoresis (p. 56)
- Hemoglobin runs toward the **positive pole (anode)** because it is negatively charged.
- **HbS** loses a negative charge (glutamate → neutral valine) → runs **slower**. **HbC** swaps glutamate for **positive lysine** → runs **slowest**.
- **Speed: A > S > C** (memory hook: "**A**lways **S**low **C**ars"; standard teaching). **HbSC disease** shows two bands, S and C.

## Intravascular hemolysis: complement and oxidants

### Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, PNH (pp. 49-50)
1. An **acquired** stem cell mutation stops the cell from making the **GPI (glycosylphosphatidylinositol) anchor**.
2. Without it, the complement shields **CD55 (DAF, decay-accelerating factor)** and **CD59 (MAC inhibitor)** cannot attach.
3. Complement destroys **red cells, white cells and platelets** → hemolysis plus **pancytopenia**.
- Your notes: attacks often at **night** (shallow breathing causes mild acidosis that switches complement on), with dark urine in the **morning**.
- **Diagnosis:** acidified serum test or **flow cytometry** showing no CD55/CD59.
- **Main cause of death: thrombosis** of the **hepatic, portal or cerebral veins** (damaged platelets trigger clotting).
- **Suspect PNH** with **hemolysis + clots + pancytopenia**.
- **Complications:** iron deficiency (iron lost in urine) and **AML** (acute myeloid leukemia) in about 10%.

**Other source (PNH presentation):** MSD: the gene is **PIGA**; "hemolysis occurs throughout the day not just at night and only ~30% of patients have overt hemoglobinuria"; **flow cytometry** (absent CD59, CD55 and **FLAER** binding) is the diagnostic test and is "highly sensitive and specific"; about **50%** of people with acquired **aplastic anemia** carry a PNH clone ([MSD: PNH, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/paroxysmal-nocturnal-hemoglobinuria-pnh)).

**Recent change (PNH treatment):** MSD lists **complement inhibitors**: **eculizumab** and **ravulizumab** (block C5), **pegcetacoplan** (blocks C3), **iptacopan** (blocks factor B) and **danicopan** (blocks factor D, added to a C5 inhibitor). Because blocking complement invites *Neisseria* infection, patients get the **meningococcal vaccine** at least 14 days before starting (same MSD page).

### G6PD deficiency (p. 50)
**The chain of events:**
1. Red cells constantly face **H2O2** (oxidant stress).
2. **Glutathione** neutralizes it and becomes oxidized.
3. **NADPH**, made by G6PD, is needed to restore glutathione.
4. **Low G6PD → low NADPH → no reduced glutathione → oxidant damage → hemolysis.**
- **X-linked recessive** (almost only males).
- **African variant:** mild. **Mediterranean variant:** severe.
- **Triggers:** **infection** (MSD: hemolysis "commonly follows" fever and infections), **drugs (primaquine, sulfa drugs, dapsone)** and **fava beans**.
- Oxidized hemoglobin clumps as **Heinz bodies**; the spleen bites them out → **bite cells**.
- **Hemoglobinuria and back pain hours after** the trigger.

**Other source (G6PD):** MSD: over **10% of African American males** are affected; other triggers include **rasburicase**, **methylene blue**, **nitrofurans**, phenazopyridine and diabetic ketoacidosis; Heinz bodies need **special stains**; the G6PD test can be **falsely normal during an attack** because the oldest, most deficient cells have already burst, so retest weeks later ([MSD: G6PD Deficiency, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/glucose-6-phosphate-dehydrogenase-g6pd-deficiency)).

## Hemolysis from outside the red cell

### Immune (autoimmune) hemolytic anemia (p. 51)
| | **Warm (IgG)** | **Cold (IgM)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Binds at | Body core temperature | Cooler fingers, toes, ears |
| Where cells die | **Extravascular**: spleen nibbles membrane → **spherocytes** | Fixes **complement**; your notes: mostly **intravascular**; MSD: mainly **extravascular** (liver and spleen) |
| Linked to | **SLE**, **CLL** (chronic lymphocytic leukemia), drugs | ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae***, **infectious mononucleosis** |
| Your notes' treatment | Stop the drug, **steroids**, IVIG, splenectomy if needed | Treat the cause |

- **Drug mechanisms:** **penicillin** sticks to the red cell and antibodies attack the drug-cell complex (hapten); **alpha-methyldopa** makes the body produce true **autoantibodies** against red cells.
- **Coombs tests:**
  - **Direct Coombs (DAT, direct antiglobulin test):** are the patient's **red cells already coated**? Anti-IgG added to the patient's cells → clumping. **The key test for immune hemolysis.**
  - **Indirect Coombs:** are there **free antibodies in the patient's serum**? Patient serum + test red cells + anti-IgG → clumping.

**Other source (AIHA):** your notes call SLE the most common cause of warm AIHA (p. 51); MSD divides warm AIHA into **primary (idiopathic)** and secondary (for example SLE) without ranking them. The DAT is typically **IgG-positive, C3-negative** in warm AIHA and **C3-positive, IgG-negative** in cold agglutinin disease; lymphoma is another cold cause. **Glucocorticoids** are first line for warm AIHA, **rituximab** second line, and about one-third to one-half respond lastingly to splenectomy ([MSD: Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/autoimmune-hemolytic-anemia)).

**Other source (where cold agglutinin cells die):** your notes say IgM disease "usually involves intravascular hemolysis" (p. 51). MSD says "The chronic hemolysis occurs largely in the extravascular mononuclear phagocyte system of the liver and spleen", and "intravascular hemolysis can occur when a complement-amplifying condition, such as infection, is present" ([MSD: Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-hemolysis/autoimmune-hemolytic-anemia)). For AIHA overall MSD says "Hemolysis is predominantly extravascular."

**Recent change (cold agglutinin treatment):** MSD lists avoiding cold and treating the cause, **rituximab** (sometimes with bendamustine), and the complement C1s inhibitor **sutimlimab** for severe anemia (same MSD page).

### Mechanical and infectious hemolysis (p. 53)
- **Microangiopathic (MAHA):** red cells shredded by tiny clots in **TTP/HUS, DIC, HELLP, SLE, malignant hypertension** → **schistocytes (helmet cells)**. Long-term loss can cause **iron deficiency**. (TTP, HUS and DIC are in guide 38.)
- **Macroangiopathic:** **prosthetic heart valves**, **aortic stenosis** → schistocytes.
- **Infections:** **malaria** (red cells burst on schedule, causing **cyclic fevers**) and **Babesia**.

## Underproduction: low reticulocytes (pp. 54-56)
- **Kidney failure:** less **EPO (erythropoietin)** from peritubular interstitial cells.
- **Anemia of chronic disease:** see guide 39.

### Aplastic anemia (p. 55)
- **Stem cells damaged** → **pancytopenia** (low red cells, white cells and platelets) with **low reticulocytes**. Not the same as an **aplastic crisis**, which hits red cells only.
- **Causes:** idiopathic (immune; may follow acute **hepatitis**), **radiation**, drugs (**benzene, chloramphenicol, alkylating agents, antimetabolites**), viruses (**parvovirus B19, EBV, HIV, hepatitis**), **Fanconi anemia** (DNA repair defect; short stature, **thumb and radial defects**, café-au-lait spots, more cancers).
- **Labs:** low reticulocytes, **high EPO** (kidneys are trying). Marrow aspirate is "**dry**"; biopsy shows marrow replaced by **fat**.
- **Symptoms:** tiredness and pallor (anemia), bleeding and petechiae (platelets), **infections** (neutrophils).
- **Your notes' treatment:** stop the drug; transfusions and growth factors (EPO, GM-CSF, G-CSF); immunosuppression (abnormal T cells drive many idiopathic cases); bone marrow transplant **as a last resort**.

**Recent change (aplastic anemia treatment order):** MSD now calls stem cell transplant curative and the treatment of choice "particularly in younger patients with a matched donor", so it is **first line**, not a last resort, for them. For adults, expert consensus gives "ATG, cyclosporine, and eltrombopag as initial management" (ATG = antithymocyte globulin) when transplant is not the first choice. MSD says about **half** of cases are idiopathic ([MSD: Aplastic Anemia, updated Apr 2026](https://msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-deficient-erythropoiesis/aplastic-anemia)).

### Pure red cell aplasia (p. 56)
- Only the **red cell line** fails; white cells and platelets are normal.
- Caused by **IgG autoantibodies or cytotoxic T cells** against red cell precursors; linked to **thymoma** and lymphocytic leukemias. Removing a thymoma can sometimes cure it.
- **Parvovirus B19** destroys **proerythroblasts**; confirm with **anti-B19 IgM**.
- Your notes: every patient should get a **chest CT** to look for thymoma.

**Other source (pure red cell aplasia):** MSD gets a chest CT for thymoma "in the absence of another identified cause"; after thymoma removal "most patients improve but are not always cured"; parvovirus-related cases (mostly in people with HIV or chronic hemolysis) get **IVIG**; drugs, including recombinant EPO and immune checkpoint inhibitors, are other causes ([MSD: Pure Red Blood Cell Aplasia, updated Apr 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/anemias-caused-by-deficient-erythropoiesis/pure-red-blood-cell-aplasia)).

## Clinical vignette
A 9-year-old boy with sickle cell disease has had a fever and a cold for 5 days. Now he is very pale and tired. Hb has fallen from his usual 8.5 to 4.1 g/dL, and the corrected reticulocyte count is 0.2%. His spleen is not palpable.
- **Diagnosis:** aplastic crisis from **parvovirus B19**.
- **Why:** parvovirus infects red cell precursors, and in a patient whose red cells already die fast, a short pause in production causes a steep fall; the near-zero reticulocyte count shows the marrow has stopped (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, p. 46; MSD Sickle Cell Disease).
- **Trap:** splenic sequestration, which also drops the Hb but has a **big spleen** and **high** reticulocytes (standard teaching).

## Common traps
- **Correct the reticulocyte count (× Hct/45) before judging the marrow.**
- **Big spleen + jaundice + gallstones = extravascular; hemoglobin in urine + no haptoglobin = intravascular.**
- **Spherocytes: Coombs negative = hereditary spherocytosis; Coombs positive = warm AIHA.**
- **Hemolysis + clots in odd veins + pancytopenia = PNH; test with flow cytometry.**
- **Bite cells and Heinz bodies after primaquine, dapsone, sulfa or fava beans = G6PD.** A normal G6PD level during the attack does not rule it out.
- **Cold fingers and anemia after *Mycoplasma* pneumonia = IgM cold agglutinins.**
- **Sickle trait: no anemia; hematuria and poor urine concentration only.**
- **Electrophoresis speed: A > S > C.**
- **Pancytopenia + dry tap + fatty marrow = aplastic anemia;** anemia alone with no reticulocytes after a virus = aplastic crisis.
- **Pure red cell aplasia in an adult: look for a thymoma.**

## Practice questions
1. A 22-year-old has anemia, jaundice and splenomegaly. Her mother had her spleen removed. The smear shows small round cells without central pallor; MCHC is high; the direct Coombs test is negative. Best confirmatory test?
   - **Answer:** osmotic fragility test (or reduced EMA binding).
   - **Why:** hereditary spherocytosis: membrane protein defect (spectrin, ankyrin, band 3), dominant inheritance, Coombs negative (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc, p. 44; MSD Hereditary Spherocytosis).
   - **Trap:** warm AIHA, which also shows spherocytes but has a **positive** Coombs test.
2. A 35-year-old has dark morning urine, pancytopenia and a new hepatic vein thrombosis (Budd-Chiari syndrome). Which protein anchor is missing on his blood cells?
   - **Answer:** the GPI anchor (so CD55 and CD59 are missing).
   - **Why:** PNH; complement destroys unprotected cells, and clots in the hepatic, portal or cerebral veins are the main cause of death (pp. 49-50).
   - **Trap:** factor V Leiden, which causes clots but not hemolysis or pancytopenia.
3. A soldier from Greece starts primaquine for malaria prevention and two days later has back pain and cola-colored urine. The smear shows cells with bites taken out of them. Which molecule is he short of?
   - **Answer:** NADPH (from G6PD), so glutathione cannot be kept reduced.
   - **Why:** oxidant drug + bite cells + hemoglobinuria = G6PD deficiency; the Mediterranean variant is severe (p. 50).
   - **Trap:** "glutathione synthase"; the problem is regenerating glutathione, not making it.
4. A 20-year-old with *Mycoplasma* pneumonia has anemia and painful blue fingertips in the cold. Which direct antiglobulin pattern is expected?
   - **Answer:** positive for complement (C3), negative for IgG.
   - **Why:** cold agglutinin disease: IgM binds at low temperature and fixes complement; the IgM falls off when warm, leaving C3 on the cell (standard teaching for the fall-off; p. 51; MSD AIHA for the pattern).
   - **Trap:** IgG-positive, which is warm AIHA.
5. A 6-month-old Black infant has painful swelling of both hands and feet. Hemoglobin electrophoresis shows HbS and HbF with no HbA. Which measure most reduces his risk of dying from pneumococcal sepsis in early childhood?
   - **Answer:** daily oral penicillin (plus pneumococcal vaccines).
   - **Why:** dactylitis is often the first sign of sickle cell disease; the spleen fails early, so encapsulated infections are a leading killer of children (pp. 46-47); MSD: penicillin from birth to at least 5 years.
   - **Trap:** hydroxyurea, which raises HbF and reduces pain crises but is not the main protection against pneumococcal sepsis.
6. A 24-year-old has fatigue, gum bleeding and recurrent infections 3 months after hepatitis. Hb 7, WBC 1,500, platelets 15,000, reticulocytes 0.1%. A marrow aspirate yields almost nothing. He has a matched sibling. Best treatment?
   - **Answer:** allogeneic stem cell (bone marrow) transplant.
   - **Why:** aplastic anemia after hepatitis (p. 55); MSD calls transplant the treatment of choice in younger patients with a matched donor.
   - **Trap:** "transplant as a last resort", which is out of date for young patients with a donor.
