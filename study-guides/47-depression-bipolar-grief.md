# Depression, bipolar disorder, grief and suicide risk

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the chapters on bipolar disorders (pp. 139-174) and depressive disorders (pp. 177-214), plus prolonged grief disorder (pp. 322-323). Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers. Drug facts come from Pharmacology High Yield (psychiatry drugs, PDF pp. 59-65) and are checked against the MSD Manual and FDA. DSM criteria are paraphrased here, not copied; check exact wording in the book. Where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

Abbreviations: **MDD** = major depressive disorder; **MDE** = major depressive episode; **PDD** = persistent depressive disorder; **PMDD** = premenstrual dysphoric disorder; **DMDD** = disruptive mood dysregulation disorder; **SSRI** = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor; **SNRI** = serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor; **TCA** = tricyclic antidepressant; **MAOI** = monoamine oxidase inhibitor; **NDRI** = norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor.

## In one line
Mood disorders are built from **episodes**. Learn the three episodes (**major depressive, manic, hypomanic**) by **how many symptoms, for how long, and how bad**. Then each disorder is just a combination: **any mania = bipolar I**; **hypomania plus major depression = bipolar II**; **depression only = MDD**.

## The three mood episodes
| | **Major depressive episode** | **Manic episode** | **Hypomanic episode** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minimum length | **2 weeks** | **1 week** (or **any length if hospitalized**) | **4 days in a row** |
| Symptom count | **5 of 9**, and one must be **depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure** | **3** extra symptoms (**4 if mood is only irritable**) | Same as mania: 3 (4 if only irritable) |
| How bad | Distress or impaired functioning | **Marked impairment, or hospitalization, or psychosis** | A clear change others can see, but **no marked impairment, no hospitalization, no psychosis** |
| Pages | p. 141 (and p. 183) | p. 140 | pp. 140-141 |

- **Depressive symptoms (p. 141):** depressed mood (irritable in children); loss of interest or pleasure; weight or appetite change (> 5% body weight in a month); too little or too much sleep; slowed or agitated movement that others can see; fatigue; worthlessness or guilt; poor concentration; thoughts of death or suicide. A common memory aid is **SIG E CAPS** (Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicide) plus depressed mood (standard teaching).
- **Manic symptoms (p. 140):** grandiosity; less need for sleep (DSM's example: feels rested after 3 hours); talking more or pressured speech; racing thoughts or flight of ideas; distractibility; more goal-directed activity or agitation; risky behavior such as spending sprees, sexual indiscretions, or bad investments. A common memory aid is **DIG FAST** (Distractibility, Impulsivity, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity, Sleep, Talkativeness) (standard teaching).
- **Psychosis rules out hypomania:** an episode with psychotic features is, by definition, not hypomanic but **manic** (p. 141). It still needs mania's time rule: **1 week, or any length only if hospitalization is necessary** (p. 140).
- **Antidepressant-triggered mania counts:** if a full manic episode starts on an antidepressant (or ECT) and persists at full strength after the drug's effect should be gone, it counts toward **bipolar I** (p. 140). One or two symptoms such as irritability after starting an antidepressant are not enough (p. 141).

## The disorders
| Disorder | What you need | Key exclusions | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bipolar I** | **At least one manic episode** in a lifetime. Depression is common but **not required** | Not better explained by a psychotic disorder | pp. 139-142 |
| **Bipolar II** | **At least one hypomanic episode + at least one major depressive episode** | **Never a manic episode** | pp. 139, 150 |
| **Cyclothymic disorder** | **2 years** (1 year in youth) of many hypomanic-type and depressive-type periods that never reach full episodes; symptoms present at least half the time, never gone > 2 months | Never met criteria for a major depressive, manic or hypomanic episode | p. 159 |
| **Major depressive disorder** | At least one MDE | **Never a manic or hypomanic episode** | pp. 183-184 |
| **Persistent depressive disorder** | Low mood on most days for **2 years** (1 year in children and teens, when irritability can stand in for low mood), plus **at least 2** of: appetite change, sleep change, low energy, low self-esteem, poor concentration, hopelessness; **no symptom-free stretch > 2 months** | Never manic or hypomanic. Full MDEs may also occur, and then both are diagnosed | pp. 193-194 |
| **Premenstrual dysphoric disorder** | In most cycles, **at least 5 symptoms in the last week before menses** that improve within days of menses and are minimal the week after. At least one is a core mood symptom (mood swings, irritability, depressed mood, anxiety) | Must be confirmed by **prospective daily ratings over at least 2 cycles** (diagnosis is "provisional" until then) | pp. 197-198 |
| **DMDD** | Severe temper outbursts **3 or more times a week** with **irritable mood between** outbursts, for **12 months or more**, in 2 of 3 settings | **First diagnosed only between ages 6 and 18; onset before age 10**. Cannot coexist with ODD, intermittent explosive disorder or bipolar disorder (if both DMDD and ODD fit, diagnose only DMDD) | p. 178 |

**Why DMDD exists:** it was added to DSM-5 so that children with **constant, non-episodic irritability** are not labeled as having bipolar disorder (pp. 178-179).

## Specifiers worth knowing (pp. 171, 210-214)
- **With melancholic features (pp. 211-212):** near-total loss of pleasure, or mood that does not lift even when something good happens, plus at least 3 of: a distinct empty or despairing mood, **worse in the morning**, **early-morning waking (at least 2 hours early)**, marked psychomotor change, weight loss, excessive guilt.
- **With atypical features (p. 212):** **mood still brightens** with good events, plus at least 2 of: **weight gain or bigger appetite**, **sleeping too much**, **"leaden paralysis"** (heavy arms or legs), long-standing **sensitivity to rejection**. "Atypical" does not mean uncommon (p. 212).
- **With psychotic features (pp. 212-213):** delusions or hallucinations. **Mood-congruent** content fits depressive themes such as guilt, disease, death or deserved punishment.
- **With peripartum onset (p. 213):** the episode starts **during pregnancy or within 4 weeks after delivery**. About **half** of "postpartum" depressions actually begin **before** delivery.
- **With seasonal pattern (p. 214):** for recurrent MDD. Episodes start and end at a regular time of year (usually fall or winter onset, spring remission), with **2 seasonal episodes in the last 2 years** and no non-seasonal ones in that time. Typical symptoms: low energy, too much sleep, overeating, carbohydrate craving.
- **With rapid cycling (p. 171):** **4 or more mood episodes in 12 months** in bipolar disorder. In bipolar I it is "a common variant associated with poorer outcomes" (p. 146).

## After a baby: blues, depression or psychosis
| | **Maternity ("baby") blues** | **Depression with peripartum onset** | **Postpartum psychosis** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How common | Very common (MSD) | Just under **7%** have an MDE between birth and 12 months (p. 213); MSD: **7%** in the first year | **1 in 500 to 1 in 1,000** deliveries (p. 213) |
| What it is | Sudden mood changes and tearfulness **without functional impairment**; **not a mental disorder**; improves quickly, within about a week (p. 213). MSD: usually 2-3 days, up to 2 weeks | A full MDE starting in pregnancy or within 4 weeks of delivery (p. 213) | A mood episode (often manic or mixed) with psychosis, **strongly tied to bipolar I** (p. 148) |
| Danger | None | Suicide | **Infanticide** (rare), linked to command hallucinations or delusions about the baby (p. 213) |
| Management | **Reassurance** | Antidepressants and psychotherapy (MSD) | **Psychiatric hospitalization and antipsychotics** (MSD) |

- After one postpartum psychotic episode, the risk of recurrence with each later delivery is **30-50%** (p. 213).
- Rule out **delirium** (fluctuating attention) and **thyroid disease** in the postpartum period (p. 213).

**Recent change (drugs for postpartum depression):** on 4 Aug 2023 the FDA approved **zuranolone (Zurzuvae)**, "the first oral medication indicated to treat postpartum depression (PPD) in adults". It is taken once daily in the evening for **14 days**, with a warning not to drive for at least 12 hours after a dose ([FDA press release, 4 Aug 2023](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-oral-treatment-postpartum-depression)). Before this, the only specific option was an IV infusion. MSD: the two approved drugs are "brexanolone given intravenously, and zuranolone given orally", both neurosteroids that act on **GABA-A receptors** ([MSD: Postpartum Depression, updated Jan 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gynecology-and-obstetrics/postpartum-care-and-associated-disorders/postpartum-depression)). The DSM-5-TR (2022) does not cover treatment.

## Grief: normal vs prolonged grief disorder (pp. 141-142, 322-323)
- **Normal grief can look like depression** (sadness, poor sleep and appetite, weight loss). DSM says to weigh whether a true MDE is **also** present, using clinical judgment and cultural norms (pp. 141-142).
- **Prolonged grief disorder** (new in DSM-5-TR, pp. 322-323):
  - The death was **at least 12 months ago** (**6 months** for children and adolescents).
  - Since the death: **intense yearning** for the person and/or **preoccupation** with them, nearly every day for at least the last month.
  - Plus **3 or more** of 8 symptoms, such as: feeling part of oneself has died, disbelief, avoiding reminders, intense emotional pain, trouble re-engaging with life, numbness, feeling life is meaningless, intense loneliness.
  - It goes beyond cultural norms and is not better explained by MDD or PTSD.

## Who gets these disorders
| | **MDD** | **Bipolar I** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 12-month prevalence (US) | About **7%**; 3 times higher at age 18-29 than at 60+ (pp. 187-188) | **1.5%** (p. 146) |
| Sex | **Women about 2 times men**, from puberty to menopause (p. 188) | About **equal** (lifetime ratio about 1.1:1) (p. 146) |
| Onset | Rises sharply at puberty; US incidence peaks in the 20s (p. 188) | Peak 20-30; US mean age 22 (p. 146) |
| Genetics | First-degree relatives at 2-4 times the risk; heritability about **40%** (p. 189) | Heritability about **90%** in some twin studies; first-degree relative risk **5-10%**; identical-twin concordance **40-70%** (p. 147) |
| Course | **40% begin to recover within 3 months, 80% within 1 year** (p. 188) | **More than 90%** of people with one manic episode have more episodes (p. 146) |

- New mania in **late middle age or older** should prompt a search for a **medical or drug cause** (for example frontotemporal neurocognitive disorder) (p. 146).
- Some apparent MDD turns out to be **bipolar** later, especially with **onset in adolescence, psychotic features, or a family history of bipolar disorder** (p. 188).
- Women with bipolar I have a high risk of mood episodes **after delivery**. Stopping medication during pregnancy raises relapse risk (p. 148).

## Suicide risk (pp. 148, 190)
- **Depression:** a **17-fold** higher suicide risk than the general population (p. 190).
- **Bipolar disorder:** lifetime suicide risk **20-30 times** the general population; an estimated **5-6%** die by suicide (p. 148).
- **A previous suicide attempt or threat is the risk factor reported most consistently**, yet most people who die by suicide had **no** earlier non-fatal attempt (p. 190).
- **Other risk factors (p. 190):** being single or living alone, social disconnection, early adversity, **access to a firearm**, sleep problems, **hopelessness**, anhedonia, and coexisting borderline personality disorder or substance use.
- **Women attempt suicide more often; men die by suicide more often** (pp. 148, 190).
- **Alcohol use disorder** affects nearly half of people with bipolar disorder and raises suicide risk (p. 148).

## Drugs

### Antidepressants (Pharmacology High Yield PDF pp. 59-62; MSD)
| Class | Examples | How it works | Classic side effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SSRI** | Fluoxetine (longest acting, least discontinuation), paroxetine, sertraline (safe in heart disease), citalopram (**QT prolongation**), escitalopram | Blocks serotonin reuptake | **Sexual dysfunction** (MSD: in more than half of patients), insomnia, nausea; serotonin syndrome with MAOIs (PDF p. 60; MSD) |
| **SNRI** | Venlafaxine (**raises blood pressure**), duloxetine (**neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia**) | Blocks serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake | Hypertension (PDF p. 62) |
| **TCA** | Amitriptyline (also migraine prevention, diabetic neuropathy), clomipramine (**OCD**), imipramine, nortriptyline | Blocks reuptake; also blocks **H1, muscarinic and α1** receptors | Sedation and weight gain (H1); **dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, fast heart rate** (muscarinic); **orthostatic hypotension** (α1). **Overdose: arrhythmia, seizures, coma, acidosis** (PDF p. 61) |
| **NDRI** | **Bupropion** (also smoking cessation, ADHD) | Boosts dopamine and norepinephrine; no serotonin effect (MSD) | **Seizures**; no sexual side effects (standard teaching). MSD: **contraindicated in bulimia** and seizure-prone patients (PDF p. 62) |
| **Mirtazapine** | | Blocks **α2** autoreceptors and **5-HT2 and 5-HT3** receptors (MSD) | **Sedation and weight gain** (H1); **little sexual dysfunction** (PDF p. 60; MSD) |
| **Trazodone** | | Mainly blocks 5-HT2 (MSD) | Very sedating (used at bedtime); **priapism**; orthostatic hypotension (MSD) |
| **MAOI** | Phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline patch | Blocks monoamine oxidase | **Hypertensive crisis** with sympathomimetics and serotonin syndrome with SSRIs (MSD); with **tyramine** in aged foods it is the "cheese reaction" (standard teaching) |

**Correction (mirtazapine):** Pharmacology High Yield says mirtazapine "Blocks 5HT1A + Alpha 2" (PDF p. 60). MSD says it "blocks alpha-2 adrenergic autoreceptors, as well as 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 receptors", causing sedation and weight gain through H1 blockade ([MSD: Medications for Treatment of Depression, updated May 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/mood-disorders/medications-for-treatment-of-depression)). Mirtazapine does **not** block 5-HT1A. The drug that acts at 5-HT1A is **buspirone**, which her notes call a 5-HT1A agonist (PDF p. 59); it is a partial agonist (standard teaching).

**Other source (antidepressant use):** from MSD.
- Response usually takes **about 2-3 weeks** (range 4 days to 8 weeks).
- Antidepressants "may increase the risk of suicidal thinking and behavior" in "pediatric and young adult patients."
- **Taper SSRIs** (about 25% a week) to avoid discontinuation symptoms (dizziness, nausea, irritability, flu-like aches); the risk is higher with shorter half-lives.
- After remission, continue treatment for **6-12 months** (up to 2 years in people over 50) to prevent relapse.
- Her notes list vilazodone and vortioxetine under SNRIs (PDF p. 62). MSD lists **vilazodone among the SSRIs**.

**Recent change (newer depression drugs):** MSD now lists **esketamine** nasal spray (an NMDA-receptor drug) as "FDA-approved as therapy for treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder with suicidality". It also lists **dextromethorphan-bupropion**: dextromethorphan blocks NMDA receptors, and bupropion slows its breakdown by inhibiting CYP2D6. Her notes do not include these.

### Serotonin syndrome (PDF p. 60; MSD)
- **Cause:** too much serotonin, classically an **SSRI combined with an MAOI** (PDF p. 60; MSD).
- **Signs:** MSD lists "mental status changes, hyperthermia" with autonomic and neuromuscular overactivity. Her notes list severe anxiety, diarrhea and vomiting. Tremor, **clonus** and **hyperreflexia** are the classic neuromuscular signs (standard teaching).
- **Treatment:** stop the drugs; give **cyproheptadine** (PDF p. 60), which blocks 5-HT2 (and H1) receptors (PDF p. 58).

### Mood stabilizers (Pharmacology High Yield PDF pp. 63-65; MSD)
- **Lithium:**
  - **Narrow therapeutic index** (PDF p. 63). Her notes give target levels of 0.5-0.8 mEq/L for maintenance and 0.8-1.2 mEq/L for acute mania, with toxicity above 1.5 mEq/L and dialysis at about 4 mEq/L.
  - **Toxicity:** MSD: early signs are "gross tremor, increased deep tendon reflexes, persistent headache, vomiting, and confusion", progressing to stupor, seizures and arrhythmias.
  - **Raises lithium levels:** **thiazides, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs** (MSD), and **low sodium or dehydration** (PDF p. 63). The body holds on to lithium the way it holds on to sodium in the proximal tubule when volume is low (standard teaching).
  - **Side effects (her "Li-THIUM" list, PDF p. 63):** more white cells, **tremor**, **hypothyroidism**, **increased urination** (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, treated with **amiloride**), and in pregnancy **Ebstein anomaly** (a heart defect). MSD adds worse **acne and psoriasis** and kidney damage after ≥ 15 years.
  - **Monitoring (MSD):** lithium level every 6 months and after dose changes; TSH, BUN and creatinine; yearly calcium and PTH.
  - **Benefit:** her notes call lithium the mood stabilizer with an **anti-suicide** effect (PDF p. 64 question).
- **Valproate:** "a 1 to 4% risk of neural tube defects", so avoid it in pregnancy; hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis (MSD).
- **Carbamazepine:** aplastic anemia and agranulocytosis (MSD); SJS risk with **HLA-B\*15:02** (see guide 45).
- **Lamotrigine:** helps **bipolar depression** (PDF p. 63; MSD); risk of **SJS** if the dose is raised too fast (MSD).
- **Second-generation antipsychotics:** used for acute mania (for example olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, quetiapine). Quetiapine also treats bipolar depression (MSD; her notes on PDF p. 63 list lamotrigine for bipolar depression).
- **Acute mania:** lithium takes **4-10 days** to work, so antipsychotics or valproate are often started first (MSD).
- **Antidepressants in bipolar disorder:** MSD: "not recommended as monotherapy for depressive episodes", because they can trigger mania.

## Clinical vignette
A 24-year-old graduate student is brought in by her roommate. For 8 days she has slept 2-3 hours a night yet feels "amazing". She talks so fast that she is hard to interrupt and jumps between ideas. She has spent $8,000 on equipment for a business she plans to launch next week, and she believes she has been chosen to "fix the economy". Six months ago she was treated for 3 weeks of low mood, poor sleep and guilt. Grandiosity, little sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, risky spending and a **delusion** add up to a **manic episode**: it has lasted more than a week (p. 140), and the delusion rules out hypomania (p. 141). The diagnosis is **bipolar I disorder, current episode manic, with psychotic features**. A second-generation antipsychotic or valproate works faster than lithium for acute mania (MSD). An antidepressant alone would be the wrong choice.

## Common traps
- **Any manic episode ever = bipolar I**, even if there has never been depression.
- **Mania ≥ 1 week, or any length if hospitalized; hypomania ≥ 4 days.** Hospitalization waives the 1-week rule (p. 140); psychosis rules out hypomania but does not waive the week (p. 141).
- **Bipolar II is not "mild bipolar"**: it needs a major depressive episode, and DSM says it is not less severe (p. 139).
- **PDD = 2 years** (1 year in kids); **cyclothymia = 2 years of sub-threshold highs and lows**.
- **DMDD: diagnose at 6-18, onset before 10**, constant irritability between outbursts. **It is not pediatric bipolar disorder**, and it overrides ODD.
- **PMDD needs prospective daily ratings for 2 cycles.**
- **Peripartum onset = pregnancy or the first 4 weeks after delivery. Blues = no impairment, gone in about a week.**
- **Prolonged grief disorder = 12 months (6 in kids)** after the death, with yearning or preoccupation.
- **Strongest suicide risk factor = a previous attempt.** Women attempt more; men complete more.
- **Lithium + thiazide, ACE inhibitor, NSAID or dehydration → toxicity.** Polyuria = nephrogenic DI → amiloride.
- **Pregnancy: lithium → Ebstein anomaly; valproate → neural tube defects.**
- **Bupropion: no sexual side effects, but avoid in seizures and bulimia.** **Mirtazapine: appetite and sleep.**

## Practice questions

1. A 35-year-old man has had three episodes of depressed mood, poor sleep and low energy, each lasting about a month. His wife reports that last spring, for 5 days, he slept 4 hours a night, talked more than usual, started several home projects and seemed unusually cheerful. He kept working normally, and coworkers commented that he seemed "full of energy". He has never been hospitalized or had psychotic symptoms. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **bipolar II disorder**.
   - **Why:** major depressive episodes plus a hypomanic episode (at least 4 days, noticeable to others, without marked impairment, hospitalization or psychosis), and no manic episode (pp. 140-141, 150).
   - **Trap:** **bipolar I disorder**, which requires a manic episode (at least 1 week, or marked impairment, hospitalization or psychosis).

2. A 27-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department after 3 days without sleep, during which she spoke rapidly, gave away her savings and tried to climb onto the roof to "talk to the city". She is admitted to an inpatient psychiatric unit for her safety. Urine drug screen and thyroid tests are normal. She has never been depressed. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **bipolar I disorder, current episode manic**.
   - **Why:** a manic episode can be of **any duration if hospitalization is needed**, and one lifetime manic episode is enough for bipolar I; depression is not required (pp. 139-140).
   - **Trap:** **a hypomanic episode**, which needs at least 4 days and cannot involve hospitalization or marked impairment (pp. 140-141).

3. A 42-year-old accountant says she has "always been a gloomy person". For at least the past 3 years she has felt down most days, with low energy, poor self-esteem and trouble making decisions. She has never felt normal for more than a few weeks at a time. She has never had 2 weeks with enough symptoms for a major depressive episode, and she has never had elevated mood. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **persistent depressive disorder**.
   - **Why:** low mood on most days for 2 years or longer, plus at least 2 associated symptoms, with no symptom-free stretch longer than 2 months (pp. 193-194).
   - **Trap:** **major depressive disorder**, which requires a 2-week episode with at least 5 symptoms.

4. A 29-year-old woman has felt depressed for 2 months. She sleeps 11-12 hours a day, has gained 6 kg from eating more, and says her arms and legs feel "heavy as lead". Her mood lifts for a while when friends visit. She has always been very hurt by perceived rejection. Which specifier best describes her depressive episode?
   - **Answer:** **with atypical features**.
   - **Why:** mood reactivity plus at least 2 of: increased appetite or weight gain, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, long-standing rejection sensitivity (p. 212).
   - **Trap:** **with melancholic features**, in which mood does not brighten and patients wake early and lose weight (pp. 211-212).

5. A 26-year-old woman delivered a healthy baby 3 days ago. She has been tearful several times a day and feels overwhelmed, but she is sleeping when the baby sleeps, eating well, bonding with and caring for the baby, and has no thoughts of harming herself or the baby. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **reassurance that this is likely to resolve on its own within about 1-2 weeks**, with follow-up.
   - **Why:** maternity blues cause sudden tearfulness without functional impairment, are not a mental disorder and improve quickly (p. 213; MSD: usually 2-3 days, up to 2 weeks).
   - **Trap:** **starting an SSRI**, which is for depression with peripartum onset (a full MDE).

6. A 9-year-old boy has had explosive temper outbursts, including screaming and throwing furniture, about four times a week for the past 18 months, at home and at school. Between outbursts he is angry and irritable almost every day. The outbursts began at age 7. He has never had a period of elevated mood or decreased need for sleep. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **disruptive mood dysregulation disorder**.
   - **Why:** severe outbursts at least 3 times a week, persistent irritability between them, at least 12 months, 2 settings, onset before 10, diagnosed between 6 and 18 (p. 178).
   - **Trap:** **bipolar I disorder**, which requires a distinct manic episode; DMDD was created for chronic, non-episodic irritability (pp. 178-179).

7. A 31-year-old woman reports irritability, mood swings, anxiety, bloating and food cravings that begin about a week before each period, are worst at the onset of menses, and are gone a few days later. The symptoms strain her marriage and work. She has no symptoms during the week after menses. Which is required to confirm the diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **prospective daily symptom ratings over at least 2 menstrual cycles**.
   - **Why:** DSM-5-TR requires the timing in PMDD to be confirmed by daily ratings over at least 2 symptomatic cycles; until then the diagnosis is "provisional" (pp. 197-198).
   - **Trap:** **serum estradiol and progesterone levels**, which do not establish the diagnosis (standard teaching).

8. A 58-year-old man has taken lithium for bipolar I disorder for 10 years. He urinates large volumes day and night and is always thirsty. Serum sodium is high-normal, urine osmolality is low and does not rise after desmopressin, and his lithium level is in the target range. Which drug is most appropriate for his urinary symptoms?
   - **Answer:** **amiloride**.
   - **Why:** lithium causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (kidneys don't respond to ADH), and amiloride treats it (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 63). Amiloride blocks the ENaC channel through which lithium enters collecting duct cells (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **desmopressin**, which works only in central diabetes insipidus.

9. A 50-year-old woman with bipolar I disorder has been stable on lithium for 4 years. Two weeks ago her primary care physician started a new medication for blood pressure, and since then her urinary calcium excretion has fallen. Now she has vomiting, a coarse tremor, unsteady gait and confusion. Which medication was most likely started?
   - **Answer:** **hydrochlorothiazide**.
   - **Why:** thiazides raise lithium levels (MSD; Pharmacology High Yield PDF pp. 63, 65); the mild volume loss makes the proximal tubule reabsorb more sodium and lithium (standard teaching for the mechanism).
   - **Trap:** **lisinopril**. ACE inhibitors also raise lithium levels (MSD), but only a thiazide lowers urinary calcium, because it increases calcium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule (standard teaching).

10. A 45-year-old man taking phenelzine for depression is started on another drug by a new physician. Within hours he is agitated, sweating and confused, with a temperature of 39.5 °C, diarrhea, hyperreflexia and inducible ankle clonus. Besides stopping the drugs and cooling him, which medication is most appropriate?
    - **Answer:** **cyproheptadine**.
    - **Why:** this is serotonin syndrome, classically from an MAOI combined with a serotonergic drug (MSD; PDF p. 60). Cyproheptadine blocks 5-HT2 receptors (PDF p. 58).
    - **Trap:** **dantrolene**, which is used for **malignant hyperthermia** after anesthesia (standard teaching).

11. A 79-year-old widow has lost 5 kg because she has no appetite, sleeps only 3-4 hours a night, and meets criteria for major depressive disorder. Her physician chooses an antidepressant that is expected to improve her sleep and appetite and has little effect on sexual function. This drug works mainly by blocking which receptor?
    - **Answer:** **α2-adrenergic receptors** (the drug is mirtazapine).
    - **Why:** MSD: mirtazapine "blocks alpha-2 adrenergic autoreceptors, as well as 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 receptors", and its H1 blockade causes sedation and weight gain.
    - **Trap:** **5-HT1A receptors**, which are the target of **buspirone** (a 5-HT1A agonist, PDF p. 59). Her notes list mirtazapine as a 5-HT1A blocker, which is incorrect (see Correction).

12. A 33-year-old woman with major depressive disorder stopped sertraline because of sexual side effects, and she also wants help quitting smoking. Her physician considers bupropion. Which condition in her history would most strongly argue against this choice?
    - **Answer:** **bulimia nervosa**.
    - **Why:** MSD says bupropion is contraindicated in bulimia or seizure-prone patients, because it lowers the seizure threshold (Pharmacology High Yield PDF p. 62 lists seizures).
    - **Trap:** **erectile or orgasmic problems on SSRIs**, which are a reason to **choose** bupropion, since it has little sexual effect (standard teaching).

13. A 52-year-old man with major depressive disorder is seen after his antidepressant dose is increased. He lives alone since his divorce, drinks alcohol daily and has trouble sleeping. Two years ago he took an overdose of acetaminophen. Which factor in his history is the most consistently described risk factor for death by suicide?
    - **Answer:** **his previous suicide attempt**.
    - **Why:** DSM-5-TR calls a past history of suicide attempts or threats "the most consistently described risk factor", while noting that most suicide deaths are not preceded by a non-fatal attempt (p. 190).
    - **Trap:** **living alone**, which is a real risk factor (p. 190) but not the most consistent one.

14. A 70-year-old man's wife died 16 months ago. Nearly every day for the past several months he has longed intensely for her, and he says "a part of me died with her". He cannot believe she is gone, avoids the room where she died, feels life has no meaning and has stopped seeing friends. His family and religious community say his grief is far beyond what they expect. He does not have pervasive guilt, worthlessness or suicidal thoughts, and he does not meet criteria for a major depressive episode. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
    - **Answer:** **prolonged grief disorder**.
    - **Why:** the death was at least 12 months ago, with intense yearning plus at least 3 symptoms (identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance, meaninglessness, difficulty re-engaging), beyond cultural norms, and not better explained by MDD (pp. 322-323).
    - **Trap:** **normal (uncomplicated) bereavement**, which does not cause this degree of persistent impairment beyond cultural norms a year or more after the death (pp. 322-323).
