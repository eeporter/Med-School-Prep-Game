# Multiple sclerosis, other demyelinating disease, and myasthenia gravis vs Lambert-Eaton

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (MS and other demyelinating diseases pp. 50, 156-163), El-Husseiny's Essentials of MSK (neuromuscular junction pp. 157-160) and the Osmosis High-Yield Neurology notes (myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where a newer or different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
**Central** myelin is made by oligodendrocytes, so diseases that hit them (MS, PML, osmotic demyelination) cause **upper motor neuron and brain signs**. **Peripheral** myelin is made by Schwann cells, and its diseases (such as Guillain-Barré, guide 25) cause **lower motor neuron signs**. At the neuromuscular junction, myasthenia gravis gets **worse** with use (postsynaptic receptors are blocked) and Lambert-Eaton gets **better** with brief effort (presynaptic release is low).

## Core mechanism
1. **Multiple sclerosis (MS):** patches (plaques) of demyelination with destruction of oligodendroglia, mainly in white matter. Diagnosis needs CNS lesions **separated in time and space**. [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms) Schwann cells myelinate peripheral nerves (one axon each) and oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons (many each); the optic nerve is the only cranial nerve myelinated by oligodendrocytes, which is why it is the one cranial nerve MS hits (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 50, 139).
2. **Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML):** reactivation of **JC virus**, which infects oligodendrocytes. [MSD Manual: PML](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/brain-infections/progressive-multifocal-leukoencephalopathy-pml)
3. **Osmotic demyelination syndrome:** can follow **too-rapid correction of hyponatremia**, classically in the **pons**, "but other areas of the brain can also be affected" (extrapontine). Severe cases can leave a **locked-in** state: awake, but able to communicate only "by vertical eye movements controlled above the pons". [MSD Manual: Hyponatremia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/electrolyte-disorders/hyponatremia) Why, in your notes: a fast rise in blood tonicity pulls water out of brain cells, and the shrinking damages **astrocytes and oligodendrocytes**; extrapontine sites include the basal ganglia and cerebral white matter; the picture is **quadriplegia** (corticospinal tracts) and **pseudobulbar palsy** (corticobulbar tracts). Mnemonic: "from low to high, your pons will die; from high to low, your brains will blow" (correcting high sodium too fast causes brain swelling). (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 160-161) That the brain cells had first shed solutes to adapt to low sodium is standard teaching.
4. **Myasthenia gravis:** an autoimmune attack on **postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors**. About 85% of generalized cases have acetylcholine receptor antibodies; 30-60% of the rest have anti-MuSK antibodies. [MSD Manual: Myasthenia Gravis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/myasthenia-gravis)
5. **Lambert-Eaton syndrome:** less acetylcholine is released, because **presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels** are lost. [MSD Manual: Disorders of Neuromuscular Transmission](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/disorders-of-neuromuscular-transmission) Your notes: **antibodies against the presynaptic calcium channels** (a type II hypersensitivity) cut acetylcholine release; it is usually a paraneoplastic syndrome from **small cell lung cancer** and improves when the cancer is removed (El-Husseiny MSK, p. 160; Osmosis HY Neurology, "Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome").

## Multiple sclerosis
- **Pathology in your notes:** active plaques have **perivenular** T cells and macrophages attacking myelin; the blood-brain barrier is damaged; macrophages fill with myelin debris; B cells make myelin antibodies; axons are mostly spared, and old plaques leave astrocyte scars (gliosis). Demyelination slows conduction because saltatory conduction fails. Suspect MS when deficits can't be explained by one lesion. (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 156-157)
- **Who:** women somewhat more often (your notes: women in their 20s and 30s, p. 156); onset typically age 20-40; more common in people who grew up in temperate climates (about 1 in 2,000) than in the tropics (about 1 in 10,000). [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms)
- **Classic findings:** one-sided or uneven **optic neuritis**; **internuclear ophthalmoplegia** (a medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion, typically on both sides in MS); **Lhermitte sign** (an electric shock down the spine when the neck bends); heat worsening symptoms (**Uhthoff phenomenon**); tingling and patchy sensory loss; **bladder dysfunction**. [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms)
- **Findings in your notes** (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 157-158):
  - **Optic neuritis:** painful vision loss in one eye with a **Marcus Gunn pupil** (relative afferent pupillary defect). On the swinging flashlight test, both pupils constrict when light hits the normal eye, and both seem to dilate when it swings to the affected eye.
  - **Internuclear ophthalmoplegia:** the heavily myelinated MLF links the abducens and oculomotor nuclei. With an MLF lesion the eye cannot adduct on side gaze, but **convergence is intact**, and there is no ptosis or big pupil (which would mean a CN III lesion).
  - Brainstem and cerebellar signs: intention tremor, scanning speech, ataxia, vertigo, nystagmus. Weakness, spasticity, sensory loss, bowel and bladder problems.
  - **Fatigue** is the most common and least specific symptom, worse after a hot shower because heat slows conduction.
  - Your notes' "SIN" list: **S**canning speech, **I**ntention tremor, **I**ncontinence, **I**nternuclear ophthalmoplegia, **N**ystagmus (the classic Charcot triad is scanning speech, intention tremor and nystagmus).
- **Tests:** MRI lesions in typical places (periventricular, cortical or juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord, optic nerve); CSF **oligoclonal IgG bands** and raised IgG. Periventricular lesions on sagittal FLAIR MRI are "sometimes called Dawson fingers". [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms) That they point outward from the ventricles along small veins is standard teaching. Your notes call **oligoclonal bands** diagnostic and **MRI** the gold standard, with white matter plaques favoring the periventricular regions (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 158). **Other source:** MSD bases the diagnosis on lesions separated in time and space, with CSF findings as one of the tests.
- **Course patterns:** relapsing-remitting, secondary progressive, primary progressive and progressive relapsing. [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms) Relapsing-remitting being the most common is standard teaching. Note: the 2013 Lublin revision dropped "progressive relapsing": adding disease activity as a modifier "allows elimination of the progressive relapsing (PR) category", so newer questions may not use it. [Lublin FD et al. Neurology 2014;83(3):278-286, doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000000560, as summarized by Mount Sinai, 2014](https://www.mountsinai.org/about/newsroom/2014/mount-sinai-researchers-lead-international-advisory-committee-to-define-the-clinical-course-of-multiple-sclerosis)
- **Treatment:** short courses (3-5 days) of glucocorticoids for disabling relapses. Long-term disease-modifying drugs include interferon beta and glatiramer (moderate efficacy), and ocrelizumab (anti-CD20, high efficacy). **Natalizumab** may raise the risk of **PML**. Spasticity: baclofen or tizanidine. [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms) Your notes: disease-modifying drugs are interferon beta, glatiramer and natalizumab; **IV steroids** for flares; bladder: catheterization and antimuscarinics; spasticity: **baclofen** (a GABA-B agonist); pain: tricyclics and anticonvulsants (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 159).

## Compare: other CNS demyelinating diseases
| Disease | Cause or marker | Key exam clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder | **Aquaporin-4 IgG** (NMO-IgG) | Severe optic neuritis, often both eyes and the chiasm; transverse myelitis over **3 or more** spinal segments; few brain white matter lesions (MS usually involves a single cord segment) |
| PML | JC virus (a **polyomavirus**) in oligodendrocytes | Advanced HIV (most common risk), leukemia or lymphoma, **natalizumab**, rituximab; rapidly progressive and usually fatal (El-Husseiny p. 162); clumsiness then weakness, aphasia, vision loss; white matter lesions on MRI; CSF PCR for JC virus DNA |
| Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) | After an infection (often measles or varicella) or some vaccines (rabies, smallpox in your notes) | Rapid, multifocal deficits and altered mental status; periventricular inflammation and demyelination (El-Husseiny p. 161) |
| Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE) | Measles virus that persists in the brain (a variant missing the **M protein** escapes clearance) | Years after measles in childhood: personality change, seizures, **myoclonus**, spasticity, coma (El-Husseiny p. 161) |
| Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (peripheral) | Autosomal dominant defect in **myelin** genes (hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy) | Distal weakness and sensory loss, **"inverted champagne bottle"** legs, **foot drop**, **pes cavus** and hammer toes, low reflexes; no treatment (El-Husseiny pp. 162-163) |
| Osmotic demyelination | Sodium corrected too fast | Alcohol use disorder or undernutrition; days to weeks later: flaccid paralysis, slurred speech, trouble swallowing; pons, sometimes other brain areas; can cause locked-in syndrome |

Sources: [MSD Manual: NMOSD](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/neuromyelitis-optica-spectrum-disorder-nmosd), [MSD Manual: PML](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/brain-infections/progressive-multifocal-leukoencephalopathy-pml), [MSD Manual: Hyponatremia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/electrolyte-disorders/hyponatremia)

- Leukodystrophies (Krabbe, metachromatic, adrenoleukodystrophy) are in your biochemistry notes and will go in an enzyme-deficiency guide (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 163).
- **PML treatment** is mainly supportive. Stop natalizumab or other immune-suppressing drugs. In advanced HIV, antiretroviral therapy raised 1-year survival from 10% to 50%, but it can trigger immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome. [MSD Manual: PML](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/brain-infections/progressive-multifocal-leukoencephalopathy-pml)
- **Safe sodium correction:** a rise of **8 mEq/L or less over the first 24 hours**. [MSD Manual: Hyponatremia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/electrolyte-disorders/hyponatremia) Worked example: a patient with sodium 110 mEq/L should be no higher than 110 + 8 = 118 mEq/L at 24 hours.

## Compare: myasthenia gravis vs Lambert-Eaton
| Feature | Myasthenia gravis | Lambert-Eaton |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | **Postsynaptic** acetylcholine receptor | **Presynaptic** P/Q-type calcium channel, so less acetylcholine release |
| Pattern | Worse with use, better with rest; fluctuates over minutes to days; **ptosis and double vision** most common; bulbar symptoms in some (more often with anti-MuSK). Your notes: chewing fatigue, nasal speech, "weaker at the end of the day"; later, proximal limb weakness; more common in women (El-Husseiny MSK, pp. 157-158). Osmosis: two age peaks, 20-30 (mostly women) and 60-70 (mostly men) | **Proximal** weakness (hips, thighs, shoulders) that **improves with use** ("warming up"); **reflexes are reduced** and recover after muscle activation; **dry mouth** is the most common autonomic symptom, with constipation and erectile dysfunction (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome"; El-Husseiny MSK, p. 160). **Other source:** your El-Husseiny notes say the eyes are usually spared but also that over 60% have some eye or bulbar involvement; Osmosis says only a small minority do |
| Associated with | **Thymic hyperplasia (65%)**, **thymoma (10%**; about half malignant) | **Small cell lung cancer** (50-60% go on to develop it) |
| Repetitive nerve stimulation | **Decrease** of more than 10% (in 60%) with slow stimulation | **Increase** with fast stimulation or after 10 seconds of exercise, up to 400%; more than 100% is diagnostic of a presynaptic disorder |
| Treatment | **Pyridostigmine** (symptoms only); immunosuppressants; thymectomy (all thymomas; under 65 with generalized disease); IVIG or plasma exchange for crisis. Your notes also list steroids and plasmapheresis (El-Husseiny MSK, p. 158) | Treat the cancer; cholinesterase inhibitors help little (negative edrophonium test); **aminopyridines** block potassium channels, prolonging depolarization so more calcium enters and more acetylcholine is released; then immunosuppression or IVIG/plasma exchange if needed (Osmosis HY Neurology; El-Husseiny MSK, p. 160). The drug name amifampridine is standard teaching |

**Read more on EyeWiki:** [Myasthenia Gravis](https://eyewiki.org/Myasthenia_Gravis) (links for extra reading only; facts above come from the cited sources)

Sources: [MSD Manual: Myasthenia Gravis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/myasthenia-gravis), [MSD Manual: Disorders of Neuromuscular Transmission](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/disorders-of-neuromuscular-transmission)

- **How the antibodies work (your notes):** a **type II hypersensitivity**. Antibodies block the receptor, make the cell take it in and break it down, and fix complement that damages the end plate. With fewer receptors, the end-plate potential no longer reaches threshold. More acetylcholine (from a cholinesterase inhibitor) can partly overcome this. (El-Husseiny MSK, p. 157) Thymic hyperplasia or thymoma is common; the thymus (and inferior parathyroids) come from the **3rd pharyngeal pouch** (El-Husseiny Endocrinology, pp. 3, 7). **Correction:** your MSK notes say "3rd pharyngeal arches" (p. 159); your Endocrinology notes give the pouch, which is right.
- **Myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis (your notes, El-Husseiny MSK, p. 159):**

  | | Myasthenic crisis | Cholinergic crisis |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Cause | Too **little** cholinesterase inhibitor | Too **much** cholinesterase inhibitor |
  | Clue | Worse weakness | Weakness with **muscle twitching** (and muscarinic signs) |
  | Edrophonium | Improves | Does not improve |
  | Treat | Protect breathing; more drug, IVIG or plasma exchange | Protect breathing; stop the drug; **atropine** |

  Muscarinic side effects of cholinesterase inhibitors can be blocked with glycopyrrolate, hyoscyamine or propantheline (p. 159).
- **Anesthesia:** patients with myasthenia are very **sensitive to nondepolarizing blockers** such as vecuronium, because few receptors are left; tiny doses can paralyze (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 237).
- **Drugs that can worsen myasthenia:** aminoglycosides, quinine, magnesium sulfate, procainamide, calcium channel blockers and immune checkpoint inhibitors. [MSD Manual: Myasthenia Gravis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/myasthenia-gravis)
- **Bedside clue:** the ice pack test. Ice on closed eyes for 2 minutes improves myasthenic ptosis. **Recent change:** your notes use the **edrophonium (Tensilon) test**, where a short-acting cholinesterase inhibitor briefly restores strength (El-Husseiny MSK, p. 158). MSD says edrophonium testing is no longer routine; antibody tests, the ice pack test and nerve studies are used instead. [MSD Manual: Myasthenia Gravis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/myasthenia-gravis) Your notes call electromyography with repetitive stimulation the most accurate test (p. 158).
- **Botulism** also blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release: the toxin binds irreversibly to presynaptic cholinergic endings. [MSD Manual: Disorders of Neuromuscular Transmission](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/disorders-of-neuromuscular-transmission) How: the toxin is a protease that cuts SNARE proteins, so acetylcholine vesicles cannot fuse with the membrane (standard teaching).
  - **Pattern:** "bilateral and symmetric, beginning with the cranial nerves and followed by descending weakness or paralysis": double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech and trouble swallowing first, with a diminished or lost pupillary light reflex. There are no sensory changes, and the mind stays clear. [MSD Manual: Botulism](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/anaerobic-bacteria/botulism)
  - **Infants:** swallowed spores grow in the gut and make toxin there. "The source of the spores is usually unknown", but "some cases have been traced to ingestion of honey". Constipation comes first in 90%, then a weak cry, poor suck and a "floppy baby". Infants under 12 months should not be fed honey. Treatment is human botulism immune globulin (BabyBIG). [MSD Manual: Botulism](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/anaerobic-bacteria/botulism); [MSD Manual: Infant Botulism](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/anaerobic-bacteria/infant-botulism)
  - **Botulism vs Guillain-Barré:** botulism descends from the cranial nerves; Guillain-Barré usually ascends from the legs (guide 25).

## Clinical vignette
A 28-year-old woman has blurred vision and pain on moving her right eye that improved over a month. A year later she has numbness in her legs, and an electric shock runs down her back when she bends her neck. On looking left, her right eye does not move inward, and the left eye shows nystagmus. What is the diagnosis, and what will CSF show?

**Answer:** multiple sclerosis; CSF oligoclonal IgG bands.
**Why:** optic neuritis, Lhermitte sign and internuclear ophthalmoplegia (medial longitudinal fasciculus) are separate CNS events in time and space in a young woman. [MSD Manual: Multiple Sclerosis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/demyelinating-disorders/multiple-sclerosis-ms) The pattern of internuclear ophthalmoplegia (the eye on the lesion side can't adduct, the other eye has nystagmus) is standard teaching.
**Trap:** neuromyelitis optica. Look for severe optic neuritis and a long spinal cord lesion with aquaporin-4 antibodies.

## Common traps
- Oligodendrocytes are CNS myelin; Schwann cells are peripheral myelin; CN II is the only cranial nerve with CNS myelin (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 50, 139).
- INO: no adduction on side gaze, but convergence works and there is no ptosis (El-Husseiny p. 158).
- Crisis in a treated myasthenia patient with twitching and drooling = **cholinergic** crisis; give atropine, not more drug (El-Husseiny MSK p. 159).
- Child with personality change and myoclonus years after measles = **SSPE** (El-Husseiny p. 161).
- Foot drop, high arches and stork-like legs in a family = **Charcot-Marie-Tooth** (pp. 162-163).
- MS on natalizumab plus new clumsiness or aphasia = PML (JC virus).
- Hyponatremia corrected by more than 8 mEq/L in 24 hours, then trouble speaking and swallowing = osmotic demyelination of the pons.
- Myasthenia: ptosis that gets worse through the day, with normal reflexes and sensation (normal reflexes are standard teaching). Look for a thymoma on chest CT.
- Lambert-Eaton: think small cell lung cancer, and strength improves with effort.
- Floppy infant with constipation and a weak cry after honey = infant botulism, not myasthenia.
- Gentamicin or IV magnesium can unmask or worsen myasthenia.

## Practice questions
1. A 30-year-old with MS on natalizumab develops progressive clumsiness, right-sided weakness and trouble finding words. MRI shows new white matter lesions without mass effect. What test confirms the diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** CSF PCR for JC virus DNA (PML).
   - **Why:** natalizumab is the drug most often linked to PML; clumsiness and hemiparesis with white matter lesions fit, and a positive CSF PCR with matching imaging is nearly diagnostic. [MSD Manual: PML](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/brain-infections/progressive-multifocal-leukoencephalopathy-pml)
   - **Trap:** calling it an MS relapse and giving steroids.
2. A man with alcohol use disorder arrives with sodium 108 mEq/L. Twenty-four hours later it is 126 mEq/L. Three days later he cannot speak or swallow and has weakness of all four limbs. What happened?
   - **Answer:** osmotic demyelination syndrome from overly fast correction: 126 − 108 = 18 mEq/L in 24 hours, more than twice the 8 mEq/L limit.
   - **Why:** [MSD Manual: Hyponatremia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/nephrology/electrolyte-disorders/hyponatremia) (too-rapid correction, alcohol use disorder as a risk, pontine demyelination, ≤ 8 mEq/L in the first 24 hours).
   - **Trap:** a new stroke. The timing after correction and the risk group point to osmotic demyelination.
3. A 35-year-old woman has double vision and drooping eyelids that are worse in the evening. Chest CT shows an anterior mediastinal mass. What is the target of her antibodies, and what is the mass?
   - **Answer:** postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors; a thymoma.
   - **Why:** myasthenia gravis weakens with use and fluctuates; 10% have a thymoma, and all thymomas should be removed. [MSD Manual: Myasthenia Gravis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/myasthenia-gravis) The thymus sitting in the anterior mediastinum is standard teaching.
   - **Trap:** Lambert-Eaton, which goes with small cell lung cancer, not a thymoma.
4. A 62-year-old smoker has weak hip muscles. With fast repetitive nerve stimulation, the muscle response grows by 250%. Where is the defect?
   - **Answer:** presynaptic: loss of P/Q-type calcium channels, so less acetylcholine is released (Lambert-Eaton syndrome).
   - **Why:** an increase of more than 100% with fast stimulation is diagnostic of a presynaptic disorder, and 50-60% of patients develop small cell lung cancer. [MSD Manual: Disorders of Neuromuscular Transmission](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/disorders-of-neuromuscular-transmission)
   - **Trap:** myasthenia gravis, which shows a decrease with slow stimulation.
5. A woman treated for myasthenia gravis has her pyridostigmine dose doubled. Two days later she is weaker, with muscle twitching, sweating and drooling. Edrophonium does not help. What is happening, and what is the treatment?
   - **Answer:** cholinergic crisis from too much cholinesterase inhibitor; support her breathing, stop the drug and give atropine.
   - **Why:** overdosing makes the muscles unresponsive, weakness fails to improve with edrophonium, and anticholinergics relieve the symptoms. (El-Husseiny MSK, p. 159)
   - **Trap:** myasthenic crisis, which improves with edrophonium and needs more treatment, not less.
