## In one line
**Sort a breast lump by age and feel:**
- **Young woman, smooth, mobile, rubbery:** fibroadenoma.
- **Breastfeeding, red, hot, tender:** mastitis or abscess.
- **Tender, lumpy, both sides, changes with the cycle:** fibrocystic change.
- **Bloody discharge from one duct:** intraductal papilloma.
- **Older woman, hard, fixed, irregular, skin dimpling:** carcinoma until proven otherwise.

**Then link each cancer feature to its mechanism:**
- **dimpling** = tethering of suspensory ligaments;
- **peau d'orange** = blocked lymphatics;
- **single-file cells** = lost E-cadherin (lobular);
- **ER-positive** = tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor;
- **HER2-positive** = trastuzumab, which can weaken the heart.

## Inflammatory and traumatic lumps (Elnemr pp. 1-2; MSD)
- **Acute mastitis and abscess:**
  - **Who and why:** usually during **breastfeeding**, from ***Staphylococcus aureus*** entering through **cracked nipples** (Elnemr p. 1). MSD names *S. aureus* as the most common cause, usually several weeks into breastfeeding.
  - **Look:** red, swollen, tender breast. Localized infection forms an abscess, and an untreated abscess can become a chronic abscess that mimics a tumor (Elnemr p. 1).
  - **Treatment (MSD):** **keep breastfeeding or pumping**, because emptying the breast is part of treatment. Give **dicloxacillin**, or **cephalexin** if penicillin-allergic. An abscess is found with **ultrasound** and **drained**.
- **Chronic mastitis causes** (Elnemr p. 2): chronic abscess, TB, syphilis, actinomycosis, idiopathic granulomatous mastitis, and reaction to leaked silicone.
- **Duct ectasia (plasma cell mastitis):**
  - Usually over age 40, cause unknown.
  - **Dilated ducts** full of **thick, tan-yellow secretion**, surrounded by inflammation rich in **plasma cells**, with foamy macrophages (Elnemr p. 2).
- **Traumatic fat necrosis:** a **hard, sometimes chalky-white mass** after injury that can be **mistaken for cancer** (Elnemr p. 2).
- **Galactocele:** a mobile, milk-filled cyst, usually within 6-10 months after breastfeeding stops (MSD).

## Fibrocystic change (Elnemr pp. 3-4; MSD)
- **The most common breast condition.** It is an exaggerated response to **estrogen**, mostly at **ages 35-50** (Elnemr p. 3).
- **Clinical picture:**
  - irregular firm lumps, often **multiple and bilateral**;
  - pain;
  - sometimes nipple discharge.
- MSD: breasts feel nodular and tender, and symptoms usually ease after menopause.
- **What the microscope shows:** a mixture of changes (Elnemr p. 4):
  - **adenosis** (more acini);
  - lobular and usual ductal hyperplasia;
  - **cysts** (which can rupture and inflame);
  - **apocrine metaplasia** (large pink cells lining cysts);
  - stromal fibrosis;
  - **sclerosing adenosis**.
- **Sclerosing adenosis mimics invasive cancer:** fibrosis squeezes and distorts the lobules. **Myoepithelial cells are still present** around the glands, which proves it is benign (Elnemr p. 4).
- **Cysts:** check with **ultrasound** first. Aspirate if symptomatic. Send fluid for cytology if it is **bloody or turbid**, or if a mass remains. Biopsy if a cyst recurs a third time (MSD).

**Cancer risk by finding** (Elnemr p. 4)

| Finding | Cancer risk |
| --- | --- |
| Fibrosis, cysts, inflammation, mild hyperplasia, apocrine metaplasia | **No increase** |
| Moderate or florid hyperplasia | **1.5-2 times** |
| **Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH)** | About **5 times** (Elnemr p. 4) |

**Other source:** MSD gives about **3-5 times** the risk for atypical ductal *or lobular* hyperplasia (MSD Breast Cancer, Risk factors). Your notes give about 5 times for ADH (Elnemr p. 4). Either way, atypia is the benign finding that raises risk the most.

- ADH has some, but not all, features of DCIS (Elnemr p. 4).
- Atypical hyperplasia is also on your NBME risk-factor chart (NBME TC p. 439).

## Benign tumors (Elnemr pp. 5-6; MSD)
| Tumor | Key points | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fibroadenoma** | Most common in **young women** ("breast mouse"). **Single, round, smooth, encapsulated, rubbery and mobile**. A **biphasic** tumor of ducts within fibrous stroma: the stroma surrounds the ducts (pericanalicular) or squashes them into slits (intracanalicular). Over 5 cm = giant fibroadenoma. Cancer arising in one is **extremely rare**. The adolescent (juvenile) type keeps growing | Elnemr p. 5; MSD |
| **Phyllodes tumor** | **Large** biphasic tumor like an intracanalicular fibroadenoma, but with **hypercellular stroma in a leaf-like pattern**. **Most are benign but recur**. Borderline or malignant types are graded by stromal atypia, mitoses, necrosis and infiltrative edges | Elnemr p. 6 |
| **Intraductal (duct) papilloma** | Arises in the **large lactiferous ducts** under the nipple. Causes **serous or bloody nipple discharge**. A soft polyp in the duct, usually under 3 cm. Papillae have fibrovascular cores covered by epithelial and **myoepithelial** cells. A single papilloma without atypia raises risk slightly; **multiple papillomas with atypia** raise it significantly | Elnemr p. 6 |

- **Bloody nipple discharge** (Elnemr p. 15): duct papilloma, duct carcinoma, mammary hyperplasia, or a general bleeding disorder.
- MSD: **bloody or spontaneous** discharge is a red flag that needs evaluation.

## Breast cancer: who gets it (Elnemr p. 7; NBME TC p. 439; MSD)
- **How common:** the most common cancer in women (Elnemr p. 7). The US lifetime risk is about **13% (1 in 8)** (MSD). It is rare before 25 and usually occurs after 40 (Elnemr p. 7).
- **Core idea:** prolonged **estrogen** exposure in a genetically susceptible woman (Elnemr p. 7).
- **Risk factors:**
  - **First-degree relative** (mother, sister, daughter) (Elnemr p. 7; NBME TC p. 439).
  - **BRCA genes:** 5-10% of breast cancers are familial (Elnemr p. 7). By age 80, about **72% of BRCA1** and **69% of BRCA2** carriers develop breast cancer. BRCA1 carriers also face about a 44% lifetime ovarian cancer risk (MSD).
  - **More estrogen exposure:** early menarche, late menopause, **nulliparity**, older age at first pregnancy, an estrogen-making ovarian tumor (Elnemr p. 7; MSD; NBME TC p. 439).
  - **Obesity after menopause:** fat makes estrogen locally (Elnemr p. 7). Risk rises about 10% for each extra 5 BMI units (MSD).
  - **Alcohol** (dose-dependent) and **chest radiation before age 45** (MSD).
  - **Atypical hyperplasia**, florid hyperplasia, multiple papillomas with atypia (Elnemr p. 7), and **LCIS** (MSD).
  - **Age**: postmenopausal women (NBME TC p. 439).
- **Where:** **upper outer quadrant 50%**, central 20%, rest 30% (Elnemr pp. 7-8).
- **Men:** men with a BRCA mutation have a 1-2% lifetime breast cancer risk (MSD). Your NBME chart links **BRCA2 with male breast cancer** (NBME TC p. 439).

**Correction (BRCA1 and lobular cancer):** your NBME risk chart lists BRCA1 with "medullary, ILC, serous ovarian cancer" (NBME TC p. 439). The ovarian link is right (MSD: about 44% lifetime risk). The link to **invasive lobular carcinoma** is not.
- In a familial lobular cancer study, lobular cancer was **only 3% of breast cancers in BRCA1 carriers**, versus 9% in BRCA2 carriers. The study found a germline **E-cadherin (CDH1)** mutation in one woman and concluded that germline CDH1 mutations "can be associated with" lobular cancer even without gastric cancer (Masciari et al., *J Med Genet* 2007).
- BRCA tumors are more often **high-grade and hormone receptor-negative** (MSD). The "medullary" look overlaps with BRCA1-associated cancers (Wysocka 2020).
- **For exams:**
  - BRCA1 = high-grade, triple-negative, medullary-like breast cancer, plus serous ovarian cancer.
  - **Lobular cancer = E-cadherin.**

## Carcinoma in situ (Elnemr pp. 8-9; NBME TC p. 440; MSD)
| | **DCIS** | **LCIS** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Malignant cells filling ducts, with **no invasion through the basement membrane** | Small, uniform, bland lobular cells filling the acini |
| Clues | **Comedo type**: high-grade cells with **central necrosis**; the cut mass oozes necrotic material like toothpaste. Non-comedo types (solid, cribriform, papillary) are usually low or intermediate grade and show nothing on gross exam | Often **multicentric and bilateral** |
| What happens | Untreated, it can progress to invasive cancer. It makes up about **20%** of breast cancers (MSD) | Your notes: if not treated, it "may be followed by invasion" (Elnemr p. 8). MSD calls it a risk marker for invasive cancer: about **7-11% at 10 years and 20% at 20 years** |

**Recent change (LCIS in staging):** your notes list "LCIS or DCIS" under Tis, the in situ stage (Elnemr p. 13). The **AJCC 8th edition** no longer treats LCIS as a breast malignancy and **excludes it from TNM staging**, so Tis now covers DCIS (and Paget disease of the nipple) (CancerNetwork, 27 Mar 2019, reporting a talk on the AJCC 8th edition by breast pathologist Aysegul Sahin at the 2019 NCCN Annual Conference; the NCI TNM page lists Tis as DCIS and Paget disease, with no LCIS).
- **For exams:** LCIS is a **risk marker**, not a stageable cancer.
- DCIS is Tis.

## Invasive cancer types (Elnemr pp. 10-12; NBME TC p. 440; MSD)
**Invasive ductal carcinoma (NST)**
- About **75%** of breast cancers (Elnemr p. 10; MSD).
- **Gross:** a hard, gritty, gray-white mass with infiltrative borders.
- **What the patient shows** (Elnemr p. 10):
  - **Nipple retraction or skin dimpling:** the tumor sticks to the skin and its fibrous stroma pulls inward. Your NBME question blames the **suspensory (Cooper) ligaments** (NBME TC pp. 439, 444).
  - **Peau d'orange:** lymphatic blockage causes skin edema, and the skin dimples only where hair follicles anchor it.
  - **Cancer en cuirasse:** a hard, fixed breast from lymphedema plus invasion of the pectoral muscles.
  - **Microcalcifications** on mammography in 60-70%.
- **Microscope:** tubules, sheets and single cells in a fibrous (**desmoplastic**) stroma. Well-differentiated tumors form more tubules.

**Invasive lobular carcinoma**
- **Small, uniform cells in single file** ("Indian file") that **don't stick together**, because **E-cadherin** is lost (Elnemr p. 11; NBME TC p. 440).
- Often **multicentric and bilateral** (Elnemr p. 11).
- Lobular cancers are about 10% of invasive cancers (MSD).

**Medullary carcinoma** (as your notes describe it)
- A **soft**, well-circumscribed tumor with **"pushing" borders**, sheets of large anaplastic cells, little stroma and a **heavy lymphocyte and plasma cell infiltrate** (Elnemr p. 11).
- Your notes give it a favorable prognosis (p. 12).

**Recent change (medullary carcinoma):** the **2019 WHO classification (5th edition)** no longer counts medullary carcinoma as a separate type. It is now **invasive carcinoma NST with a "medullary pattern"**: a feature of tumors rich in lymphocytes that overlaps with basal-like and **BRCA1-associated** cancers. Pathologists also did not agree reliably on the diagnosis (Wysocka, *Nowotwory J Oncol* 2020).
- **For exams:** recognize the classic picture (pushing borders + lymphocytes) and its **BRCA1 link**.
- Expect newer sources to call it a pattern, not a type.

**Paget disease of the nipple**
- A **red, scaly, ulcerated, eczema-like nipple** caused by cancer cells spreading up the ducts into the epidermis.
- The skin holds **Paget cells**: large cells with clear cytoplasm and big dark nuclei.
- There is **always an underlying carcinoma** (in situ or invasive), and the prognosis is that of the underlying cancer (Elnemr p. 11).
- MSD calls it a form of DCIS that extends into the skin of the nipple and areola.
- NBME TC p. 440 pairs "eczematoid" with Paget disease.

**Inflammatory carcinoma**
- A **clinical** diagnosis: an enlarged, **red, swollen breast** from tumor cells **plugging dermal lymphatics**.
- It is usually high-grade ductal carcinoma and the most aggressive form (Elnemr p. 12).
- MSD: rapid redness, edema and skin thickening like peau d'orange (MSD, Pathology section). In its general biopsy advice, MSD adds that any skin taken with a breast biopsy should be examined, because it may show cancer cells in **dermal lymphatic vessels**.
- It is staged **T4d** (NCI).
- Your NBME notes test it as a "mastitis" that fails antibiotics, with the mechanism being **lymphatic obstruction** (NBME TC pp. 440, 444).

## Prognosis, staging and receptors (Elnemr pp. 12-13; NBME TC p. 440; MSD; NCI)
- **Worse prognosis** (Elnemr p. 12):
  - age **under 35**;
  - larger size (more node spread);
  - medial location;
  - cancer during **pregnancy or lactation**;
  - **inflammatory** type;
  - higher grade.
- **Better prognosis:** tubular and mucinous types, and medullary in your notes (Elnemr p. 12).
- **TNM, simplified** (NCI, AJCC-based):
  - **T1** is 20 mm or smaller, **T2** is over 20 to 50 mm, **T3** is over 50 mm, and **T4d** is inflammatory.
  - **N1** is 1-3 axillary nodes, **N2** is 4-9, and **N3a** is **10 or more**.
  - Your notes' table matches, except that its T1 line is cut off in the file and it writes "> 10" for N3, where NCI says 10 or more (Elnemr p. 13).
- **Receptors:**

| Marker | What it means | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ER/PR** | **Intranuclear** receptors. Positive tumors respond to hormone therapy (**tamoxifen**) and do better. ER-positive in about 80% of postmenopausal and 20% of premenopausal patients | Elnemr p. 13; NBME TC p. 440; MSD |
| **HER2** | A tyrosine kinase receptor (EGFR family) whose **gene is amplified**. Overexpressed in about **15%**; an **independent marker of poor prognosis**. Treated with **trastuzumab** | Elnemr p. 13; NBME TC p. 440; MSD |
| **Triple-negative** (ER−, PR−, HER2−) | About 11%, with a poorer prognosis. More common in Black women | MSD |
| **Ki-67** | Proliferation marker. Your notes call over 14% high | Elnemr p. 13 |

- Most breast cancers arise from **luminal** cells, and some from **basal** cells (Elnemr p. 13).

## Breast cancer drugs (Pharm HY; NBME TC p. 440; El-Husseiny; MSD)
- **Tamoxifen (a SERM):**
  - **Blocks** estrogen receptors in the **breast** but **stimulates** them elsewhere.
  - That causes **hot flushes**, **endometrial proliferation and uterine cancer**, and **clots (DVT)** (Pharm HY PDF 107).
  - MSD: given to **premenopausal** women, or postmenopausal women who can't tolerate aromatase inhibitors.
- **Raloxifene (another SERM):** used for **osteoporosis** (El-Husseiny MSK p. 119). Your Pharm HY notes list it with the other SERMs (PDF 107).
- **Aromatase inhibitors** (letrozole, anastrozole, exemestane):
  - Block peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogen (Pharm HY PDF 108).
  - Used in **postmenopausal** women (MSD). The letrozole label covers postmenopausal receptor-positive breast cancer (FDA Femara label).
  - See the guide 58 Correction: they are not prostate cancer drugs.
- **Trastuzumab** (also pertuzumab):
  - An antibody against **HER2** for HER2-positive breast cancer.
  - The key adverse effect is **cardiotoxicity, a drop in ejection fraction** (NBME TC p. 440; El-Husseiny Heme-Onc p. 111; Pharm HY PDF 115; MSD).
- Your Pharm HY notes also list **cyclosporine** as a drug linked to fibroadenoma (PDF 130).

**Recent change (screening age):** in **April 2024** the USPSTF began recommending **mammography every 2 years for women aged 40 to 74** (grade B). The 2016 version started routine screening at **50**.
- Evidence is insufficient (I statement) for women **75 and older**.
- It is also insufficient for **extra ultrasound or MRI in dense breasts**.

## Male breast (Elnemr p. 14; MSD)
- **Male breast cancer:** rare, with the same types as in women.
  - It invades the chest wall, lymphatics and blood early because the breast is small (Elnemr p. 14).
  - Presentation and treatment are the same as in women, but men present **later** (MSD).
- **Gynecomastia** (enlarged male breast tissue: duct hyperplasia with periductal fibrosis, one or both sides) (Elnemr p. 14):
  - **Normal:** in **newborns** (maternal estrogen), at **puberty** (estrogen-testosterone imbalance) and in the elderly.
  - **Disease causes:**
    - **too much estrogen:** liver failure, estrogen therapy for prostate cancer, estrogen-making testicular or adrenal tumors;
    - **testicular atrophy**;
    - **drugs** such as **digitalis**.
  - **From your other notes:**
    - **spironolactone** causes gynecomastia, low libido and impotence, and **eplerenone** has fewer of these effects (El-Husseiny Nephrology p. 148);
    - **hypothyroidism** can raise prolactin and cause gynecomastia in men (El-Husseiny Endocrinology p. 69);
    - **Klinefelter syndrome** (guide 58);
    - **Leydig cell tumor** (guide 59, standard teaching).

## Clinical vignette
A 46-year-old premenopausal woman finds a lump in the upper outer part of her left breast. It is hard, irregular and fixed. The skin over it puckers when she raises her arm, and a firm axillary node is palpable. Core biopsy shows invasive carcinoma forming tubules and nests in a dense fibrous stroma. The tumor is ER-positive and PR-positive with HER2 gene amplification.

- **Diagnosis:** **invasive ductal carcinoma (NST)**, the commonest type (75%). It is in the commonest site, the upper outer quadrant (Elnemr pp. 7, 10; MSD).
- **Skin puckering:** tethering by the tumor's fibrous stroma, pulling on the suspensory ligaments (Elnemr p. 10; NBME TC p. 444).
- **The node:** spread to 1-3 axillary nodes would be N1 (NCI).
- **ER/PR-positive:**
  - Hormone therapy works. Being premenopausal, she would get **tamoxifen** (MSD).
  - Warn her about **uterine cancer and clots** (Pharm HY PDF 107).
- **HER2-amplified:**
  - Add **trastuzumab**. Check heart function, because it can lower the ejection fraction (MSD; NBME TC p. 440).
  - HER2 overexpression is a poor-prognosis marker (MSD).

## Common traps
- **Lactational mastitis: keep breastfeeding** and give dicloxacillin. Drain an abscess (MSD).
- **Mastitis that doesn't respond to antibiotics, with axillary nodes:** think **inflammatory carcinoma** (dermal lymphatics) (NBME TC p. 440; Elnemr p. 12).
- **Fat necrosis and sclerosing adenosis both mimic cancer.** In sclerosing adenosis, **myoepithelial cells** prove it is benign (Elnemr pp. 2, 4).
- **Cysts, fibrosis and apocrine metaplasia carry no extra risk. Atypical hyperplasia raises risk about 5 times** in your notes (Elnemr p. 4); MSD gives 3-5 times (Other source).
- **Fibroadenoma vs phyllodes:** both are biphasic. Phyllodes is **larger**, has **hypercellular leaf-like stroma** and **recurs** (Elnemr pp. 5-6).
- **Bloody discharge from one duct, no mass = intraductal papilloma** (Elnemr pp. 6, 15).
- **DCIS:** central (comedo) necrosis, with the **basement membrane intact** (Elnemr p. 9).
- **LCIS is a risk marker, not staged as cancer** in AJCC 8th edition (CancerNetwork 2019).
- **Single-file cells = lobular = E-cadherin loss.** Lobular cancer is **not** a BRCA1 hallmark (Masciari 2007).
- **Eczema of the nipple in an adult = Paget disease** until proven otherwise. Look for the underlying carcinoma (Elnemr p. 11).
- **Dimpling = suspensory ligaments. Peau d'orange = lymphatics** (Elnemr p. 10; NBME TC p. 444).
- **Tamoxifen** (premenopausal) blocks breast receptors but stimulates the uterus. **Aromatase inhibitors are for postmenopausal women** (Pharm HY PDF 107-108; MSD).
- **Trastuzumab = HER2 = low EF** (NBME TC p. 440).
- **Screening now starts at 40, every 2 years** (USPSTF 2024).
- **Gynecomastia:**
  - liver failure, spironolactone (switch to eplerenone), digitalis, estrogen therapy, estrogen-making tumors;
  - normal in newborns, at puberty and in old age (Elnemr p. 14; El-Husseiny Nephrology p. 148).

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. A 29-year-old woman who is breastfeeding her 4-week-old baby has a temperature of 38.9°C (102°F) and a painful, red, firm wedge of skin on her right breast. Her nipple has a small crack. Ultrasound shows no fluid collection. Which is the most appropriate management?
   - **Answer:** **oral dicloxacillin and continued breastfeeding (or pumping) from both breasts.**
   - **Why:** lactational mastitis, usually from *S. aureus* entering through a cracked nipple (Elnemr p. 1; MSD). Emptying the breast is part of treatment (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **stop breastfeeding until the infection clears**. Stopping lets milk build up and is not advised.

2. A 51-year-old woman has a hard, irregular lump in her left breast 2 months after a car crash in which the seatbelt bruised her chest. Mammography shows a mass with coarse calcification. Biopsy shows no malignant cells. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **traumatic fat necrosis.**
   - **Why:** injury to breast fat can leave a hard, sometimes chalky-white mass that mimics cancer (Elnemr p. 2). It still needs biopsy to rule out cancer, as here.
   - **Trap:** **invasive ductal carcinoma**. The history of trauma and the benign biopsy point away from it.

3. A 44-year-old woman has a breast biopsy for a suspicious area on mammography. The pathologist sees crowded, distorted glands squeezed by dense fibrous tissue, which at first look like infiltrating cancer. Which finding most strongly supports a benign diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **a preserved layer of myoepithelial cells around the glands.**
   - **Why:** this is sclerosing adenosis, a fibrocystic change. Fibrosis distorts the lobules, but the myoepithelial cells remain, unlike in invasive carcinoma (Elnemr p. 4).
   - **Trap:** **a low number of mitoses**. Low-grade cancers can also have few mitoses, so this does not prove the lesion is benign.

4. A 47-year-old woman has a biopsy of a lumpy, tender area of her breast. The report lists several fibrocystic changes. Which one would most increase her future risk of breast cancer?
   - **Answer:** **atypical ductal hyperplasia.**
   - **Why:** your notes give about 5 times the risk (Elnemr p. 4). Other source: MSD gives 3-5 times for atypical ductal or lobular hyperplasia.
   - **Trap:** **apocrine metaplasia** or simple cysts, which carry no increased risk (Elnemr p. 4; MSD).

5. A 23-year-old woman has a painless, 2-cm, smooth, rubbery lump in her right breast that slips under the fingers. Core biopsy shows ducts lined by epithelial and myoepithelial cells, compressed into slit-like spaces by abundant but bland fibrous stroma. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** **fibroadenoma** (intracanalicular pattern).
   - **Why:** a young woman, a mobile rubbery mass and a biphasic tumor with bland stroma (Elnemr p. 5; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **phyllodes tumor**, which is usually larger, with **hypercellular, leaf-like stroma**, and tends to recur (Elnemr p. 6).

6. A 45-year-old woman notices spots of blood on her bra. Gentle pressure at one spot beside the areola produces bloody fluid from a single duct. No mass is felt, and a mammogram is normal. Which is the most likely cause?
   - **Answer:** **intraductal (duct) papilloma.**
   - **Why:** a benign polyp in a large lactiferous duct that presents with serous or bloody nipple discharge (Elnemr pp. 6, 15). Bloody discharge still needs evaluation (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **duct ectasia**, which gives thick, tan-yellow secretions from dilated ducts in older women (Elnemr p. 2).

7. A 58-year-old woman's screening mammogram shows fine, branching calcifications. Biopsy shows ducts filled with markedly atypical cells with a central core of necrotic debris. Which feature separates this lesion from invasive carcinoma?
   - **Answer:** **an intact basement membrane, with no tumor cells in the surrounding stroma.**
   - **Why:** this is comedo-type DCIS: high-grade cells with central necrosis, confined to the ducts (Elnemr p. 9; NBME TC p. 440).
   - **Trap:** **the degree of nuclear atypia**. Comedo DCIS is high grade, but grade does not define invasion.

8. A 62-year-old woman has a vague thickening in her breast that is hard to see on mammography. Biopsy shows small, uniform cells infiltrating the stroma in single-file lines, with no gland formation. Loss of which protein explains this growth pattern?
   - **Answer:** **E-cadherin.**
   - **Why:** invasive lobular carcinoma. Loss of the adhesion protein E-cadherin makes the cells non-cohesive, so they line up in single file (Elnemr p. 11; NBME TC p. 440). Germline CDH1 mutations "can be associated with" lobular cancer without gastric cancer (Masciari 2007).
   - **Trap:** **HER2 overexpression**, which is a receptor growth signal, not the cause of single-file growth.

9. A 64-year-old woman has had a red, scaly, weeping rash on her left nipple for 4 months that has not improved with topical steroids. Biopsy of the nipple skin shows large cells with clear cytoplasm and large dark nuclei scattered within the epidermis. What is the next most important consideration?
   - **Answer:** **an underlying breast carcinoma (in situ or invasive) must be found.**
   - **Why:** this is Paget disease of the nipple, with Paget cells in the epidermis. It always has an underlying ductal carcinoma, which sets the prognosis (Elnemr p. 11; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **treat as eczema with a stronger steroid**. Persistent nipple eczema needs a biopsy and a search for cancer.

10. A 44-year-old woman has been taking an oral drug for 3 years after surgery for ER-positive breast cancer. She now has irregular vaginal bleeding, and ultrasound shows a thickened endometrium. The drug's action at which site explains this?
    - **Answer:** **estrogen receptor stimulation (agonism) in the endometrium.** The drug is **tamoxifen**.
    - **Why:** tamoxifen blocks breast estrogen receptors but acts as an agonist elsewhere, causing endometrial proliferation and uterine cancer risk, as well as clots and hot flushes (Pharm HY PDF 107).
    - **Trap:** **aromatase inhibition**. Aromatase inhibitors lower estrogen and do not stimulate the endometrium.

11. A 50-year-old woman has a newly diagnosed breast cancer with HER2 gene amplification. Her oncologist plans an antibody that targets this receptor. Which test should be done before and during treatment?
    - **Answer:** **cardiac function testing (echocardiogram for ejection fraction).**
    - **Why:** trastuzumab can lower the ejection fraction (cardiotoxicity) (NBME TC p. 440; El-Husseiny Heme-Onc p. 111; Pharm HY PDF 115; MSD).
    - **Trap:** **audiometry**, which is for cisplatin ototoxicity (El-Husseiny Heme-Onc p. 111).

12. A 60-year-old man with alcoholic cirrhosis and ascites is treated with a diuretic. Three months later he has tender enlargement of both breasts. Which change in his diuretic is most likely to help?
    - **Answer:** **switch from spironolactone to eplerenone.**
    - **Why:** spironolactone has antiandrogen side effects (gynecomastia, low libido, impotence); eplerenone is more selective, with fewer endocrine effects (El-Husseiny Nephrology p. 148). Liver failure itself also raises estrogen (Elnemr p. 14).
    - **Trap:** **keep the spironolactone, because cirrhosis alone explains it**. Liver failure does raise estrogen (Elnemr p. 14), but the drug's antiandrogen effect is the cause you can change.

13. A 66-year-old man has a firm mass under his right nipple. Biopsy shows invasive ductal carcinoma. His mother had ovarian cancer and his sister breast cancer. A germline mutation in which gene is most associated with breast cancer in men?
    - **Answer:** **BRCA2.**
    - **Why:** your NBME chart links BRCA2 with male breast cancer (NBME TC p. 439). MSD: men with BRCA mutations have a 1-2% lifetime risk, and men tend to present later.
    - **Trap:** **CDH1 (E-cadherin)**, which has been linked to *lobular* cancer in some families, not to male breast cancer (Masciari 2007).

14. A 42-year-old woman with no family history of breast cancer and no symptoms asks whether she should have a mammogram. According to the current USPSTF recommendation, what is the best advice?
    - **Answer:** **screening mammography every 2 years, starting now (ages 40-74).**
    - **Why:** the USPSTF lowered the starting age from 50 to 40 in April 2024 (grade B).
    - **Trap:** **wait until 50**, the 2016 recommendation.
