## In one line
On the exam, the **patient with decision-making capacity decides**. Respect autonomy, tell the truth and keep confidences, with a short list of exceptions: emergencies, lack of capacity, minors in certain cases, and danger to self or others (FA pp. 265-267; Ch. 6 pp. 100-101). The best answer is usually the one that **explores the patient's reasons, uses plain language and keeps the patient in the conversation**. Refusing to see the patient, referring them away, coercing them or giving false reassurance is usually wrong (FA p. 268).

## Core ethical principles (FA p. 265; Ch. 6 p. 100)
| Principle | Meaning | Exam example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Autonomy** | Respect patients' own choices: truth-telling, confidentiality, informed consent, the right to refuse | A capable adult refuses surgery: honor it |
| **Beneficence** | Duty (fiduciary) to act in the patient's best interest | Can conflict with autonomy; with capacity, **autonomy wins** (Ch. 6 p. 102) |
| **Nonmaleficence** | "Do no harm"; weigh against benefit | A risky surgery is fine if benefits outweigh risks and the patient agrees |
| **Justice** | Fair and equitable, **not always equal** | Triage |
| Paternalism | Deciding for the patient without their input (Ch. 6 p. 100) | Usually the wrong answer |
| Proportionality | Treatment matches the illness and the goals of care (Ch. 6 p. 100) | |

## Informed consent (FA p. 265; Ch. 6 p. 100)
- Consent is **a process, not a signature**. It needs:
  - **disclosure** (with a medical interpreter if needed);
  - **understanding**;
  - **capacity**;
  - **voluntariness**, meaning no coercion.
- Discuss the diagnosis, the risks and benefits, and the alternatives, **including no treatment**. The patient can **revoke consent at any time, even orally** (FA p. 265).
- **Exceptions, "WIPE it away":**
  - **W**aiver: the patient explicitly waives the right to be informed.
  - **I**ncompetent: no capacity, so get consent from the legal surrogate.
  - **P**rivilege (therapeutic): withhold information only if disclosure would **severely harm** the patient or undermine their capacity.
  - **E**mergency: consent is implied.
- If a patient asks to hear only some things, **respect that wish**. Otherwise never conceal a diagnosis, because concealing it violates truth-telling (Ch. 6 p. 100).

## Minors (FA p. 265; Ch. 6 p. 100)
- A minor is generally anyone under **18**. Parental consent is usually needed, **except:**
  - **emergencies** (for example, a blood transfusion);
  - **emancipated minors**: married, self-supporting, in the military (FA), or pregnant, raising children or living on their own (Ch. 6);
  - **"Sex, Drugs, Rock and roll"**: contraception, STIs and pregnancy; substance use; emergency or trauma (FA p. 265).
- Seek the minor's **assent** even when their consent is not required, and encourage minor-parent communication (FA p. 265).
- **Abortion:** many states require parental notification or consent. Do not sway the decision either way (FA p. 268; Ch. 6 p. 100).
- **A pregnant teenager decides about her own baby**, even if her parents disagree (FA p. 268).
- **Parents cannot refuse lifesaving emergency treatment for a child on religious grounds.** Transfuse the minor. An adult's (or proxy's) refusal is honored when the patient's wishes are known (FA p. 269).

**Other source (minors and mental health care):** Ch. 6 p. 100 adds **psychiatric illness** to the list of things a minor can consent to. FA p. 265 does not list it. Both books say the laws **vary by state**. For exam questions, the safe core is sex, drugs, emergencies and emancipation.

## Capacity vs competency (FA p. 266; Ch. 6 pp. 100-101)
| | Capacity | Competency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who decides | **Any physician** (psychiatry consult if unclear) | **A judge (court)** |
| Scope | **One specific decision** at one time; can be temporary | **Global**, usually more permanent (for example, severe dementia); the court assigns a guardian |

- **Capacity components, "GIEMSA":**
  - consistent with the patient's **G**oals and values;
  - **I**nformed;
  - **E**xpresses a choice;
  - not the result of altered **M**ental status (delirium, psychosis, intoxication) or a **M**ood disorder;
  - **S**table over time;
  - **A**ge 18 or over, or emancipated (FA p. 266).
- Intellectual disability alone (for example Down syndrome or autism) **does not** remove capacity. A decision made with capacity **stands** if the patient later loses capacity (FA p. 266).
- A capable patient may make a decision the doctor thinks **unwise**. Capacity means reasoning, "though not necessarily wisely" (Ch. 6 p. 100).

## Advance directives and surrogates (FA p. 266; Ch. 6 pp. 101-102)
- **Living will** (written directive): lists the treatments the patient would accept or refuse.
- **Medical power of attorney (DPoA):** names an agent to decide if the patient loses capacity. It is more flexible than a living will, and the patient can revoke it while they still have capacity.
- **Oral directive:** prior statements are more valid when the patient was informed, specific and consistent, and repeated them to several people (FA p. 266). Oral statements are ethically binding but not legally binding in every state (Ch. 6 p. 102).
- **DNR** forbids CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation). Other resuscitative measures that may follow (for example a feeding tube) are also usually avoided (FA p. 266). But **DNR ≠ do not treat** (Ch. 6 p. 102). Ask about code status at every admission. The patient's own stated wishes **override family wishes** (Ch. 6 p. 102).
- **No directive and no capacity:** the surrogate decides what the **patient would have wanted** (substituted judgment), not what the surrogate wants. The order is **spouse, then adult children, then parents, then siblings, then other relatives** ("the spouse ChiPS in") (FA p. 266).

## Confidentiality (FA p. 267; Ch. 6 p. 101)
- Do not discuss the patient with family **without the patient's permission** (FA p. 268). HIPAA sets the privacy rules (Ch. 6 p. 101).
- **Exceptions: "The physician's good judgment SAVED the day":**
  - **S**uicidal or homicidal patients;
  - **A**buse of children, elders or prisoners;
  - **V**ictim: a state-specific duty to protect a third party;
  - **E**pilepsy and other impaired drivers;
  - reportable **D**iseases (STIs, hepatitis, TB and others).
- Use the exceptions only when the harm is serious and likely and no other way to protect the person exists (FA p. 267).
- **Child abuse and elder abuse: you must report.** For child abuse, contact CPS and make sure the child is safe (FA p. 269; Ch. 6 p. 101).
- **IPV in an adult is NOT a mandated report** in general (Ch. 6 p. 101). Ask about **safety and an emergency plan**. Do not pressure the patient to leave the partner or to report, unless state law requires it (FA p. 269).
- **Reportable diseases:** reporting is mandatory and is **not** a confidentiality violation (Ch. 6 p. 101).
- **Involuntary hospitalization** is allowed only for danger to self or others. **Restraints** are allowed for risk of harm and must be reassessed at least daily (Ch. 6 p. 101).

## Ethical situations: the right answer (FA pp. 268-269)
| Situation | Best response |
| --- | --- |
| Patient doesn't take medicine as prescribed | **Find the reason** and willingness to change; don't coerce or refer away |
| Trouble taking medicines | Written instructions, a **simpler regimen**, **teach-back** |
| Patient wants an unnecessary procedure | Explore the reason and address the concern; don't refuse to see them; don't do the procedure |
| Family asks you not to tell the patient bad news | Ask why. A patient with capacity is told unless they say otherwise. Therapeutic privilege only if the patient might **seriously harm themselves or others** if told |
| Patient says they find you attractive | **Direct, closed-ended questions**, a chaperone, possibly transfer care; never a romantic relationship |
| Patient after mastectomy feels "ugly" | **Ask why**; no false reassurance |
| Angry about a long wait | **Acknowledge** the anger and **apologize**; don't explain the delay |
| Upset with another doctor | Suggest they talk to that doctor; if it's office staff, you talk to the staff member |
| Mistake made (for example a test on the wrong patient) | **Always disclose**, harmful or not (also FA p. 274) |
| Treatment not covered by insurance | Discuss **all** options; never limit care because of cost |
| Wants alternative medicine | Explore the reasons without judgment; explain the known risks, benefits and interactions |
| Colleague impaired at work | Report to **local supervisor**; if no action, the **state licensing board** |
| Brain-dead patient, family wants support continued | Explain gently that **brain death = death**; movement is a **spinal reflex**; involve the ethics board |
| Drug company sponsorship for promoting a drug | **Decline**. Exceptions: gifts of minimal value, gifts that directly benefit patients, independently chosen education grants |
| Procedure against your beliefs (nonemergent) | Give unbiased information and **refer** |
| A 7-year-old feels responsible for a sibling's death | Concrete words, no euphemisms; reassure he is not to blame; children understand death is permanent by about ages 5-7 |
| Suicidal patient | Assess seriousness. Offer voluntary admission; involuntary if serious and they refuse (FA p. 268) |

## End-of-life care (FA p. 272; Ch. 6 pp. 102-103)
- **Hospice:** comfort care when life expectancy is **6 months or less** (FA says < 6 months). It is covered by Medicare (Part A), Medicaid and most private plans (FA p. 272; Ch. 6 p. 103).
- **Palliative care:** symptom relief for any serious illness, **even alongside curative treatment** and even with a good prognosis (Ch. 6 p. 103).
- **Double effect:** giving enough opioid to relieve pain is acceptable even if it may shorten life, because relief is the intent (FA p. 272; Ch. 6 p. 102). Don't worry about addiction in terminal illness (Ch. 6 p. 102).
- **Withdrawing treatment = withholding it**, both legally and ethically. When people disagree, the **patient's wishes take precedence**. In futile cases where the family disagrees, involve the **ethics committee** or a court (Ch. 6 p. 103).
- **Persistent vegetative state:** the brainstem is intact, with sleep-wake cycles but **no awareness**. It is **not brain death** (Ch. 6 p. 102).
- **Kübler-Ross stages:** denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance (Ch. 6 p. 102). They need not occur in order (standard teaching).
- **Euthanasia** (the physician gives the lethal drug) is **illegal in all states**. **MAID** (the physician prescribes and the patient **self-administers**) requires an informed, competent patient with a prognosis of **6 months or less** (Ch. 6 p. 103).

**Recent change (MAID states):** your Ch. 6 notes (p. 103) say physician-assisted suicide is legal in only six states (WA, OR, MT, VT, CA, CO). As of October 2026 it is authorized in **13 states plus Washington, DC**. The other seven states are **Hawaii, Maine, New Jersey, New Mexico, Delaware, Illinois and New York** ([Compassion & Choices list](https://compassionandchoices.org/state-advocacy/)). The newest three:
- **Delaware**: in effect 1 Jan 2026 (Hinshaw alert below).
- **Illinois**: signed 12 Dec 2025, in effect 12 Sep 2026 ([Hinshaw & Culbertson legal alert](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/en/insights/healthcare-alert/illinois-joins-twelve-other-jurisdictions-in-adopting-a-medical-aid-in-dying-law)).
- **New York**: signed 6 Feb 2026, in effect 5 Aug 2026 ([NY State Department of Health](https://healthweb-back.health.ny.gov/press/releases/2026/2026-08-05_medical_aid_in_dying_law.htm)).

Most states still do not allow it, and euthanasia is still illegal everywhere. Exam answers don't change: **a patient who asks for help dying gets pain control, palliative care and exploration of the request** (FA p. 268).

## Communication skills
**Published research (thin area):** the sources for this section are Baile et al. 2000 (SPIKES), the AHRQ Health Literacy Toolkit, and the federal language-access rule (45 CFR 92.201), plus her FA pages where noted.

**Breaking bad news: SPIKES (Baile et al., *The Oncologist* 2000;5:302-311)**
| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **S**etting | Private room, **sit down**, include family only if the patient wants it, avoid interruptions |
| **P**erception | **"Before you tell, ask."** Use open-ended questions to find out what the patient already understands |
| **I**nvitation | Ask **how much the patient wants to know** |
| **K**nowledge | **Warn first** ("Unfortunately I have some bad news"). Use plain words ("spread," not "metastasized") and give information in small chunks |
| **E**motions | **Empathic response**: notice the emotion, name it, identify its cause, and connect ("I can see this is a shock") |
| **S**trategy and summary | Check readiness, discuss options, check understanding, make a plan |

**Checking understanding: teach-back (AHRQ Tool 5; FA p. 268)**
- Ask the patient to explain **in their own words** what they need to know or do.
- It is **not a test of the patient**. You are checking how well **you** explained.
- **Chunk and check**: teach one part, check it, then move on.
- "Do you understand?" doesn't work, because patients usually say yes.
- If the explanation was misunderstood, **re-teach a different way** and check again ([AHRQ Tool 5](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)).

**Interpreters (45 CFR 92.201, Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act; eCFR current to 20 Jul 2026)**
- Covered health programs must offer a **qualified interpreter** to patients with limited English proficiency, and cannot make the patient bring or pay for their own.
- **Do not use a minor child** to interpret, except as a temporary emergency measure while no qualified interpreter is available.
- **Do not use an accompanying adult** except in an emergency, or when the patient asks for it **in private, with a qualified interpreter present**, and the request is documented.
- Video or phone interpreting is allowed if it meets quality standards ([eCFR 45 CFR 92.201](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-C/section-92.201)).
- Her FA notes also say to use a medical interpreter as part of disclosure (FA p. 265).

**Interview basics (standard teaching unless cited)**
- Start with **open-ended questions**, then narrow with closed ones.
- Avoid leading questions and jargon.
- Summarize back to the patient.
- **Use closed questions and a chaperone** when a patient is seductive or flirting (FA p. 268).
- **No false reassurance** (FA p. 268).
- **Acknowledge anger** without becoming defensive (FA p. 268).

## Healthcare delivery (FA pp. 270-272)
- **Prevention levels:**
  - **Primary**: prevent the disease (HPV vaccine).
  - **Secondary**: screen for early, symptom-free disease (Pap smear).
  - **Tertiary**: limit complications of existing disease (chemotherapy).
  - **Quaternary**: avoid unnecessary interventions (extra imaging, polypharmacy).
- **Insurance plans:**

| Plan | Network | Specialist referral? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **HMO** | Limited panel (except emergencies); denies services outside evidence-based guidelines | **Yes**, from primary care |
| **EPO** | Limited panel (except emergencies) | No |
| **POS** | Can go out of network at higher cost | **Yes** |
| **PPO** | Can go out of network at higher cost | No |
| **ACO** | Providers enroll voluntarily; Medicare | |

- **Payment models:**
  - **Capitation**: a set amount per patient per period, regardless of use (used by some HMOs).
  - **Bundled**: a set amount per service, shared among all providers.
  - **Fee-for-service** (discounted in PPOs).
  - **Global**: one payment for a whole episode of care, such as elective surgery.
- **Medicare** covers age **65 and over**, certain disabilities and **end-stage renal disease** ("MedicarE = Elderly"). **Medicaid** is joint federal-state coverage for low income ("MedicaiD = Destitute"). Medicare parts:
  - **Part A**: hospit**A**l and hospice.
  - **Part B**: **B**asic doctor bills and tests.
  - **Part C**: A + B "**C**ombo" through private plans.
  - **Part D**: prescription **D**rugs.
- **Leading causes of death by age (her table):**
  - Under 1: congenital malformations, preterm birth, maternal complications.
  - Ages 1-44: **unintentional injury**. For ages 15-34, suicide is #2 and homicide #3.
  - Ages 45-64: **cancer**.
  - 65 and over: **heart disease**.
- **Most common 30-day readmissions:** Medicare, heart failure; Medicaid and uninsured, mood disorders (FA p. 272).

## Quality and patient safety (FA pp. 273-274)
- **Safety culture:** anyone can raise a concern without fear of blame.
- **Forcing functions** are the most effective design fix, because they make the error impossible (for example, a feeding syringe that can't connect to IV tubing). Standardization (checklists) and simplification help too.
- **PDSA cycle:** **P**lan, **D**o (test the change), **S**tudy (measure), **A**ct (adopt).
- **Quality measures**, with diabetes examples:

| Measure | What it counts | Diabetes example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Structure** | Resources | Number of diabetes educators |
| **Process** | Whether the system worked as planned | % of diabetic patients with HbA1c checked in 6 months |
| **Outcome** | Effect on patients | Average HbA1c |
| **Balancing** | Harm elsewhere | Hypoglycemia rate after the push to lower HbA1c |

- **Swiss cheese model:** harm happens when the **holes in several layers of defense line up**. It focuses on systems, not individuals.
- **Types of error:**
  - **Active error**: made at the frontline, with immediate effect (wrong dose programmed into an IV pump).
  - **Latent error**: built into the system, "an accident waiting to happen" (different IV pump models in one hospital).
  - **Never event**: serious and preventable (a retained surgical instrument).
- **Error analysis:**
  - **RCA**: **retrospective**, done after an event using records and interviews.
  - **FMEA**: **forward-looking**, done before a new process starts; it lists the ways the process could fail and ranks them by likelihood and impact.
- **Burnout vs fatigue:**
  - **Burnout**: prolonged stress, with cynicism, detachment and errors from lack of concern.
  - **Fatigue**: sleep deprivation, with errors from impaired thinking.

## Normal aging (FA p. 270)
- **Sexual function:** men have slower erection and ejaculation and a longer refractory period, but **libido is unchanged**. Women have vaginal shortening, thinning and dryness.
- **Sleep:** less REM and slow-wave sleep, longer time to fall asleep, early waking.
- **Other changes:** suicide rate rises; vision, hearing, immune function, kidney, lung and GI function fall; muscle mass falls and fat rises.
- **Intelligence does not decrease.**

## Clinical vignette
A 64-year-old retired teacher is admitted for weight loss. A CT-guided biopsy shows metastatic pancreatic cancer. Before you see her, her son stops you in the hall: "Please don't tell Mom. She'll give up. Tell her it's an infection." She is alert, oriented and making her own decisions. She has never said she doesn't want to know her results.

- **Best response:** **ask the son why** he is worried, and acknowledge it. Then explain that **a patient with capacity is told about her own care unless she chooses otherwise** (FA p. 268). Concealing the diagnosis violates truth-telling (Ch. 6 p. 100).
- **Why not therapeutic privilege:** it applies only if the patient might **seriously harm herself or others** if told (FA pp. 265, 268). Fear that she will be sad or "give up" is not enough.
- **How to tell her:** use SPIKES. Ask what she knows, ask **how much she wants to know** and who she wants present, warn her, use plain words, respond to her emotion and make a plan (Baile 2000). If she says she'd rather her son hear the details, **respect that** (Ch. 6 p. 100).

## Common traps
- **Capacity is decided by a doctor, for one decision. Competency is decided by a judge** (FA p. 266; Ch. 6 p. 100).
- **The surrogate decides what the patient would have wanted**, in the order spouse, then adult children, then parents, then siblings (FA p. 266).
- **Jehovah's Witness: adult refusal stands; transfuse the child in an emergency** (FA p. 269; Ch. 6 p. 102).
- **Child and elder abuse: must report. IPV in an adult: ask about safety, don't report unless state law requires** (Ch. 6 p. 101; FA p. 269).
- **Disclose every error, even with no harm** (FA pp. 268, 274).
- **Impaired colleague: supervisor first, then the licensing board** (FA p. 269).
- **Brain death = death. Persistent vegetative state ≠ brain death** (FA p. 269; Ch. 6 p. 102).
- **Withdrawing = withholding. DNR ≠ do not treat** (Ch. 6 pp. 102-103).
- **Euthanasia is illegal everywhere. MAID is legal in 13 states plus DC (2026)**, not the 6 in your notes.
- **Never use a child as interpreter** except as a stopgap in an emergency (45 CFR 92.201).
- **Teach-back, not "Do you understand?"** (AHRQ Tool 5).
- **SPIKES: ask what the patient knows before telling them** (Baile 2000).
- **RCA is after an event; FMEA is before a new process** (FA p. 274).

## Practice questions
Answer, then check the explanation.

1. An 81-year-old man with mild Alzheimer disease has wet gangrene of the foot. His surgeon recommends below-knee amputation. He refuses. He explains that he understands he could die of infection without surgery, and that he would rather die than lose his leg, as he has told his family for years. He is not delirious or depressed, and his answer is the same the next day. His daughter asks the team to operate anyway. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **respect his refusal** and offer other treatment and comfort care.
   - **Why:** he is informed, expresses a choice, his choice fits his values, it is stable and his mental status is not altered. That meets GIEMSA (FA p. 266). Capacity is judged for a specific clinical question and is not broadly assigned, so his diagnosis does not decide it (Ch. 6 pp. 100-101).
   - **Trap:** **petition the court to declare him incompetent**. A court is not needed when he has capacity for this decision.

2. A 47-year-old man is unconscious after a ruptured cerebral aneurysm and needs a decision about surgery. He has no advance directive. His wife wants surgery. His 24-year-old son and his mother say he would not want it. Whose decision should guide the team?
   - **Answer:** **the wife's**, as she reports what he would have wanted.
   - **Why:** surrogate order is spouse, then adult children, then parents, then siblings (FA p. 266).
   - **Trap:** **majority vote of the family**. Surrogate priority is ranked, not a vote.

3. A 16-year-old girl asks to be tested and treated for a sexually transmitted infection. She asks that her parents not be told. Which is the most appropriate action?
   - **Answer:** **test and treat her confidentially** while encouraging her to talk with her parents.
   - **Why:** sexual health (contraception, STIs, pregnancy) is an exception to parental consent (FA p. 265; Ch. 6 p. 100).
   - **Trap:** **call her parents for consent first**.

4. A 10-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department after a bicycle crash. He has a splenic laceration, a falling hemoglobin and hypotension. His parents refuse blood because of their religious beliefs. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **transfuse the child**.
   - **Why:** parents cannot refuse emergency lifesaving treatment for a minor on religious grounds alone (FA p. 269). Emergency care for minors does not need consent (FA p. 265).
   - **Trap:** **seek a court order and wait**, which delays emergency care.

5. A 34-year-old man has had two generalized seizures in the past month because he stopped his antiseizure drug. He drives a delivery truck and says he will keep driving because he needs the job. Which is the most appropriate action?
   - **Answer:** **tell him he must not drive, and report him to the state authority as state law requires.**
   - **Why:** impaired drivers, including people with epilepsy, are a confidentiality exception ("SAVED") (FA p. 267).
   - **Trap:** **keep this confidential, because he hasn't hurt anyone yet**.

6. A 29-year-old woman has bruises on her arms of different ages. She says her boyfriend hits her when he drinks. She does not want the police involved. Her state has no mandatory reporting law for adult IPV. Which is the most appropriate next step?
   - **Answer:** **ask whether she is safe now and help her make a safety plan.**
   - **Why:** IPV in an adult is not a mandated report (Ch. 6 p. 101). Ask about safety and an emergency plan, and don't pressure her to leave or report (FA p. 269).
   - **Trap:** **call the police yourself**.

7. A nurse gives a hospitalized patient a dose of enoxaparin meant for the patient in the next bed. The patient has no bleeding and no harm. Which is the most appropriate action regarding the patient?
   - **Answer:** **tell the patient about the error** and apologize.
   - **Why:** errors must be disclosed whether harm occurred or not (FA pp. 268, 274).
   - **Trap:** **no disclosure needed because there was no harm**.

8. A 76-year-old man with heart failure has been readmitted twice. At each discharge he said yes when asked "Do you understand your medicines?" He takes 9 drugs. Which approach best improves his understanding at this discharge?
   - **Answer:** **ask him to explain in his own words how and when he will take each medicine** (teach-back), and simplify the regimen where possible.
   - **Why:** teach-back checks the clinician's explanation, and "Do you understand?" doesn't work (AHRQ Tool 5). FA p. 268 lists teach-back and simpler regimens.
   - **Trap:** **give him a longer printed handout**. Written instructions help, but on their own they don't confirm he understood.

9. A Vietnamese-speaking woman with limited English needs to hear her new diagnosis of breast cancer. Her 11-year-old daughter speaks English well and offers to translate. A qualified interpreter can be reached by video in 10 minutes. Which is the most appropriate action?
   - **Answer:** **wait for and use the qualified medical interpreter.**
   - **Why:** federal rules bar using a minor to interpret except as a temporary emergency measure (45 CFR 92.201(e)). Using a medical interpreter is part of informed disclosure (FA p. 265).
   - **Trap:** **let the daughter interpret, because it is faster**.

10. A physician is about to tell a 58-year-old man that his liver biopsy shows cancer. They are seated in a private room. Following the SPIKES protocol, what should the physician do next?
    - **Answer:** **ask what he understands about why the biopsy was done** (Perception).
    - **Why:** SPIKES moves from Setting to Perception, then Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions and Strategy. "Before you tell, ask" (Baile 2000).
    - **Trap:** **state the diagnosis right away** (Knowledge), which skips perception and invitation.

11. A 70-year-old woman with metastatic lung cancer in hospice has severe bone pain despite her current morphine dose. Her husband worries that more morphine could slow her breathing and hasten death. Which is the most appropriate action?
    - **Answer:** **increase the opioid to control her pain.**
    - **Why:** the principle of double effect: comfort is the intended effect, and possible respiratory depression is an accepted side effect (FA p. 272; Ch. 6 p. 102).
    - **Trap:** **keep the dose to avoid shortening her life**.

12. A hospital will replace paper medication orders with a new barcode scanning system next month. The safety committee wants to find the ways the new process could fail and rank them before it goes live. Which method fits best?
    - **Answer:** **failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA).**
    - **Why:** FMEA is forward-looking and done before implementation. It ranks failures by probability and impact (FA p. 274).
    - **Trap:** **root cause analysis**, which looks back after an error has happened.

13. A resident notices that an attending surgeon smells of alcohol and is slurring words before a scheduled operation. Which is the most appropriate first action?
    - **Answer:** **report it right away to the supervising authority** (for example, the department chief) so the surgeon doesn't operate.
    - **Why:** an impaired colleague threatens patient safety. Report to local supervisory personnel, and go to the state licensing board only if the organization doesn't act (FA p. 269).
    - **Trap:** **report directly to the state licensing board first**.

14. A primary care physician reviews an 84-year-old woman's 14 medicines and stops 4 that no longer help her. This is an example of which level of prevention?
    - **Answer:** **quaternary prevention.**
    - **Why:** quaternary prevention avoids unnecessary interventions, including cutting polypharmacy (FA p. 270).
    - **Trap:** **tertiary prevention**, which treats existing disease to limit complications.
