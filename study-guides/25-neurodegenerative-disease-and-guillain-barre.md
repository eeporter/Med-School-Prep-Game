# Dementias, movement disorders and Guillain-Barré syndrome

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials of Neurology (basal ganglia pp. 100-103, dementias pp. 150-154, Parkinson and Alzheimer drugs pp. 239-243) and the Osmosis High-Yield Neurology notes (Guillain-Barré syndrome, normal pressure hydrocephalus). Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where a newer or different source disagrees with your notes, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Tie each disease to its protein or lesion. **Alzheimer**: amyloid plaques plus tau tangles, memory first. **Parkinson**: substantia nigra loss with α-synuclein Lewy bodies, tremor, rigidity and slowness. **Huntington**: CAG repeat, caudate atrophy, chorea. **Lewy body dementia**: hallucinations and fluctuations with parkinsonism within a year. **Frontotemporal**: personality first, memory later. **Normal pressure hydrocephalus**: "wet, wobbly, wacky". **Guillain-Barré**: ascending weakness with lost reflexes after an infection.

## Core mechanism
1. **Alzheimer disease:** the two hallmarks are extracellular **beta-amyloid** in neuritic plaques and intracellular **neurofibrillary tangles** made of hyperphosphorylated tau. Atrophy usually starts in the mesial temporal lobe (hippocampus). [MSD Manual: Alzheimer Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/alzheimer-disease) Your notes add: acetylcholine falls because **choline acetyltransferase** is deficient, most of all in the **nucleus basalis of Meynert** and the hippocampus, which is why cholinesterase inhibitors help. The number of tangles tracks how severe the dementia is. Amyloid also builds up in vessel walls (**amyloid angiopathy**), raising the risk of **lobar** bleeds in the elderly, most often occipital and parietal. Amyloid stains with **Congo red** and shows **apple-green birefringence** under polarized light. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 151)
2. **Parkinson disease:** pigmented dopamine neurons of the **substantia nigra** degenerate, and synuclein-filled **Lewy bodies** appear. Losing these neurons drains dopamine from the putamen, which drives most motor symptoms. [MSD Manual: Parkinson Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/parkinson-disease) The exact subregion is the **pars compacta** of the substantia nigra (standard teaching). The street-drug contaminant **MPTP** is turned into **MPP+**, which kills these neurons and causes permanent parkinsonism (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 100). Signs in your notes: Parkinson **TRAPS** your body and masks your face: **T**remor at rest (pill-rolling), **R**igidity (cogwheel), **A**kinesia or bradykinesia, **P**ostural instability, **S**huffling gait. For symptoms that drugs cannot control, **deep brain stimulation** of the globus pallidus internus or subthalamic nucleus can help (p. 100).
3. **Huntington disease:** an expanded **CAG** repeat in the *HTT* gene on chromosome 4 adds extra glutamine to huntingtin. The **caudate** atrophies, and inhibitory (GABA) medium spiny neurons of the striatum die. More repeats mean earlier, more severe disease. MSD notes GABA and substance P levels fall. [MSD Manual: Huntington Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/huntington-disease) Your notes give the exam summary: **low GABA, low acetylcholine, high dopamine**, with low GABA the most typical change; caudate shrinkage **enlarges the frontal horns** of the lateral ventricles; and because the lost neurons mainly serve the **indirect pathway** (which normally suppresses unwanted movement), the result is chorea and athetosis (writhing), plus personality change, aggression, depression and dementia (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 101, 243). That is why dopamine-depleting drugs treat chorea.
4. **Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS):** an acute, self-limited inflammatory polyneuropathy thought to be autoimmune. It often starts 5 days to 3 weeks after an infection (classically *Campylobacter jejuni*), surgery or vaccination. [MSD Manual: Guillain-Barré Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/guillain-barr%C3%A9-syndrome-gbs) Osmosis explains it as **molecular mimicry**: antibodies and macrophages aimed at the microbe attack the myelin of peripheral nerves. Triggers besides *C. jejuni* include *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, CMV, EBV, influenza A, Zika and HIV. (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Guillain-Barré syndrome") Your El-Husseiny notes give the same mechanism: *C. jejuni* and some viruses (such as Zika) carry a **ganglioside-like** molecule, and antibodies against it cross-react with gangliosides in peripheral myelin, causing segmental demyelination with inflammation (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 159). The damaged cells are **Schwann cells**, and GBS is demyelinating and so reversible, unlike polio, which kills the motor neurons (pp. 30, 139).

## Compare and contrast: dementias
| Feature | Alzheimer | Lewy body | Frontotemporal | Normal pressure hydrocephalus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First clue | **Short-term memory loss** | **Visual hallucinations**, fluctuating attention, parkinsonism, REM sleep behavior disorder (2 of 4 = probable) | **Personality and behavior** change, disinhibition; or language loss | **Gait** problem ("magnetic", feet stick to the floor), urinary incontinence; dementia can come late |
| Age | Older adults; most common dementia, usually over 60 (El-Husseiny p. 150) | Older adults | Younger: usually 55-65 (your notes: 50s-60s, p. 153) | Older adults (Osmosis: onset about 60) |
| Pathology or imaging | Plaques and tangles; mesial temporal atrophy first; the **hippocampus** shrinks most (El-Husseiny p. 151) | Lewy bodies (alpha-synuclein, mainly in the cortex; El-Husseiny p. 153) | Tauopathy; Pick cells and Pick bodies (tau inclusions; El-Husseiny p. 152); autosomal dominant in 20-40% (p. 153); "severely atrophic, sometimes paper-thin gyri" in the frontal and temporal lobes; TDP-43 inclusions in many cases (standard teaching) | Ventricles enlarged out of proportion to atrophy; normal CSF opening pressure (see the Other source note below) |
| Key treatment fact | Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil first line; nausea, dizziness, insomnia); memantine (NMDA antagonist; dizziness, confusion, hallucinations) for moderate to severe; your notes also list vitamin E as an antioxidant (El-Husseiny pp. 242-243). **Recent change:** anti-amyloid antibodies for selected mild cases (MSD) | Cholinesterase inhibitors may help; **extreme sensitivity to antipsychotics** | — | Removing 30-50 mL CSF predicts benefit; **ventriculoperitoneal shunt** |

Sources: [MSD Manual: Alzheimer Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/alzheimer-disease), [MSD Manual: Dementia With Lewy Bodies and Parkinson Disease Dementia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/dementia-with-lewy-bodies-and-parkinson-disease-dementia), [MSD Manual: Frontotemporal Dementia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/frontotemporal-dementia), [MSD Manual: Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/normal-pressure-hydrocephalus). The "wet, wobbly, wacky" mnemonic is standard teaching.

- **Rule out reversible causes first** (your notes): depression ("pseudodementia"), hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, neurosyphilis and normal pressure hydrocephalus (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 150).
- **Alzheimer, early signs in your notes:** word-finding trouble early; executive and behavior problems later, with trouble shopping or cooking (p. 150).
- **Lewy body dementia in your notes:** dementia and visual hallucinations ("haLewycinations") come first, then parkinsonism (p. 153).
- **Vascular dementia:** the **2nd most common** dementia in the elderly; **step-wise** decline from many infarcts, with memory loss late; imaging shows multiple infarcts (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 154).
- **Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD):** **rapidly** progressive dementia (weeks to months) with **startle myoclonus**. A prion disease: the normal prion protein's alpha-helix refolds into **beta-pleated sheet**, which resists proteases and builds up in gray matter. The gray matter becomes **spongiform** (vacuoles in neurons, no inflammation). EEG shows **periodic sharp waves**; CSF **14-3-3** protein is raised. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 154)
- **Normal pressure hydrocephalus in Osmosis:** stretched fibers of the corona radiata cause incontinence, and the gait is "magnetic" or "glue-footed"; a high-volume lumbar puncture or lumbar drain trial predicts benefit; imaging shows big ventricles **without cortical atrophy** (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Normal pressure hydrocephalus"). **Other source:** Osmosis says the name is a misnomer and pressure is slightly to moderately raised; MSD describes a normal CSF opening pressure. For exams, "normal opening pressure on lumbar puncture" is the expected answer (standard teaching).

- **Lewy body dementia vs Parkinson disease dementia:** in Lewy body dementia, thinking and movement problems start within **1 year** of each other; in Parkinson disease dementia, thinking problems usually come 10-15 years after the motor symptoms. [MSD Manual: Dementia With Lewy Bodies and Parkinson Disease Dementia](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/dementia-with-lewy-bodies-and-parkinson-disease-dementia)
- **Alzheimer genetics:** familial forms involve the amyloid precursor protein and presenilin 1 and 2 genes. Your notes give the chromosomes for early-onset (under 60) familial disease: **APP on 21, presenilin 1 on 14, presenilin 2 on 1**; sporadic disease goes with **ApoE4**, and ApoE2 lowers risk (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 152). Two APOE ε4 alleles raise risk up to 15-fold; ε2 **may** lower it. Test APOE ε4 before anti-amyloid antibodies, because of brain swelling and microbleeds (ARIA, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities). [MSD Manual: Alzheimer Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/alzheimer-disease) **Down syndrome** raises Alzheimer risk because the APP gene sits on chromosome 21, so three copies make extra amyloid (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 152).

## Movement disorders
| Feature | Parkinson disease | Huntington disease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lesion | Substantia nigra (dopamine) → putamen | Caudate atrophy; striatal GABA neurons lost |
| Genetics | Mostly sporadic | Autosomal dominant, CAG repeat, chromosome 4; **anticipation** (repeats can grow when the parent passes it on) |
| Pathway (your notes) | Loss of the dopamine drive to the **direct** pathway, so the motor cortex is underactive (p. 100) | Loss of the **indirect** pathway (p. 101) |
| Movements | **Resting** "pill-rolling" tremor, **cogwheel** rigidity, **bradykinesia**; postural instability later | **Chorea** (later rigid), plus depression, irritability and dementia; suicide risk |
| Onset | Mean about 60 years (MSD Parkinson Disease) | About age 35-40 (MSD); **Other source:** your notes give 20-50 (El-Husseiny p. 101) |
| Drugs | **Carbidopa/levodopa** (carbidopa blocks peripheral conversion, so less is needed and fewer side effects); dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole); MAO-B inhibitor (selegiline); COMT inhibitors (entacapone); amantadine for levodopa dyskinesias; anticholinergics mainly for tremor | **VMAT2 inhibitors** (tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine) for chorea, avoided with depression; antipsychotics as an alternative |

Sources: [MSD Manual: Parkinson Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/parkinson-disease), [MSD Manual: Huntington Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/huntington-disease)

**Other basal ganglia disorders in your notes** (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 101-103):
- **Wilson disease** (hepatolenticular degeneration): autosomal recessive defect in copper transport, so copper builds up in the liver (fatty change, hepatitis, cirrhosis), the basal ganglia (**cystic degeneration of the putamen**, giving parkinsonism or chorea plus psychiatric symptoms) and the cornea (**Kayser-Fleischer ring** in Descemet membrane). Treatment: penicillamine (a chelator). Caudate = Huntington; putamen = Wilson.
- **Hemiballismus:** wild flinging of the **opposite** limbs from damage to the **subthalamic nucleus**, most often a lacunar stroke in someone with long-standing hypertension.
- **Tremors:** intention tremor = cerebellum; resting pill-rolling tremor = basal ganglia (Parkinson). **Essential tremor** is the most commonly diagnosed movement disorder, often autosomal dominant (familial), a slowly worsening symmetric **postural or action** tremor of the arms that **improves with alcohol**; first-line treatment is **propranolol**.

**Parkinson drugs in your notes: "BALSA"** (El-Husseiny Neurology, pp. 239-242)

Non-ergot: **pramipexole, ropinirole** | Long half-life smooths levodopa swings. Bromocriptine also treats high prolactin; pramipexole and ropinirole treat restless legs. - **Recent change (pergolide):** the FDA had pergolide withdrawn from human use in the US in 2007 because of serious **heart valve damage**. [JAVMA News: FDA announces voluntary withdrawal of pergolide (15 May 2007)](https://www.avma.org/javma-news/2007-06-01/fda-announces-voluntary-withdrawal-pergolide)
- **Recent change (amantadine for flu):** the CDC does not recommend amantadine or rimantadine to treat or prevent flu because resistance is high. [CDC: Influenza Antiviral Medications, Summary for Clinicians (updated 10 Mar 2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/hcp/antivirals/summary-clinicians.html)
- **Correction (selegiline and COMT):** your notes say selegiline blocks dopamine's conversion to 3-MT and tolcapone blocks its conversion to DOPAC. These are swapped. **MAO** turns dopamine into **DOPAC** (dihydroxyphenylacetic acid), and **COMT** turns it into **3-MT** (3-methoxytyramine) (Osmosis HY Physiology, p. 450, catecholamine degradation; Ninja Nerd Pharmacology, Parkinson drugs note). So selegiline (MAO-B) blocks the DOPAC route and tolcapone (COMT) blocks the 3-MT route.
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism:** usually from first-generation antipsychotics blocking D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway. Stop the drug, then treat with a central antimuscarinic (benztropine, trihexyphenidyl); avoid levodopa and dopamine agonists, which can bring on psychosis (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 242).
- **Tetrabenazine** blocks VMAT, so less dopamine is packaged into vesicles and released (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 243).

- **Drug-induced parkinsonism**, most often from antipsychotics, has normal dopamine-transporter imaging, which separates it from Parkinson disease. [MSD Manual: Parkinson Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/parkinson-disease)
- **Why levodopa and not dopamine:** "Levodopa, the metabolic precursor of dopamine, crosses the blood-brain barrier into the basal ganglia", where it is converted to dopamine. [MSD Manual: Parkinson Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/parkinson-disease) Dopamine itself cannot cross (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 240).

## Guillain-Barré syndrome
- **Presentation:** fairly **symmetric** flaccid weakness with tingling that starts in the **legs** and climbs to the arms; **reflexes are lost**; sphincters are usually spared. About 20% need a ventilator. Facial and throat muscles are weak in over half of severe cases. Autonomic instability can be life-threatening but is infrequent. [MSD Manual: Guillain-Barré Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/guillain-barr%C3%A9-syndrome-gbs)
- **Tests:** CSF shows **albuminocytologic dissociation** (high protein, normal white cell count), which can take up to a week to appear and is absent in 10%. Nerve conduction shows slow velocities and demyelination. [MSD Manual: Guillain-Barré Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/guillain-barr%C3%A9-syndrome-gbs)
- **Monitoring and treatment:** measure forced vital capacity every 6-8 hours; MSD: "if vital capacity is < 15 mL/kg, endotracheal intubation is indicated." Many review books teach a broader "20/30/40" rule (vital capacity below 20 mL/kg as a warning sign; standard teaching). **Plasma exchange or IVIG** are the treatments; **steroids are not recommended**. [MSD Manual: Guillain-Barré Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/guillain-barr%C3%A9-syndrome-gbs)
- **More from your notes:** weakness starts after a respiratory or gut infection has cleared; it can climb to the cranial nerves, especially the **facial nerve** (facial palsy in about half, usually both sides); paralysis of breathing muscles is fatal without support; autonomic swings (arrhythmias, high or low blood pressure) occur; the high CSF protein can cause papilledema; almost everyone survives and most recover fully over weeks to months. Treatment: plasma exchange or IVIG with breathing support. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 159)
- **Miller Fisher variant:** eye muscle weakness, ataxia and areflexia. [MSD Manual: Guillain-Barré Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/guillain-barr%C3%A9-syndrome-gbs) Osmosis adds that it hits CN III, IV and VI and goes with IgG antibodies to ganglioside **GQ1b**. Other variants: acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) and acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN). Weakness peaks within about 4 weeks; nerve pain can be treated with gabapentin or carbamazepine. (Osmosis HY Neurology, "Guillain-Barré syndrome")

**Worked example:** a 70 kg patient has an FVC of 1.0 L. 1,000 mL / 70 kg = 14.3 mL/kg, which is below 15 mL/kg, so intubation is indicated.

## Clinical vignette
A 24-year-old had bloody diarrhea 2 weeks ago. Over 3 days he develops tingling feet, then weakness that has climbed from his legs to his arms. Knee and ankle reflexes are absent. CSF: protein 110 mg/dL, white cells 2/µL. What is the diagnosis, and which treatment should be avoided?

**Answer:** Guillain-Barré syndrome after *Campylobacter jejuni*; steroids should be avoided (they don't help and may delay recovery). Treat with IVIG or plasma exchange and watch his vital capacity.
**Why:** ascending, symmetric weakness with areflexia after a gut infection, plus high CSF protein with a normal cell count (albuminocytologic dissociation), is GBS. [MSD Manual: Guillain-Barré Syndrome](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/peripheral-nervous-system-and-motor-unit-disorders/guillain-barr%C3%A9-syndrome-gbs) The normal CSF protein cutoff (about 45 mg/dL) is standard teaching.
**Trap:** high CSF protein with many white cells would suggest infection, not GBS.

## Common traps
- Memory loss first = Alzheimer; personality first = frontotemporal; hallucinations and fluctuations = Lewy body.
- Lewy body dementia + haloperidol = severe reaction; avoid antipsychotics.
- Parkinson tremor is at **rest**; essential tremor is with posture or action and improves with alcohol; cerebellar tremor is an intention tremor (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 103).
- Rapid dementia with startle myoclonus and periodic sharp waves = **CJD**; step-wise decline = **vascular dementia** (El-Husseiny p. 154).
- Lobar bleed in an elderly patient with dementia = **amyloid angiopathy** (p. 151).
- Drug-induced parkinsonism: stop the antipsychotic and give benztropine, **not** levodopa (p. 242).
- MAO makes DOPAC; COMT makes 3-MT (Osmosis HY Physiology, p. 450).
- Huntington: caudate atrophy, GABA loss, avoid VMAT2 inhibitors in depression.
- GBS: no steroids. Reflexes are lost (lower motor neuron pattern), unlike a spinal cord lesion.
- NPH: gait first and a shunt helps; CSF pressure is normal despite big ventricles.

## Practice questions
1. A 72-year-old has had 2 years of forgetting recent conversations and getting lost while driving. MRI shows hippocampal atrophy. What two lesions are found on autopsy?
   - **Answer:** extracellular beta-amyloid (neuritic) plaques and intracellular tau neurofibrillary tangles.
   - **Why:** these are the hallmarks of Alzheimer disease, which usually starts with short-term memory loss and mesial temporal atrophy. [MSD Manual: Alzheimer Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/alzheimer-disease)
   - **Trap:** Lewy bodies, which go with Lewy body dementia and Parkinson disease.
2. A 70-year-old with Parkinson disease is started on levodopa together with carbidopa. What does carbidopa do?
   - **Answer:** it blocks peripheral decarboxylase, so levodopa is not turned into dopamine outside the brain.
   - **Why:** this lowers the levodopa dose needed and reduces peripheral side effects. [MSD Manual: Parkinson Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/parkinson-disease)
   - **Trap:** thinking carbidopa enters the brain to raise dopamine; it works outside the brain (standard teaching that carbidopa doesn't cross the barrier).
3. A 40-year-old has jerky, dance-like movements, irritability and depression. His father had the same at 50. Which part of the brain shrinks?
   - **Answer:** the caudate nucleus (Huntington disease).
   - **Why:** autosomal dominant CAG expansion in *HTT* causes caudate atrophy and loss of striatal GABA neurons; repeats can grow from parent to child, so onset can be earlier. [MSD Manual: Huntington Disease](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/movement-and-cerebellar-disorders/huntington-disease)
   - **Trap:** treating the chorea with tetrabenazine without considering his depression.
4. A 75-year-old has a shuffling, "stuck to the floor" gait, urinary incontinence and mild forgetfulness. CT shows large ventricles with little cortical atrophy. What test predicts whether treatment will help?
   - **Answer:** remove 30-50 mL of CSF and look for improvement; if he improves, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt is likely to help.
   - **Why:** "Removal of 30 to 50 mL of CSF can be done as a diagnostic trial," and improvement in gait, continence and thinking afterward suggests a good candidate for a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. [MSD Manual: Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/normal-pressure-hydrocephalus)
   - **Trap:** Parkinson disease. The gait can look similar, but incontinence and large ventricles point to NPH.
5. A 63-year-old has 2 months of fast-worsening confusion and memory loss. A loud clap makes his limbs jerk. EEG shows periodic sharp waves. What protein change causes this?
   - **Answer:** prion protein refolding from alpha-helix into protease-resistant **beta-pleated sheet** (Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease).
   - **Why:** rapidly progressive dementia with startle myoclonus, periodic sharp waves and raised CSF 14-3-3 is CJD; the brain shows spongiform change without inflammation. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 154)
   - **Trap:** Alzheimer disease, which progresses over years, not months.
6. A 55-year-old on haloperidol develops a shuffling gait, rigidity and a resting tremor. After stopping haloperidol, which drug is preferred?
   - **Answer:** a central antimuscarinic such as benztropine.
   - **Why:** the antipsychotic blocked D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway; levodopa and dopamine agonists can bring on psychosis, so antimuscarinics are preferred. (El-Husseiny Neurology, p. 242)
   - **Trap:** levodopa-carbidopa.
