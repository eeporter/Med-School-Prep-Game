# Blood-thinning drugs: anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs and clot busters

Exam focus: NBME comprehensive (CBSE) and USMLE Step 1 · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-28
Based on your notes: El-Husseiny's Essentials for USMLE Step 1, Hematology & Oncology, Pharmacology chapter (warfarin pp. 88-90, heparin and LMWH pp. 91-94, direct thrombin and factor Xa inhibitors pp. 95-96, thrombolytics p. 97, antiplatelet drugs pp. 98-100). The cancer drugs (pp. 102-111) are in guide 43. Each fact is also checked against the MSD Manual; where another source disagrees, guidance has changed, or a document is wrong, a marked note says so.

## In one line
Ask **what each drug blocks**. **Warfarin** stops the liver making working vitamin K factors (follow the **PT/INR**). **Heparin** boosts **antithrombin** (follow the **PTT**). **"-xabans"** block **factor Xa**, and **dabigatran** blocks **thrombin**, directly. **Antiplatelet drugs** stop platelets sticking together. **Thrombolytics** turn on **plasmin** to dissolve a clot that has already formed. Then learn each drug's **antidote** and its **signature side effect**.

Memory hook (standard teaching): **"WEPT"**: **W**arfarin = **E**xtrinsic pathway = **PT**. Heparin goes with the other test, the PTT (intrinsic).

## Warfarin (pp. 88-90)
- **How it works:** blocks **vitamin K epoxide reductase**, so the liver cannot **γ-carboxylate** factors **II, VII, IX and X** and **proteins C and S**. Gene differences in **VKORC1** change the dose a person needs (p. 88).
- **Lab:** **factor VII** falls first, so the **PT** rises first; the **INR** (international normalized ratio) is the patient's PT compared with a control. Target **INR 2-3**; bleeding risk rises above **3** (pp. 88-89).
- **Uses:** long-term anticoagulation: preventing clots after venous thromboembolism and **stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation** (p. 89).
- **Not in pregnancy:** warfarin **crosses the placenta**; heparin does not (p. 89).
- **Side effects:** bleeding, birth defects, **skin necrosis**, many drug interactions (p. 89).

### Why warfarin can cause clots at first (pp. 89-90)
| Protein | Role | Half-life rank (your notes) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Factor VII** | Clotting | **Shortest** |
| **Protein C** | **Natural anticoagulant** | **Second shortest** |
| Factors II, IX, X | Clotting | Longer; fall slowly |

- In the first days, **protein C falls faster than II, IX and X**, so the balance tips toward **clotting**. Small skin vessels clot → **warfarin skin necrosis**, worst with **inherited protein C (or S) deficiency** (p. 90).
- Treatment: **stop warfarin** and give **fresh frozen plasma** to replace protein C (p. 90).
- **Heparin "bridge":** start heparin with warfarin so antithrombin covers that early clotting window; this lowers repeat clots and skin necrosis (p. 90).

**Other source (warfarin start-up):** MSD: warfarin "takes about 5 days to achieve desired therapeutic effect", so heparin is continued until it works; "Rarely, warfarin causes skin necrosis in patients with inherited protein C deficiency" (also protein S); target "INR of 2.0 to 3.0" ([MSD: Medications for DVT, updated May 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/peripheral-venous-disorders/medications-for-deep-venous-thrombosis)).

### Reversing warfarin (p. 90)
- Your notes: **vitamin K** to reverse; **fresh frozen plasma** for rapid reversal (p. 90).
- **Rat poison:** most rodenticides contain **brodifacoum**, a **long-acting** warfarin relative ("superwarfarin"; nickname is standard teaching). A patient who is bleeding needs **fresh frozen plasma plus vitamin K** right away (p. 90).

**Recent change (fast warfarin reversal):** MSD now says "If hemorrhage is severe, prothrombin complex concentrate should be given" and "fresh frozen plasma may be used if prothrombin complex concentrate is unavailable" (MSD Medications for DVT). **PCC** (prothrombin complex concentrate) is a concentrate of the vitamin K-dependent factors (standard teaching). So for life-threatening warfarin bleeding, the current first choice is **PCC + vitamin K**; FFP is the backup.

## Heparin (pp. 91-92, 94)
- **How it works:** heparin's **pentasaccharide** binds **antithrombin** and changes its shape so it inactivates **factor Xa** faster. Only **unfractionated heparin** has chains long enough (**more than 18 sugar units**) to hold **antithrombin and thrombin together**, so it blocks **thrombin and Xa equally** (p. 91).
- **Short half-life**; follow the **PTT** (pp. 91-92).
- **Uses:** **immediate** anticoagulation for **PE** (pulmonary embolism), **DVT** (deep vein thrombosis), **acute coronary syndrome / MI**; **safe in pregnancy** (does not cross the placenta) (p. 92).
- **Side effects:** bleeding, **HIT**, **osteoporosis** with long use, drug interactions (p. 92).
- **Antidote:** **protamine sulfate**, a peptide that **binds heparin** into an inactive complex (chemical inactivation) (p. 92).

**Other source (heparin details):** MSD: unfractionated heparin is adjusted to an "activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT] 1.5 to 2.5 times" normal; protamine works better on unfractionated heparin than on LMWH (MSD Medications for DVT). MSD's hemostasis page confirms that antithrombin "inhibits thrombin, factor Xa, factor XIa, and factor IXa", that heparins speed this up, and that fondaparinux enhances antithrombin against **Xa but not thrombin** ([MSD: Overview of Hemostasis, updated Sept 2025](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology/hemostasis/overview-of-hemostasis)).

### HIT: heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (pp. 92, 94)
- **IgG antibodies** against **heparin bound to platelet factor 4 (PF4)**. The antibody-heparin-PF4 complex **activates platelets** → platelets are used up (**low count**) and form **clots** (p. 92).
- HIT causes **clots more often than bleeding** ("paradoxical thrombosis"); it is much more common with **unfractionated heparin** than with LMWH; your notes call heparin **the most important cause of low platelets in hospital patients** (pp. 93-94).
- **First step:** **stop all heparin**. Patients still need anticoagulation, with drugs that **do not need antithrombin**; your notes name the **direct thrombin inhibitors** (hirudin, lepirudin, argatroban, dabigatran) (p. 94).

**Other source (HIT numbers):** MSD: HIT "occurs in up to 2.4% of patients receiving unfractionated heparin"; LMWH "is ten-fold less immunogenic"; it can happen even with "very-low-dose heparin (eg, used in flushes...)"; stop heparin if the platelet count "decreases by more than 50%" while tests are pending; LMWH "cannot be used" in HIT because the antibodies cross-react; and "Warfarin should not be substituted for heparin" (start warfarin only after platelets recover) ([MSD: Thrombocytopenia: Other Causes, updated Jun 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hematology-and-oncology/thrombocytopenia-and-platelet-dysfunction/thrombocytopenia-other-causes)).

**Recent change (HIT drugs):** MSD's current list is "argatroban, bivalirudin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, fondaparinux", used "at least until platelet recovery"; fondaparinux "is an acceptable alternative in many patients" but its long half-life makes it a poor choice before a procedure (same MSD page). Your notes' list includes hirudin and lepirudin; MSD's list does not.

### Clots in pregnancy (p. 94)
- Pregnancy raises clot risk: **more clotting factors**, **less fibrinolysis**, **lower protein S**, looser veins and the **uterus pressing on the IVC** (inferior vena cava) (p. 94).
- Your notes say DVT happens **equally in all three trimesters**; treatment is **heparin**, because warfarin is teratogenic (p. 94).

**Other source (timing and drug):** MSD says "most thromboemboli develop postpartum and result from vascular trauma during delivery", with risk raised for **about 6 weeks after delivery**; it does not compare trimesters. MSD names **LMWH** as "the anticoagulant of choice" in pregnancy (too large to cross the placenta) and says "Warfarin crosses the placenta and may cause fetal abnormalities or death" ([MSD: Thromboembolic Disorders in Pregnancy, updated Jul 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/gynecology-and-obstetrics/approach-to-the-pregnant-woman-and-prenatal-care/thromboembolic-disorders-in-pregnancy)).

## Low-molecular-weight heparins (p. 93)
- **Enoxaparin, dalteparin.** They activate antithrombin but act **mainly on Xa**, not thrombin (p. 93).
- Better absorbed, **2-4 times longer half-life**, given **under the skin**, **no routine lab monitoring** (p. 93).
- Less HIT than unfractionated heparin; **protamine works poorly** (p. 93). Your notes add that enoxaparin lowered death and repeat MI compared with unfractionated heparin in acute MI trials (p. 93).

**Other source (the enoxaparin trials):** the trials behind p. 93 are **ESSENCE and TIMI 11B** (your notes print "TIMM 1"). Both enrolled patients with **unstable angina or non-ST-elevation MI**, not ST-elevation MI. Pooled, "death/nonfatal MI was approximately 20% lower in patients receiving enoxaparin at all time points" (odds ratio 0.77 at day 8), but "pooled overall mortality trended lower in the enoxaparin group, but did not reach statistical significance" ([ACC: TIMI 11B/ESSENCE Meta-Analysis](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2010/02/23/19/23/TIMI-11BESSENCE-MetaAnalysis)). So enoxaparin cut the **combined** death-or-MI outcome, not death by itself.

**Other source (LMWH):** MSD: LMWHs "are as effective as unfractionated heparin (UFH) for reducing DVT recurrence", can be used "on an outpatient basis", and need **reduced doses in kidney failure** (or a switch to UFH); protamine "only partially neutralizes" them (MSD Medications for DVT).

## Fondaparinux (p. 96)
- A **synthetic pentasaccharide**: an **indirect** Xa inhibitor (works through antithrombin). It is too short to bridge antithrombin to thrombin, so it has **no anti-thrombin effect** (p. 96).
- **Other source:** MSD: fondaparinux "is less likely to cause thrombocytopenia" (MSD Medications for DVT).

## Direct oral anticoagulants (p. 95)
| | **Direct thrombin inhibitors** | **Direct factor Xa inhibitors** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drugs | **Bivalirudin** (from hirudin, the leech anticoagulant), **argatroban**, **dabigatran** (the only **oral** one) | **Apixaban, rivaroxaban** ("**Xa**" in the name) |
| Action | Block **free and clot-bound thrombin** | Bind the **active site of factor Xa** |
| Uses | Venous clots, atrial fibrillation, **HIT** | DVT/PE treatment and prevention, stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation |
| Monitoring | Not needed | Not needed |
| Reversal | **Idarucizumab** (dabigatran); activated PCC or tranexamic acid can be tried | **Andexanet alfa** in your notes |

**Other source (starting DOACs):** MSD: dabigatran needs **5 days of LMWH first**, and so does **edoxaban** (5-7 days, a third Xa inhibitor); **rivaroxaban and apixaban** can start immediately with no heparin lead-in. Idarucizumab is "a humanized monoclonal antibody" (MSD Medications for DVT).

**Recent change (andexanet alfa):** andexanet alfa (Andexxa) was **withdrawn from the US market** in **December 2025**. The FDA concluded "the serious risks outweigh the product's benefits", based on postmarketing data showing "an increased risk of serious and fatal thromboembolic events"; AstraZeneca withdrew it "for commercial reasons" ([AABB News, 23 Dec 2025](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2025/12/23/astrazeneca-withdraws-factor-xa-reversal-agent-from-u.s.-market)). In the ANNEXA-I bleeding-in-the-brain trial, clots by 30 days were **14.6% vs 6.9%** with andexanet vs usual care; it remains sold in Europe, the UK and Japan ([TCTMD, 22 Dec 2025](https://www.tctmd.com/news/andexanet-alfa-pulled-us-market)). MSD's DVT drug page (updated May 2026) still calls it "available in the United States", so that line is out of date. For Step 1, still know **andexanet = decoy factor Xa that soaks up Xa inhibitors** (mechanism is standard teaching).

## Thrombolytics (p. 97)
- **Drugs:** **alteplase (tPA)**, **reteplase**, **tenecteplase**, **streptokinase** (p. 97).
- **How they work:** help turn **plasminogen into plasmin**, which breaks down clots. **Fibrin-specific** drugs (tPA, reteplase, tenecteplase) act mainly on fibrin in a fresh clot; **streptokinase** is not fibrin-specific (p. 97). **PT and PTT rise; platelet count does not change** (p. 97).
- **Uses (your notes):** early **MI within 6 hours**, early **ischemic stroke**, severe **PE** (p. 97).
- **Side effects:** **bleeding** (in theory less with fibrin-specific drugs); **reperfusion arrhythmias** when the artery opens, usually harmless (p. 97).
- **Toxicity:** **aminocaproic acid** or **tranexamic acid** (antifibrinolytics); FFP and cryoprecipitate replace factors (p. 97).

**Other source (plasmin and tPA):** MSD: plasmin "proteolyzes fibrin into soluble fibrin degradation products"; tPA is "a poor activator of plasminogen when free in solution but an efficient activator when bound to fibrin" (MSD Overview of Hemostasis). That is why tPA is called fibrin-specific.

**Other source (time window and agents):** for STEMI (ST-elevation MI) when PCI (a catheter procedure to open the artery) is not available, MSD says "Greatest benefit occurs within 3 hours, but the medications may be effective up to 12 hours" ([MSD: Overview of Revascularization for ACS, updated Jul 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/coronary-artery-disease/overview-of-revascularization-for-acute-coronary-syndromes)), wider than your notes' 6 hours. MSD prefers tenecteplase, reteplase and alteplase; streptokinase is "not available in the United States"; and fibrinolytics "worsen outcome for unstable angina and non-ST elevation myocardial infarction" ([MSD: Medications for ACS, updated Jul 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/coronary-artery-disease/medications-for-acute-coronary-syndromes)).

**Correction (contraindications):** p. 97's absolute list includes "Recent surgery (within the last 2 weeks)", "Severe hypertension (above 180/110)" and "Non-hemorrhagic stroke within the last 6 months". MSD's current STEMI list is different (MSD Overview of Revascularization for ACS):

| | **Absolute** (MSD) | **Relative** (MSD) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | **Over 180/110 that does not respond to treatment** | Over 180/110 on arrival, or long-standing poorly controlled hypertension |
| Stroke | **Any previous brain bleed**; **ischemic stroke within 3 months** (except an acute ischemic stroke being treated) | Ischemic stroke **more than 3 months** ago |
| Trauma and surgery | **Closed head or face trauma within 3 months**; **brain or spinal surgery within 2 months** | **Major surgery within 3 weeks** |
| Bleeding | **Active bleeding** (not menses) or a bleeding disorder | |
| Other | **Suspected aortic dissection**; **malignant brain tumor**; **known cerebrovascular malformation** | |

Your notes' points about **bleeding into the bowel or brain** and that **heme-positive brown stool is not an absolute contraindication** (p. 97) fit this table (the stool point is from your notes only).

## Antiplatelet drugs (pp. 98-100)
Your notes group them by what they block (p. 98):
1. **Stop the signal being made:** **aspirin** lowers **thromboxane A2** (TXA2).
2. **Block the signal's receptor:** **clopidogrel, ticlopidine** (and prasugrel, ticagrelor) block the **ADP receptor**.
3. **Block the final common step:** **abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban** block **GP IIb/IIIa**.
4. **Raise cAMP inside platelets:** **cilostazol, dipyridamole** block **phosphodiesterase**.

| Drug group | Mechanism | Uses | Side effects | Pages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ADP (P2Y12) receptor blockers**: clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticlopidine (**irreversible**); ticagrelor (**reversible**) | Block **P2Y12** → GP IIb/IIIa is not switched on | Acute coronary syndrome, **stents**, preventing stroke; **with aspirin** for an added effect | **Ticlopidine: neutropenia (~1%)** with fever and mouth ulcers (check blood counts every 2 weeks for 3 months); **TTP** | 98-99 |
| **GP IIb/IIIa blockers**: abciximab (antibody Fab fragments), eptifibatide, tirofiban | Block the **fibrinogen receptor** on activated platelets | Unstable angina, coronary angioplasty | **Bleeding, low platelets** | 99 |
| **Cilostazol, dipyridamole** | **Phosphodiesterase** inhibition (your notes: PDE III) → **↑ cAMP** → less aggregation; also **vasodilators** | **Intermittent claudication**, stroke/TIA prevention (with aspirin), angina | Nausea, **headache, flushing**, low BP, belly pain | 100 |

- **Aspirin allergy** (wheezing, bronchospasm): your notes name **clopidogrel** as the best alternative (p. 99).
- Your notes lump dipyridamole with cilostazol as a PDE III blocker. Cilostazol is the selective PDE III blocker; dipyridamole is a less selective PDE inhibitor that also **blocks adenosine uptake**, raising adenosine (standard teaching).

**Other source (ACS antiplatelet details):** MSD lists P2Y12 inhibitors as "clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, cangrelor" (cangrelor is IV); ticlopidine is "Rarely used routinely because neutropenia is a risk"; prasugrel is "Not used in combination with fibrinolytic therapy"; and "morphine and fentanyl interfere with some P2Y12 receptor inhibitors" (MSD Medications for ACS).

**Other source (cilostazol):** MSD: "Cilostazol is contraindicated in patients with systolic heart failure"; it is used with supervised exercise for claudication and "is not a substitute for risk factor modification and exercise"; aspirin or clopidogrel is recommended for everyone with symptoms of PAD (peripheral artery disease) ([MSD: Peripheral Artery Disease, updated Feb 2026](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/peripheral-artery-disorders/peripheral-artery-disease)).

## Clinical vignette
A 54-year-old woman starts warfarin for a new DVT, without heparin. On day 4 she has painful, dark purple patches on her thighs and breasts that turn black. Her sister had the same reaction.
- **Diagnosis:** **warfarin skin necrosis**, likely with **inherited protein C deficiency**.
- **Why:** protein C has the second-shortest half-life of the vitamin K proteins, so it drops before factors II, IX and X; with a low starting level, small skin vessels clot (pp. 89-90; MSD Medications for DVT).
- **Next step:** stop warfarin; give **fresh frozen plasma** to replace protein C (p. 90) and continue a heparin-type anticoagulant (standard teaching).
- **Trap:** **HIT**, which needs heparin exposure and a falling platelet count.

## Common traps
- **Warfarin = PT/INR; heparin = PTT.**
- **Warfarin crosses the placenta; heparin and LMWH do not.** LMWH is the pregnancy choice.
- **Skin necrosis in the first days of warfarin = protein C falls first.** Prevent it with a heparin bridge.
- **Platelets drop by half 5-10 days into heparin, with new clots = HIT** (5-10 day timing is standard teaching). Stop **all** heparin, including flushes and LMWH; use a non-heparin drug; **no warfarin until platelets recover**.
- **Protamine reverses unfractionated heparin well, LMWH poorly.**
- **Idarucizumab reverses dabigatran.** Andexanet alfa (for Xa inhibitors) left the US market in Dec 2025.
- **Life-threatening warfarin bleed = PCC + vitamin K** (FFP if no PCC).
- **Brodifacoum rat poison = very long-lasting warfarin effect.**
- **Thrombolytics raise PT and PTT but not the platelet count;** reverse with **aminocaproic acid or tranexamic acid.**
- **Any prior brain bleed = never thrombolyse.** Ischemic stroke within 3 months = absolute contraindication (MSD). **Tearing chest pain to the back = think aortic dissection; no thrombolytics** (dissection is on MSD's absolute list; the symptom is standard teaching).
- **Aspirin blocks TXA2 synthesis; clopidogrel blocks P2Y12 (ADP); abciximab blocks GP IIb/IIIa; cilostazol raises cAMP.**
- **Ticlopidine = neutropenia and TTP. Cilostazol = not in systolic heart failure.**

## Practice questions
1. A 54-year-old woman begins warfarin alone for a DVT. On day 4 she develops painful purple skin patches on her thighs that become black. Which mechanism best explains this reaction?
   - **Answer:** **protein C falls faster than factors II, IX and X**, causing a short-lived clotting tendency and clots in small skin vessels.
   - **Why:** factor VII has the shortest half-life and protein C the second shortest; the procoagulant factors fall more slowly (p. 90). Inherited protein C deficiency makes it worse (MSD Medications for DVT).
   - **Trap:** **antibodies against heparin-PF4** (HIT), which needs heparin exposure.

2. A 71-year-old man is on unfractionated heparin after hip replacement. On day 7 his platelet count is 92,000/mcL (210,000/mcL before surgery), and his left calf is newly swollen. Ultrasound shows a DVT. Which is the most appropriate change to his anticoagulation?
   - **Answer:** **stop all heparin and start a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban.**
   - **Why:** the count fell by more than half, with a new clot, during heparin = HIT (pp. 92, 94). MSD lists argatroban, bivalirudin, apixaban, rivaroxaban and fondaparinux (MSD Thrombocytopenia: Other Causes).
   - **Trap:** **switching to enoxaparin** (HIT antibodies cross-react with LMWH) or **starting warfarin now** (MSD: only after platelets recover).

3. A 29-year-old woman at 10 weeks' gestation has a swollen, tender left leg; ultrasound confirms a DVT. Which property makes the most appropriate drug safe for her fetus?
   - **Answer:** **it is too large to cross the placenta** (low-molecular-weight heparin).
   - **Why:** heparin does not cross the placenta; warfarin does (pp. 89, 92). MSD: LMWH is "the anticoagulant of choice" in pregnancy (MSD Thromboembolic Disorders in Pregnancy).
   - **Trap:** **warfarin**, which crosses the placenta and can cause fetal abnormalities or death.

4. A 76-year-old man on warfarin for atrial fibrillation falls and develops a large brain bleed. His INR is 6.8. Which is the most appropriate treatment to reverse the anticoagulation most quickly?
   - **Answer:** **prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) plus IV vitamin K.**
   - **Why:** MSD: "If hemorrhage is severe, prothrombin complex concentrate should be given"; fresh frozen plasma "may be used if prothrombin complex concentrate is unavailable" (MSD Medications for DVT). Vitamin K works only after the liver makes new factors, so it is slow on its own (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **fresh frozen plasma**, the rapid answer in your notes (p. 90) and the backup today.

5. A 68-year-old woman taking dabigatran for atrial fibrillation has a severe GI bleed. Which is the most appropriate agent to reverse her anticoagulant?
   - **Answer:** **idarucizumab**, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds dabigatran.
   - **Why:** dabigatran is the oral direct thrombin inhibitor; idarucizumab reverses it (p. 95; MSD Medications for DVT).
   - **Trap:** **protamine**, which binds heparin, not dabigatran.

6. A 59-year-old man has crushing chest pain for 2 hours, and the ECG shows ST elevation in V1-V4. The nearest catheterization lab is 4 hours away, so he is given tenecteplase. Which mechanism best explains how this drug opens the artery?
   - **Answer:** it **turns fibrin-bound plasminogen into plasmin**, which breaks down fibrin.
   - **Why:** fibrin-specific thrombolytic (p. 97); MSD: tPA is "an efficient activator when bound to fibrin", and plasmin "proteolyzes fibrin" (MSD Overview of Hemostasis).
   - **Trap:** **blocking GP IIb/IIIa** (abciximab), which stops new platelet clumping but does not dissolve an existing clot.

7. A 63-year-old man with asthma who wheezes after taking aspirin has a coronary stent placed. Which mechanism describes the most appropriate antiplatelet drug for him?
   - **Answer:** **irreversible blockade of the P2Y12 ADP receptor**, so GP IIb/IIIa is not switched on (clopidogrel).
   - **Why:** your notes name clopidogrel as the best alternative in aspirin allergy (p. 99).
   - **Trap:** **COX inhibition** (aspirin), the drug that caused his wheezing.

8. A 67-year-old man has calf pain after walking two blocks that goes away with rest. His ankle-brachial index is low and his ejection fraction is 60%. He starts a drug that lengthens his walking distance. Which mechanism best describes this drug?
   - **Answer:** **phosphodiesterase III inhibition → more cAMP** in platelets and vessel walls (cilostazol).
   - **Why:** cilostazol is used for intermittent claudication (p. 100); MSD: it is "contraindicated in patients with systolic heart failure", which is why the normal ejection fraction matters (MSD PAD).
   - **Trap:** **P2Y12 blockade** (clopidogrel), which prevents heart attacks and strokes in PAD but is not the claudication drug.

9. During cardiac surgery, a patient receives a large dose of unfractionated heparin. At the end of the operation the surgeon wants it reversed. The drug given works by which mechanism?
   - **Answer:** it **binds heparin directly** into an inactive complex (protamine sulfate; chemical inactivation).
   - **Why:** p. 92; MSD notes protamine works well on unfractionated heparin and only partly on LMWH (MSD Medications for DVT).
   - **Trap:** **giving vitamin K**, which reverses warfarin.
