# IgA nephropathy (Berger disease)

Exam focus: USMLE Step 1 and NBME · Study material, not patient care guidance · Auditor: cleared 2026-10-27

## In one line
A young adult gets cola-colored urine within 1-2 days of a cold, complement is normal, and the biopsy shows IgA in the mesangium.

## Core mechanism
1. Some people make IgA1 that is missing galactose sugars (galactose-deficient IgA1). [StatPearls: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/); MSD describes this as "defective IgA1 glycosylation": [MSD Manual: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)
2. The immune system makes antibodies against this abnormal IgA1, forming circulating immune complexes. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/)
3. The complexes deposit in the **mesangium** (the support tissue between glomerular capillaries) and trigger mesangial cell proliferation. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/)
4. A mucosal infection (upper respiratory, sinus or gut) boosts IgA production (standard teaching), so hematuria flares soon after the infection: MSD says gross hematuria "usually begins 1 or 2 days after a febrile mucosal (upper respiratory, sinus, enteral) illness" ([MSD Manual: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)). Your notes say IgA production rises during the infection, and the painless hematuria usually starts **2-3 days** after an upper respiratory infection (or gastroenteritis), lasts several days, fades, and comes back every few months (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82). NBME Top Concepts uses the same split: 2-3 days after a sore throat means IgA nephropathy, 2-4 weeks means poststreptococcal GN (pp. 307, 335).
5. Osmosis adds that the mesangial complexes switch on the **alternative complement pathway**, which draws in inflammatory cells and injures the glomerulus (Osmosis HY Pathology Vol. 2, p. 831).

## High-yield facts
- Often called the most common glomerulonephritis worldwide; peaks in the second and third decades; male predominance about 2.5:1 in the US and Europe. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/) Your notes also call it the most common nephropathy worldwide (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82; Osmosis HY Pathology Vol. 2, p. 831).
- **Other source (age):** your notes say it "presents during childhood" (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82; Osmosis Vol. 2, p. 831 says "usually"). StatPearls puts the peak in the second and third decades (teens to twenties). Expect exam patients from older children to young adults.
- **Other source (timing):** your notes say 2-3 days after the infection; MSD says usually 1 or 2 days after. All agree it is **days, not weeks**.
- Serum complement is usually normal. Plasma IgA may be high but is not diagnostic. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)
- Diagnosis needs a biopsy. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/)
  - Light microscopy: mesangial proliferation and matrix expansion.
  - Immunofluorescence: granular IgA in the mesangium (C3 often co-deposits; C1q usually absent, standard teaching). [MSD Manual: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)
  - Electron microscopy: mesangial electron-dense deposits.
  - Your notes give the same three findings: mesangial proliferation on light microscopy, IgA immune complexes in the mesangium on immunofluorescence, and mesangial deposits on electron microscopy (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82; Osmosis Vol. 2, p. 831).
- **Your notes on Henoch-Schönlein purpura (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82):** IgA nephropathy plus extrarenal signs. Skin: purpura on the extensor surfaces of the arms, legs and buttocks. Gut: abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding and **intussusception**. A skin biopsy shows necrotizing vasculitis of small dermal vessels with IgA in the dermal capillaries. NBME Top Concepts lists arthritis, palpable purpura on the legs, kidney disease and intussusception (p. 307).
- IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura) is the systemic version: same IgA deposits plus purpura on the legs and feet, joint pain and colicky abdominal pain, mostly in children. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/), [MSD Manual: IgA Vasculitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/musculoskeletal-and-connective-tissue-disorders/vasculitis/immunoglobulin-a-associated-vasculitis-igav)
- Mesangial IgA can be secondary to cirrhosis, celiac disease and HIV. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)
- Prognosis: your notes say many patients keep normal kidney function for decades, and about 25-50% slowly progress to chronic kidney failure (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82). Up to half follow a benign course, but 20-50% reach end-stage kidney disease within 20 years. ACE inhibitors or ARBs are first-line to reduce proteinuria. Newer add-ons include SGLT2 inhibitors and sparsentan (a dual endothelin and angiotensin receptor blocker). The FDA also gave full approval to **Tarpeyo** (delayed-release oral budesonide) on 20 Dec 2023 to reduce loss of kidney function in adults at risk of progression ([Drugs@FDA, NDA 215935: SUPPL-3, "Efficacy-Labeling Change With Clinical Data", action date 12/20/2023](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=215935); reported by [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/nephrology/20240109/fda-grants-full-approval-of-tarpeyo-for-iga-nephropathy)). [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/); MSD gives progression in 5-60% at 10 years and covers ACE inhibitors/ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors and sparsentan: [MSD Manual: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)

## Compare and contrast: IgA nephropathy vs poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN)
| Feature | IgA nephropathy | Poststreptococcal GN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timing after infection | Days (your notes: 2-3 days; MSD: usually 1-2 days after) | Your notes: 2-4 weeks after throat or skin infection; StatPearls: 1-2 weeks after a sore throat, 6 weeks after impetigo |
| Typical patient | Young adult, often male | Child (your notes: worse prognosis in adults) |
| Serum C3 | Usually normal | Low; normal again in 6-8 weeks |
| Antibody tests | None specific | Raised ASO or anti-DNase B (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 79) |
| Immunofluorescence | Mesangial IgA | Granular IgG and C3 on capillary walls ("starry sky", "lumpy-bumpy"); no IgA |
| Electron microscopy | Mesangial deposits | Subepithelial "humps" |
| Course | Recurrent episodes; can progress | Usually resolves |

PSGN in your notes: a type III hypersensitivity from nephritogenic group A strep strains carrying M protein; tea or cola urine, periorbital edema and hypertension; diffuse hypercellular glomeruli; subepithelial humps of IgG, IgM and C3; supportive treatment. About 95% of children recover fully, while only about 60% of adults do (El-Husseiny Nephrology, pp. 79-81).

**Other source (PSGN delay):** your notes and NBME Top Concepts give 2-4 weeks after either a throat or skin infection (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 79; NBME Top Concepts, p. 307). StatPearls (updated December 2025) says it "most frequently presents in children aged 1 to 2 weeks after a sore throat or 6 weeks after a skin infection (impetigo)." [StatPearls: Infection-Related Glomerulonephritis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538255/) On the exam, a delay of weeks means PSGN; a skin infection gives the longer delay.

Sources: [StatPearls: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/), [MSD Manual: IgA Nephropathy](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy), [StatPearls: Infection-Related Glomerulonephritis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538255/)

## Clinical vignette
A 22-year-old man notices dark red urine the day after a sore throat and fever begin. Blood pressure is 128/80. Urinalysis shows red blood cells and red cell casts. Serum C3 is normal. This is his third similar episode in two years. What will immunofluorescence on a kidney biopsy show?

**Answer:** granular IgA deposits in the mesangium (IgA nephropathy).
**Why:** hematuria starting during the infection (synpharyngitic), recurrent episodes, a young man and normal complement all fit IgA nephropathy rather than PSGN. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy), [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/)
**Trap:** choosing subepithelial humps or low C3, which belong to PSGN after a 1-2 week delay.

## Common traps
- The time gap is the fastest discriminator: days means IgA nephropathy, weeks means PSGN (NBME Top Concepts, p. 307).
- Purpura plus belly pain in a child can hide **intussusception**, a gut complication of IgA vasculitis (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 82; NBME Top Concepts, p. 307).
- Normal C3 points away from PSGN (and away from membranoproliferative GN and lupus, which also lower complement; standard teaching).
- IgA deposits sit in the **mesangium**; PSGN deposits sit on the **subepithelial** side of the capillary wall.
- Same pathology plus purpura on the legs and belly pain in a child means IgA vasculitis, not isolated IgA nephropathy.

## Practice questions
1. A 9-year-old has cola-colored urine, periorbital edema and hypertension 2 weeks after strep throat. What is the serum C3?
   - **Answer:** low.
   - **Why:** PSGN activates complement; C3 is low in the acute phase and normalizes in 6-8 weeks. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538255/) Your notes: C3 is low from consumption (El-Husseiny Nephrology, p. 79).
   - **Trap:** normal C3 belongs to IgA nephropathy.
2. A biopsy shows mesangial proliferation, mesangial IgA on immunofluorescence and mesangial electron-dense deposits. The patient also has purpura on the legs, ankle pain and colicky abdominal pain. Diagnosis?
   - **Answer:** IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura).
   - **Why:** same IgA deposition as IgA nephropathy, plus skin, joint and gut involvement. [MSD Manual: IgA Vasculitis](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/musculoskeletal-and-connective-tissue-disorders/vasculitis/immunoglobulin-a-associated-vasculitis-igav)
   - **Trap:** calling it isolated IgA nephropathy and ignoring the systemic signs.
3. Which abnormal molecule starts the immune-complex process in IgA nephropathy?
   - **Answer:** galactose-deficient IgA1.
   - **Why:** antibodies form against it, and the resulting complexes deposit in the mesangium. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538214/)
   - **Trap:** choosing IgG anti-streptococcal antibodies (PSGN) or anti-GBM antibodies (Goodpasture).
4. A 56-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis has microscopic hematuria and mild proteinuria. Kidney biopsy shows mesangial IgA deposits. What is the most likely cause of the IgA deposits?
   - **Answer:** his liver disease (secondary IgA deposition, not primary IgA nephropathy).
   - **Why:** liver disease (cirrhosis), celiac disease and HIV can all cause mesangial IgA deposits. [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/genitourinary-disorders/glomerular-disorders/immunoglobulin-a-nephropathy)
   - **Trap:** mesangial IgA is not specific to primary IgA nephropathy.
