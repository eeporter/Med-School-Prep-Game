## In one line
**Sort the organism by shape, then by the clue in the story:**
1. **Gram-negative diplococci** (oxidase positive):
   - ***N. meningitidis*** ferments **maltose and glucose**; ***N. gonorrhoeae*** ferments **glucose only** (El-Husseiny Micro p. 98);
   - ***Moraxella catarrhalis***: a close relative that causes otitis, sinusitis and COPD flares (p. 106).
2. **Gram-negative coccobacilli** (small, round-ended rods), each with a classic clue:
   - ***Haemophilus***: needs **X and V factors**; grows on chocolate agar or around *S. aureus* (p. 107);
   - ***Bordetella pertussis***: whooping cough in an unvaccinated infant (p. 114);
   - ***Pasteurella***: cat or dog bite (p. 111);
   - ***Francisella***: rabbits and ticks (p. 112);
   - ***Brucella***: unpasteurized dairy, farm animals (pp. 114-115).
3. **Faint-staining rod from water:** ***Legionella*** (silver stain, BCYE agar) (p. 116).

## Core mechanism: why capsules and complement matter
- **Capsules block phagocytosis.** *N. meningitidis* and Hib both rely on a **polysaccharide capsule** (pp. 99, 107). Antibody to the capsule plus complement clears them.
- **Who is at risk:**
  - **children under about 3** cannot yet make good antibody to polysaccharide capsules (p. 101);
  - people missing the **terminal complement proteins (C5-C9)** get repeated *Neisseria* infections (pp. 101, 104);
  - **no spleen** (surgery or sickle cell): poor clearance of encapsulated bacteria (Osmosis p. 452);
  - **complement-blocking drugs** (eculizumab for paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria) raise the risk of life-threatening *Neisseria* infection, so patients are vaccinated against meningococcus (Osmosis p. 433; MSD adds ravulizumab).
- **Why conjugate vaccines work in infants:** a plain sugar antigen is **T-cell independent** and works poorly under age 2. Linking it to a protein (tetanus or diphtheria toxoid) makes it **T-cell dependent**, giving strong IgG and memory (El-Husseiny Micro p. 110; CDC Pink Book).
- **Endotoxin:** *Neisseria* make **LOS**, which triggers **TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8**, causing septic shock (p. 100).

## Neisseria meningitidis (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 98-101)
- **Carriage:** nasopharynx of 5-30% of healthy people (p. 98; MSD gives 5-40%). Spread by **respiratory droplets**, often from healthy carriers.
- **Virulence factors** (p. 99):
  - **capsule** (most important);
  - **LOS** endotoxin;
  - **pili** for attachment;
  - **IgA1 protease**, which destroys mucosal IgA.
- **Route** (p. 99): pharynx → blood → choroid plexus → meninges.
- **Disease** (pp. 99-101):
  - **meningitis**: the second most common cause in US adults after pneumococcus; outbreaks in **military recruits and college students**;
  - **meningococcemia** (often without meningitis): fever, **petechiae and purpura**, shock, DIC;
  - **Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome**: **bilateral adrenal hemorrhage**, DIC and shock.
- **Treatment (MSD):** **ceftriaxone** (or penicillin G where strains are susceptible). Empiric therapy while waiting for results adds vancomycin.
- **Close contacts need prophylaxis** (MSD): rifampin, ceftriaxone or ciprofloxacin.
  - **Correction (Osmosis p. 511):** Osmosis gives contacts "a single dose of ceftriaxone/rifampin". **Rifampin is not single-dose**: it is **4 oral doses over 48 hours**. **Ceftriaxone** and **ciprofloxacin** are single doses (CDC, MMWR 2024).
  - **Recent change:** ciprofloxacin-resistant meningococci now occur in the US. Where resistant strains are **2 or more cases and 20% or more** of cases in 12 months, the CDC advises **rifampin, ceftriaxone or azithromycin instead of ciprofloxacin** (MMWR 2024).
- **Correction (vaccine):** your notes say the meningococcal vaccine "is only used in high-risk groups like military recruits and college students" (p. 101). The CDC recommends it for **all adolescents**:
  - **MenACWY at 11-12 years, with a booster at 16**;
  - **MenB** (2 doses, 6 months apart) by shared decision, preferably at **16-18 years**, and for people at higher risk;
  - **MenABCWY**, a combined vaccine, when both are due at the same visit.
- **Higher-risk groups for vaccination (MSD):** asplenia, complement deficiencies, eculizumab or ravulizumab, HIV, military recruits, first-year college students in dorms. NBME TC p. 222 lists the **MenACWY conjugate** for patients without a spleen.

## Neisseria gonorrhoeae (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 102-105)
- **Lab:** does **not** ferment maltose. Grows on **Thayer-Martin** agar, a chocolate agar with **vancomycin** (kills gram-positives), **colistin** (kills other gram-negatives) and **nystatin** (kills fungi) (pp. 98, 102).
- **Virulence** (pp. 102-104):
  - **pili** for attachment;
  - **IgA protease**;
  - **LOS**;
  - **antigenic variation of pili**: pilus genes recombine often, so antibodies stop working, reinfection is common and no vaccine exists.
- **Men:** urethritis with **purulent discharge**. Gram stain shows **gram-negative diplococci inside neutrophils** (p. 102).
- **Women:** infects the **cervix** (not the vagina). **About half have no symptoms** (pp. 102-103).
- **Complications:**
  - **PID** (endometritis, salpingitis): **cervical motion tenderness**, scarring, **ectopic pregnancy and infertility**. Your notes give infertility in 15-20% after one episode and 50-80% after several (p. 103);
  - **Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome**: inflammation of the **liver capsule** (perihepatitis) with right upper quadrant pain (p. 105);
  - **newborn conjunctivitis** from passage through the birth canal; can cause blindness (p. 104). Osmosis calls *N. gonorrhoeae* the most common cause of **hyperacute**, very purulent conjunctivitis (p. 588);
  - **DGI** (p. 104): more common in women (especially pregnant) and in **terminal complement deficiency**. Either **purulent arthritis** or the triad **polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis and dermatitis** (skin pustules);
  - **septic arthritis**: the most common cause in **sexually active young adults** (p. 104).
- **Your notes' treatment** (p. 105): a **3rd-generation cephalosporin** for gonorrhea, plus **azithromycin or doxycycline** for chlamydia, which often coexists and is not killed by beta-lactams. Pharm HY also lists **azithromycin** for gonorrhoea (PDF 126).
- **Recent change (CDC 2021 STI guidelines):**
  - **uncomplicated gonorrhea:** **ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once** (1 g if 150 kg or more);
  - **if chlamydia is not ruled out:** add **doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days**;
  - **azithromycin is no longer added routinely.** The CDC cites harm to gut flora and rising azithromycin resistance;
  - **DGI (arthritis-dermatitis):** **ceftriaxone 1 g IV or IM every 24 hours**, for a total of at least 7 days;
  - **gonococcal conjunctivitis:** ceftriaxone 1 g IM once;
  - **retest 3 months** after treatment; pharyngeal infection needs a **test of cure at 7-14 days**.

## Moraxella catarrhalis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 106)
- Gram-negative diplococcus in normal upper airway flora.
- Causes **otitis media, sinusitis**, and **bronchitis or pneumonia in older adults with COPD** (p. 106). NBME TC p. 115: smoker with COPD and pneumonia → think *Legionella*, *H. influenzae* or *M. catarrhalis*.

## Haemophilus influenzae (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 107-110)
- **Lab:** gram-negative coccobacillus that needs **X factor (hemin)** and **V factor (NAD)** (p. 107).
  - **Chocolate agar** (lysed red cells) supplies both.
  - On sheep blood agar it grows only next to ***S. aureus*** streaks (**satellite phenomenon**): staph hemolysis releases X factor, and staph secretes V factor (p. 110).
- **Capsule:** type b capsule = **PRP**, which blocks phagocytosis and allows spread through the blood (p. 107).
- **Type b (encapsulated) disease, mostly under age 5** (p. 108):
  - **meningitis** (the most common cause before the vaccine);
  - **epiglottitis**: cherry-red swollen epiglottis, drooling, stridor (guide 19);
  - bacteremia, pneumonia, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, facial cellulitis.
- **Nontypeable (no capsule) strains** (p. 109):
  - **otitis media** (with pneumococcus, the most common cause; over 90% of middle-ear isolates are nontypeable), **sinusitis**, **bronchitis**;
  - **pneumonia and bacteremia** in adults and older children with a predisposing factor: a recent **viral infection**, **cancer** or **cystic fibrosis** (p. 109). NBME TC p. 115 adds the smoker with **COPD** and pneumonia;
  - **the Hib vaccine does not protect** against them.
- Mnemonic: **HaEMOPhilus** = **E**piglottitis, **M**eningitis, **O**titis media, **P**neumonia (p. 109).
- **Vaccine** (pp. 109-110): **PRP conjugated to tetanus or diphtheria toxoid**, started at **2 months**. The CDC Pink Book: invasive Hib disease has fallen by **more than 99%**; the first conjugate vaccine was licensed in 1987.
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - invasive disease, including meningitis, is treated with **ceftriaxone or cefotaxime**;
  - **over 50%** of US isolates are ampicillin-resistant (**beta-lactamase**);
  - if an **unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated child under 4** lives in the home, **all household members** (except pregnant women) get **rifampin**, and the child is vaccinated.
- ***H. ducreyi*** (p. 111): **chancroid**, a **painful**, deep genital ulcer with ragged edges and gray base, with tender inguinal nodes ("you do cry with *ducreyi*"). Pharm HY lists **azithromycin** for chancroid (PDF 126).
  - **Other source (CDC 2021):** treat with **azithromycin 1 g orally once** or **ceftriaxone 250 mg IM once** (or ciprofloxacin or erythromycin). The CDC notes the tender, pus-filled inguinal nodes occur in fewer than half of cases.

## Bordetella pertussis: whooping cough (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 112-114)
- **Toxins** (p. 113):
  - **pertussis toxin** (A-B toxin): **ADP-ribosylates Gi**, so Gi can no longer **inhibit** adenylate cyclase. cAMP rises in phagocytes. Systemic effects: **lymphocytosis** and **histamine sensitivity**. Its toxoid is in the vaccine;
  - **adenylate cyclase toxin**: itself a calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase, like anthrax edema factor (guide 63);
  - **tracheal cytotoxin**: a cell-wall (peptidoglycan) fragment that **kills ciliated airway cells** and triggers IL-1 (fever).
- **Correction (wording):** your notes say pertussis toxin "has a potent adenylate cyclase activity by ADP ribosylation of Gi" (p. 113). Pertussis toxin is **not** an adenylate cyclase. It ADP-ribosylates Gi, which then cannot inhibit the cell's own adenylate cyclase, so cAMP accumulates (Mangmool and Kurose 2011). The enzyme that *is* an adenylate cyclase is the separate adenylate cyclase toxin. Your notes' own phrase "inhibiting negative regulator of adenylate cyclase, disinhibition" is the right idea.
- **Stages** (p. 114):
  - **catarrhal**: cold symptoms, low fever;
  - **paroxysmal**: bursts of cough, then an **inspiratory "whoop"**, and **vomiting after coughing**;
  - **convalescent**: slowly fading cough.
- **Who:** classic disease is in **unvaccinated infants**. Adults also get it, because immunity from vaccine or infection fades (p. 114).
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - white count usually **15,000-20,000** with **60-80% lymphocytes**. Marked lymphocytosis in infants under 6 months means a worse outlook;
  - in infants, choking spells and **asphyxia** may replace the whoop;
  - **PCR of a nasopharyngeal sample** is the preferred test;
  - treatment: **azithromycin for 5 days** (preferred in infants 6 weeks or younger) or erythromycin; TMP-SMX if macrolides cannot be used (age 2 months or older);
  - **household contacts** get antibiotics within 21 days of the case's cough onset, whether or not vaccinated.
- **Other source (CDC):** give **Tdap in every pregnancy, at 27-36 weeks**, to pass antibodies to the baby. It prevents **78%** of pertussis in infants under 2 months and is **91%** effective against hospitalized cases.

## Pasteurella multocida (El-Husseiny Micro p. 111)
- Lives in the mouths of **cats and dogs**; spread by **bites, especially cat bites**.
- Rapidly spreading **cellulitis with lymphadenitis**, often with other bacteria (p. 111).
- **Other source (MSD bite-wound table):** **amoxicillin-clavulanate** for outpatients (3 days as prophylaxis, 5-7 days as treatment); ampicillin-sulbactam for inpatients. For cat bites in adults, MSD also lists a fluoroquinolone as an option.

## Francisella tularensis: tularemia (El-Husseiny Micro p. 112)
- **Reservoir:** wild animals, especially **rabbits**, deer and rodents.
- **Routes and forms** (p. 112):
  - **tick bite** (*Dermacentor*) or **skinning rabbits** → **ulceroglandular** disease: ulcer at the entry site with large, tender, sometimes necrotic regional nodes;
  - **aerosols** (skinning rabbits) → **pneumonia**;
  - undercooked meat or contaminated water → **typhoidal** tularemia.
- A **facultative intracellular** organism and a **possible bioweapon** (p. 112).
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - ulceroglandular is the most common form; also spread by **deer flies**. **No person-to-person spread**;
  - **warn the lab**: as few as 10 inhaled organisms can cause severe pneumonia;
  - treatment: **streptomycin or gentamicin**; oral **ciprofloxacin or doxycycline** for mild to moderate disease. Pharm HY also lists streptomycin for tularemia (PDF 127).

## Brucella: brucellosis (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 114-115)
- **Species:** *B. abortus* (cattle), *B. melitensis* (goats; MSD: sheep and goats), *B. suis* (pigs) (p. 114).
- **Spread:** **unpasteurized dairy** (in the US, often linked to travel to Mexico) and contact with animals (slaughterhouse work) (p. 115).
- **Disease** (p. 115):
  - **facultative intracellular**, living in macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system, which leads to septicemia; can form **noncaseating granulomas**;
  - **undulant fever** (rises and falls in waves), sweats, aches, weakness;
  - can involve the liver, heart (**endocarditis**) and brain.
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - spine and **sacroiliac** bone infection and **orchitis** are complications. Death is rare and usually from **endocarditis** or brain involvement;
  - tell the lab, because culture is slow; **bone marrow culture** is most sensitive;
  - treatment: **doxycycline for 6 weeks plus streptomycin or gentamicin for 14 days** (or rifampin for 6 weeks in uncomplicated cases). Children under 8: TMP-SMX plus rifampin. Relapse occurs in 5-15%;
  - no human vaccine.

## Legionella pneumophila (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 116-117)
- **Lab:** stains poorly with Gram stain; **silver stain** works better. Grows on **BCYE agar with cysteine and iron** (p. 116). Mnemonic: a French legionnaire with a **silver** helmet at a **charcoal** campfire with an **iron** dagger; "no sissy" = **cysteine** (p. 117).
- **Source:** water (air conditioning, hot water tanks); inhaled **aerosols** (p. 116). MSD adds cooling towers, showers, hot tubs and fountains.
- **Risk:** smokers, chronic lung disease (p. 116). MSD adds older adults, diabetes and weak immunity.
- **Legionnaires' disease** (pp. 116-117):
  - high fever, cough, **confusion**, **diarrhea**; recent **cruise or hotel stay**;
  - X-ray: usually **one lobe**, progressing to consolidation;
  - **hyponatremia** is a classic lab clue;
  - sputum Gram stain: many neutrophils, **few or no organisms**;
  - diagnosis: **urine antigen** test.
- **Pontiac fever:** a mild flu-like illness that resolves without treatment (p. 117; MSD).
- **Treatment:** **levofloxacin** (a respiratory fluoroquinolone) or **azithromycin** (p. 117). Pharm HY also lists azithromycin for *Legionella* (PDF 126).
- **Other source (MSD):**
  - the **urine antigen detects only *L. pneumophila* serogroup 1** (65-90% of cases), so a negative test does not exclude *Legionella*;
  - relative **bradycardia**, low phosphate and high liver enzymes may occur;
  - case fatality about **10-15%**, higher in hospital-acquired cases and older or immunocompromised patients.

## Clinical vignette
An 18-year-old college freshman who lives in a dorm has fever, headache and vomiting that began this morning. By evening she is confused and hypotensive, with a spreading, non-blanching purple rash on her legs and trunk. CSF shows many neutrophils, high protein and low glucose. Gram stain shows gram-negative diplococci. Despite treatment she dies the next day; autopsy shows **bilateral adrenal hemorrhage**.

- **Diagnosis:** ***N. meningitidis*** meningitis and meningococcemia with **Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome** (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 99-101).
- **Why shock and the rash:** **LOS** endotoxin drives TNF and IL-1 release, DIC and shock; LOS is also linked to the petechiae and hemorrhagic skin lesions (pp. 100-101).
- **Why the dorm:** crowding plus droplet spread from healthy carriers (p. 99).
- **Treatment:** ceftriaxone (MSD).
- **Her roommate:** prophylaxis with rifampin (4 doses over 2 days), one IM dose of ceftriaxone, or one dose of ciprofloxacin where strains are not resistant (MMWR 2024; Osmosis p. 511).
- **Prevention:** MenACWY at 11-12 and 16 years (CDC).

## Common traps
- **Maltose:** meningococcus ferments maltose; gonococcus does not (pp. 98, 102).
- **Recurrent *Neisseria* infection → check terminal complement (C5-C9)** (pp. 101, 104). Also think of eculizumab (Osmosis p. 433).
- **Meningococcal vaccine is routine at 11-12 and 16**, not only for recruits and students (CDC).
- **Rifampin prophylaxis is 4 doses over 48 hours**, not one dose (MMWR 2024).
- **Gonorrhea: ceftriaxone 500 mg alone;** add **doxycycline**, not azithromycin, if chlamydia is not excluded (CDC 2021).
- **No gonorrhea vaccine and no lasting immunity:** pilus antigenic variation (p. 104).
- **Hib vaccine does not prevent nontypeable *H. influenzae*** otitis (p. 109).
- **Pertussis toxin raises cAMP by disabling Gi;** the separate adenylate cyclase toxin is the enzyme (p. 113; Mangmool 2011).
- **Tdap in every pregnancy at 27-36 weeks** (CDC).
- ***Legionella* urine antigen misses non-serogroup 1 strains** (MSD).
- **Animal clues:** cat bite → *Pasteurella* (amoxicillin-clavulanate); rabbit or tick → *Francisella* (gentamicin or streptomycin); goat cheese or farm animals → *Brucella* (doxycycline-based combination).

## Practice questions (NBME style)

1. A 20-year-old man is hospitalized for meningococcal meningitis. He had meningococcal bacteremia at age 15, and his older brother also had two episodes of *Neisseria* infection. He has never had pneumococcal or *Haemophilus* infections. Which laboratory finding is most likely?
   - **Answer:** **a low total hemolytic complement (CH50) from deficiency of a terminal complement protein (C5-C9).**
   - **Why:** the membrane attack complex is needed to kill *Neisseria*. Terminal complement deficiency predisposes to repeated meningococcal and disseminated gonococcal infection (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 101, 104; MSD).
   - **Trap:** **low IgA**. *Neisseria* do make IgA protease, but IgA deficiency is not the classic cause of recurrent invasive *Neisseria* disease (standard teaching).

2. A 23-year-old college student's roommate is admitted with meningococcal meningitis. The roommate's isolate is ciprofloxacin-resistant, and the county health department reports that resistant strains caused 3 of the last 10 local cases this year. The student is well and not pregnant. Which is the most appropriate prophylaxis?
   - **Answer:** **a single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone** (rifampin for 2 days is an acceptable alternative).
   - **Why:** close contacts need prophylaxis. Where resistant strains make up 20% or more of cases (2 or more cases) in 12 months, the CDC advises rifampin, ceftriaxone or azithromycin instead of ciprofloxacin (MMWR 2024).
   - **Trap:** **a single oral dose of ciprofloxacin**. It is usually an option, but not where resistance has reached this threshold (MMWR 2024).

3. A healthy 16-year-old girl comes for a sports physical. She received all childhood vaccines and a meningococcal conjugate vaccine (MenACWY) at age 11. She is not going to college or the military. Which meningococcal vaccine plan is most appropriate today?
   - **Answer:** **a MenACWY booster dose now.**
   - **Why:** the CDC recommends MenACWY for all adolescents at 11-12 with a booster at 16. MenB can also be offered by shared decision at 16-18 (CDC).
   - **Trap:** **no further dose because she is not in a high-risk group**. That reflects your notes' older wording (p. 101); routine vaccination now covers all adolescents.

4. A 24-year-old man has had dysuria and a thick yellow urethral discharge for 3 days. A Gram stain of the discharge shows gram-negative diplococci inside neutrophils. A chlamydia NAAT has been sent, and results will take 2 days. He weighs 80 kg. Which treatment is most appropriate today?
   - **Answer:** **ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once, plus doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days.**
   - **Why:** this is gonococcal urethritis (El-Husseiny Micro p. 102). The CDC's 2021 guidelines use ceftriaxone alone for gonorrhea and add doxycycline when chlamydia has not been excluded.
   - **Trap:** **ceftriaxone plus a single 1 g dose of azithromycin**. That was the older dual therapy (your notes p. 105; Pharm HY PDF 126) and is no longer recommended by the CDC.

5. A 26-year-old woman is treated for gonorrhea for the third time in 2 years. Each time, she made antibodies against the organism. Which property of the organism best explains why her earlier infections did not protect her?
   - **Answer:** **frequent antigenic variation of its pilus proteins.**
   - **Why:** pilus genes recombine to make new pilus types, so antibodies from earlier infections no longer block attachment. This is also why no vaccine exists (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 104-105).
   - **Trap:** **a polysaccharide capsule that blocks phagocytosis**. That is the key factor for meningococcus, which has a protective vaccine (pp. 99, 101).

6. A 22-year-old woman has 4 days of fever, pain moving between her wrists and knees, and painful swelling along the tendons of the back of her right hand. A few small pustules with red bases are on her fingers. She has a new sexual partner. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **IV ceftriaxone 1 g every 24 hours.**
   - **Why:** polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis and pustular skin lesions make up the DGI triad (El-Husseiny Micro p. 104). The CDC treats arthritis-dermatitis DGI with ceftriaxone 1 g IV or IM daily, for a total of at least 7 days (CDC 2021).
   - **Trap:** **a single 500 mg IM dose of ceftriaxone**. That treats uncomplicated mucosal gonorrhea only.

7. A 25-year-old woman has 2 days of fever, lower abdominal pain and sharp right upper quadrant pain that worsens with deep breathing. Pelvic exam shows purulent cervical discharge and cervical motion tenderness. Liver enzymes and gallbladder ultrasound are normal. Which structure is most likely inflamed and causing the upper abdominal pain?
   - **Answer:** **the liver capsule (perihepatitis, Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome).**
   - **Why:** infection from PID by *N. gonorrhoeae* or *C. trachomatis* can spread up to the liver capsule (El-Husseiny Micro p. 105). The liver tissue itself is spared, so enzymes are normal (standard teaching).
   - **Trap:** **the gallbladder wall (acute cholecystitis)**. The ultrasound is normal, and the pelvic findings point to PID.

8. An 18-month-old unvaccinated boy has fever and a stiff neck. CSF Gram stain shows small gram-negative coccobacilli. The organism grows on chocolate agar, and on sheep blood agar only around a streak of *S. aureus*. The vaccine against this organism links its capsule to a protein carrier. How does this linkage improve the vaccine's effect in infants?
   - **Answer:** **it makes the capsule a T-cell-dependent antigen, so helper T cells drive class-switched IgG and memory B cells.**
   - **Why:** the PRP capsule alone is T-cell independent and weak under age 2. Conjugating it to tetanus or diphtheria toxoid recruits T-cell help (El-Husseiny Micro p. 110; CDC Pink Book).
   - **Trap:** **it lets the vaccine stimulate cytotoxic T cells against infected cells**. Hib is extracellular; protection comes from anticapsular antibody and complement (p. 107).

9. A 3-year-old girl who received all doses of the Hib conjugate vaccine has fever and ear pain. The tympanic membrane bulges. Fluid from tympanocentesis grows gram-negative coccobacilli that need X and V factors. Which property of this isolate best explains why the vaccine did not prevent the infection?
   - **Answer:** **it has no polysaccharide capsule (a nontypeable strain).**
   - **Why:** the vaccine targets only the type b capsule. Over 90% of *H. influenzae* from middle-ear fluid is nontypeable (El-Husseiny Micro p. 109).
   - **Trap:** **it produces beta-lactamase**. That affects antibiotic choice (MSD: over 50% are ampicillin-resistant), not vaccine protection.

10. A 6-week-old girl has 5 days of coughing fits that end with gagging and vomiting, and two episodes of turning blue. Her older brother has had a cough for 3 weeks. White cell count is 34,000/mm³ with 78% lymphocytes. Which action of a bacterial toxin best explains the high lymphocyte count?
   - **Answer:** **ADP-ribosylation of the inhibitory G protein (Gi) by pertussis toxin.**
   - **Why:** pertussis toxin locks Gi so it cannot inhibit adenylate cyclase; cAMP rises, and lymphocytosis is a hallmark systemic effect (El-Husseiny Micro p. 113; Mangmool and Kurose 2011). MSD: marked lymphocytosis in infants under 6 months means a worse outlook.
   - **Trap:** **a calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase that enters host cells**. That is the separate adenylate cyclase toxin, which impairs phagocytes (p. 113).

11. A 31-year-old woman at 28 weeks' gestation asks about protecting her newborn from whooping cough. She received Tdap during her last pregnancy 2 years ago. Which recommendation is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **give Tdap now, during this pregnancy.**
   - **Why:** the CDC recommends Tdap in every pregnancy, ideally early in the 27-36 week window, to transfer the most antibody to each baby (CDC).
   - **Trap:** **no vaccine, because she was vaccinated in her last pregnancy**. Antibody levels fall, so each pregnancy needs a new dose.

12. A 68-year-old man who smokes has 3 days of fever of 39.8°C, cough, watery diarrhea and confusion after a week at a hotel. Chest X-ray shows right lower lobe consolidation. Serum sodium is 127 mEq/L. Sputum Gram stain shows many neutrophils but no organisms. Which test is most likely to confirm the diagnosis quickly?
   - **Answer:** **a urine antigen test for *Legionella*.**
   - **Why:** fever, confusion, diarrhea, hyponatremia and a Gram stain without organisms after a hotel stay suggest *Legionella* (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 116-117). The urine antigen is the usual quick test (p. 117). It detects only serogroup 1, so a negative test does not rule it out (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **culture on sheep blood agar**. *Legionella* needs BCYE agar with cysteine and iron (p. 116).

13. A 45-year-old woman has 2 months of fevers that come and go every few days, drenching sweats, fatigue and low back pain. She often eats fresh goat cheese brought from a family farm in Mexico. Tenderness is present over the left sacroiliac joint. Blood cultures, held for an extended time, grow small gram-negative coccobacilli. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **doxycycline for 6 weeks plus gentamicin (or streptomycin) for the first 14 days.**
   - **Why:** this is brucellosis from unpasteurized dairy (El-Husseiny Micro pp. 114-115). MSD uses combination therapy to reduce relapse, with rifampin as an alternative partner in uncomplicated cases.
   - **Trap:** **a 7-day course of a single oral drug**. Short single-drug therapy leads to relapse; *Brucella* lives inside macrophages (p. 115; MSD).

14. A 52-year-old hunter has fever and a painful ulcer on his index finger 4 days after skinning wild rabbits. A large, tender lymph node is present in the same armpit. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **gentamicin (or streptomycin).**
   - **Why:** ulceroglandular tularemia follows handling infected rabbits (El-Husseiny Micro p. 112). MSD lists streptomycin or gentamicin as the main drugs; Pharm HY also lists streptomycin (PDF 127). The lab should be warned before culture (MSD).
   - **Trap:** **amoxicillin-clavulanate**. That treats *Pasteurella* from cat and dog bites (MSD), not tularemia.

15. A 27-year-old man has a 1-week-old painful ulcer on his penis with ragged, undermined edges and a gray, pus-covered base. A tender, swollen inguinal lymph node is present on one side. Darkfield microscopy and syphilis serology are negative. Which treatment is most appropriate?
   - **Answer:** **a single oral dose of azithromycin 1 g** (or ceftriaxone 250 mg IM once).
   - **Why:** a painful ulcer with tender nodes is chancroid from *H. ducreyi* (El-Husseiny Micro p. 111). These are CDC 2021 recommended regimens.
   - **Trap:** **benzathine penicillin G**. That treats syphilis, whose chancre is classically painless (standard teaching).
