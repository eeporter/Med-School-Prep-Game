# What the research says, and how the game uses it

This is a short summary of the evidence behind each mechanic. It draws on general research on learning and memory from cognitive psychology and education, on research about games and motivation, and on studies in medical education. Every claim below was checked against the source it cites.

## Learning and memory (general research)

| Finding | Evidence | Game mechanic |
|---|---|---|
| Of ten common study techniques, practice testing and spreading study over time (distributed practice) rated highest. Rereading and underlining rated low. | Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham 2013, *Psychol Sci Public Interest* 14(1). | The whole game is practice testing on a spaced schedule. It has no pages to reread. |
| Quizzing raises classroom achievement by a medium amount. Corrective feedback is one of the factors that changes the size of the effect. | Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks 2021, *Psychol Bull* 147:399–435 (doi:10.1037/bul0000309): 222 studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.499. | Every question gives feedback right away, except in Exam Block, where it comes at the end. |
| Practice tests beat restudying and every other comparison condition. | Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, *Rev Educ Res* (doi:10.3102/0034654316689306), abstract. | Same as above. |
| The best gap between reviews depends on how long you need to remember. As a share of the time until the test, it falls from about 20–40% for a test 1 week away to about 5–10% for a test 1 year away. | Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008, *Psychol Sci*: more than 1,350 learners, test delays up to 350 days. | SM-2 intervals expand (1, 6, then × ease). **New:** with an exam date set, the gap before the next review is capped at half the days left, so each question returns on a shrinking schedule that converges on the test and never passes it. |
| Successive relearning means retrieving every item correctly at least once in a session, then doing it again in 2 or more later sessions. In real courses it raised exam scores by about 10%. In one study, it also roughly tripled recall 24 days after the exam (over 60% vs under 20%). | Dunlosky, Greve, Badali, Wissman & Rawson 2023, *Successive relearning: an introduction and guide for educators* (APA Div. 2), summarizing Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013 (*Educ Psychol Rev* 25:523–548) and Janes et al. 2020 (*Appl Cogn Psychol* 34:1118–1132). SM-2 itself has the same rule: "After each repetition session of a given day repeat again all items that scored below four … until all of these items score at least four" (Wozniak, 1990). | **New:** in Daily Rounds and Focus, any question missed or only guessed right comes back at the end of the same session, up to 3 extra times, until she answers it right with confidence. Only the first answer of the day sets the schedule and earns XP. |
| Mixing related categories (interleaving) beats studying them in blocks, especially when the categories look alike. | Brunmair & Richter 2019, *Psychol Bull* (doi:10.1037/bul0000209): meta-analysis, g = 0.42 overall. | Daily Rounds and Exam Block never put two questions from the same organ system in a row when they can avoid it. |
| The more information feedback carries, the more it helps. Feedback that explains the task and how to solve it had about four times the effect of plain reward or punishment. | Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, *Front Psychol* 10:3087: 435 studies, d = 0.48 overall; high-information d = 0.99, corrective d = 0.46, reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24. | Each answer shows why the key is right and why every distractor is wrong (the one she picked comes first), with sources. XP is a small extra, not the main feedback. |
| On multiple-choice tests without feedback, students can learn the wrong answers they picked. Feedback, immediate or delayed, raises correct answers and cuts these intrusions. | Butler & Roediger 2008, *Mem Cognit* 36:604–616 (doi:10.3758/MC.36.3.604). | No answer goes without feedback. Exam Block ends with a full review of every question. |
| Errors made with high confidence are corrected more readily after feedback (hypercorrection). | Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, *J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 27:1491–1494. | She rates her confidence before seeing the answer. Confident misses are flagged and lead the next Daily Rounds. |
| Learners are often overconfident. They think rereading beats testing and prefer blocked practice, even though spacing and interleaving work better. They stop studying items too early. Testing yourself is the best way to tell what you know. | Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013, *Annu Rev Psychol* 64:417–444. | The game decides what comes back and when, so she doesn't drop items too early. The session summary and the **new** all-time line on the home screen show how often her "sure" answers are right. Daily Rounds is mixed by default. |
| Prompting learners to explain material to themselves helps learning. | Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne 2018, *Educ Psychol Rev* (doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x): 64 reports, g = 0.55. | Not built yet; see "Considered next" below. |

## Games, gamification, and motivation

| Finding | Evidence | Game mechanic |
|---|---|---|
| Digital games beat non-game instruction for learning by a small-to-medium amount, and better-designed versions of a game beat plain versions. | Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth 2016, *Rev Educ Res* 86:79–122: g = 0.33 vs non-game (k = 57); augmented designs g = 0.34 (k = 20). | The game format is a reasonable choice, and the design choices matter. |
| Gamification improves learning by a medium amount, with no difference between types of game elements. Students liked the feedback and goal setting it gave them. Some disliked it because it caused anxiety or jealousy. | Bai, Hew & Huang 2020, *Educ Res Rev* 33:100322: 30 interventions, 3,202 participants, g = 0.504. | Points, levels, and a streak, all private. There is no leaderboard or comparison with others. |
| Gamification has a small-to-medium effect on learning, and the effect holds in rigorous studies. | Sailer & Homner 2020, *Educ Psychol Rev* 32:77–112 (doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w): cognitive g = 0.49 (0.42 in high-rigor studies). | Same as above. |
| Expected tangible rewards lower interest in the task itself (d = −0.36 overall). Positive, informational feedback raises it (d = 0.33). | Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychol Bull* 125:627–668. | XP works as feedback on progress, never as a prize. There is no speed bonus and nothing to buy. Messages explain the content rather than just praise. |

## Medical education

| Finding | Evidence | Game mechanic |
|---|---|---|
| Practicing retrieval beats rereading for long-term retention in medical students. | Larsen et al. 2013, *Adv Health Sci Educ* 18:409–425 (doi:10.1007/s10459-012-9379-7): repeated testing beat studying a review sheet at 6 months. | Every activity is a question. |
| More practice questions and more spaced-repetition flashcards go with higher Step 1 scores. | Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen 2015, *Perspect Med Educ* 4:308–313 (doi:10.1007/s40037-015-0220-x). This is an observational study, so it shows a link, not cause and effect. | Daily Rounds brings back each question on an expanding schedule (SM-2). |
| Most gamification studies in medical education measure recall, and few show gains in deeper reasoning. | Huang, Loid & Sung 2024, *BMC Med Educ* 24:20 (doi:10.1186/s12909-023-04955-1): 18 of 23 studies targeted low-level outcomes. | Questions are clinical vignettes that require two-step reasoning, not bare fact recall. |

## Exam profiles (official formats)

Elissa can add each test she is preparing for, with its date. The soonest upcoming one sets the focus. All of these exams use the same single-best-answer item style: NBME writes its subject exam items "with the same rigor used to create USMLE Step 1 and Step 2 items", and the CBSE "uses the same item formats" as Step 1. So what differs between them is content scope, length, and pacing.

| Exam | Official format | What the game does |
|---|---|---|
| USMLE Step 1 | On or after 2026-05-14: fourteen 30-minute blocks of up to 20 questions in one 8-hour session. Before that: seven 60-minute blocks of up to 40 (usmle.org, Step 1 exam content). Content outline by system, e.g. Reproductive & Endocrine 12–16%, Respiratory & Renal 11–15%, Human Development 1–3% (usmle.org, Step 1 content outline and specifications). | Exam Block defaults to 20 or 40 questions depending on the test date. New questions in Daily Rounds are drawn so each organ system's share follows the midpoint of its outline range, not how many questions the bank happens to have. |
| NBME CBSE | 200 items in 5 h 15 min, including an optional 15-minute break (NBME Subject Exam Timing Chart). It has the same system outline as Step 1 (NBME CBSE content outline). | Same weighting as Step 1, using questions tagged NBME. |
| NBME CBSSA | Four sections of 50 questions, 1 h 15 min each at standard pace. Prepares for Step 1 (nbme.org, CBSSA page). | 50-question blocks with Step 1 content weighting. |
| NBME basic science subject exams | 125 items in 3 h 8 min. Anatomy & Embryology is 145 items in 3 h 38 min, and Microbiology & Immunology is 150 items in 3 h 45 min (NBME Subject Exam Timing Chart). | New questions and Exam Blocks come from that subject first. |

Each of these works out to about 90 seconds per question, which is the Exam Block pace. With exams set, each question's review gaps are capped against the soonest exam that covers it (see the spacing row above).

## Design rules that follow

1. **Feedback right away, with reasons.** After each question (outside Exam Block), show the answer, why it is right, and why each wrong option is wrong, starting with the one the learner picked.
2. **Relearn to criterion, then space it out.** A question missed or guessed today comes back later in the same session until she answers it right with confidence. It also returns on later days on the spaced schedule.
3. **Reward accuracy and spacing, not speed or guessing.** Correct and sure = 10 XP, correct and unsure = 8, correct guess = 4, wrong = 0, plus 5 XP for a question that was due for review. Relearning repeats earn no XP, so repeating a question can't be used to farm points.
4. **Low stakes, private progress.** Nothing is lost for a wrong answer, and nothing is compared with other people.
5. **Aim at the next exam.** Add each test and its date. New questions, Exam Block length, and review timing follow the soonest one, and gaps shrink as it approaches (capped at half the days left).
6. **Exam realism when it matters.** Exam Block mimics the real test (timed, no feedback until the end) so she practices pacing, then reviews everything.

## Considered next

- **Self-explanation prompt** (Bisra et al. 2018, g = 0.55). After answering, the game would ask "In one sentence, why is this the answer?" before showing the explanation. The evidence is strong, but it adds a step to every question, and doing more questions also predicts Step 1 scores (Deng 2015). The best form is probably an optional setting.
